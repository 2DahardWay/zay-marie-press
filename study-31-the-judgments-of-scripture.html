The Judgments of Scripture

Their Subjects, Timing, Standards, Purposes, and Outcomes

A comparative biblical study designed to keep distinct judgments distinct

Scripture presents many acts and occasions of divine judgment, but it does not combine them into one undifferentiated event. This study supplies a clear framework for identifying who or what is judged, when the judgment occurs, the governing standard, its purpose, and its outcome.

Compare historical judgments, the Cross, believer self-judgment and discipline, the Judgment Seat of Christ, Israel’s prophetic accountability, the nations, angels, Kingdom judgments, and the Great White Throne without transferring features from one passage to another.

ABOUT THIS STUDY

Why Must the Judgments Be Distinguished?

The Judge is always righteous, but Scripture assigns different judgments to different subjects, times, standards, purposes, and outcomes. A judgment of living nations at the return of the Son of Man is not the final judgment of the dead after the thousand years. An evaluation for reward or loss is not a trial to determine whether Christ’s finished work was sufficient.

This study organizes the major judgment complexes of Scripture so readers can recognize genuine similarities without collapsing distinct events, audiences, or divine purposes.

WHAT YOU’LL STUDY

Use Five Questions to Identify Every Judgment

Subject

Identify who or what is being judged before drawing conclusions from the passage.

Timing

Place each judgment within its stated historical, prophetic, or Mystery setting.

Standard

Determine the revealed measure by which the judgment is administered.

Purpose

Ask what God accomplishes through the judgment in its own context.

Outcome

Trace the sentence, reward, loss, discipline, exclusion, or final state that follows.

Programmatic Setting

Keep Prophecy and Mystery distinct wherever Scripture assigns different subjects and purposes.

FROM THE STUDY

Similar Language Does Not Establish an Identical Judgment

“Never identify a judgment by one shared word alone.”

Fire, books, works, appearing, reward, and judgment may occur in different passages without making those passages one event. The immediate context and programmatic setting establish whose accountability is in view.
