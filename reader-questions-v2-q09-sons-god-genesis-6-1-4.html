Who Are “the Sons of God” in Genesis 6:1–4?

From Reader Questions, Volume 2: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 2 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

The sons of God are angelic beings. The same title is used of them in Job 1:6 and 38:7, and Peter and Jude set angels who sinned beside the flood and Sodom. The Lord’s words in Matthew 22:30 describe people in the resurrection and do not decide what angels could do before.

Where the Verses Stand

Genesis 6 begins the account of the flood. It opens with the multiplying of men, the sons of God and the daughters of men, and it ends with a statement of violence and the flood decree. The identity of the sons of God is the question that decides what the passage is describing.

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: in the Old Testament, the Hebrew phrase translated “sons of God” is used of beings who present themselves before the Lord (Job 1:6) and who watched creation (Job 38:7). Those beings are not men.
  • Stated: Peter sets “the angels that sinned” beside “the old world” in the flood (2 Peter 2:4–5), and Jude joins angels who “left their own habitation” to Sodom’s “strange flesh” (Jude 6–7).
  • Shown by the context: the passage says the sons of God “saw the daughters of men” and “took them wives of all which they chose.” The contrast is between the two groups, and the text treats the sons of God as a distinct order from “men.”
  • Shown by the context: the Lord’s words in Matthew 22:30 describe how people will be in the resurrection: “as the angels … in heaven.” He speaks of holy angels in heaven and says nothing of fallen angels on the earth, and the comparison is to the people’s state.
  • Inferred: that Peter and Jude have this event in view. Neither names Genesis 6, and the argument rests on the pairing of the angels with the flood and with “strange flesh.”

The Other Readings

The line of Seth. Gleason Archer (Encyclopedia of Bible Difficulties) holds that the sons of God are the godly line of Seth who married the line of Cain. It fits the interest of Genesis 4–5 in two lines, and it must explain why the children of such marriages were “mighty men” and why Job’s “sons of God” are not Sethites. Human rulers. Meredith Kline (“Divine Kingship and Genesis 6:1–4”) takes them as dynastic kings who practised polygamy. Angelic beings. Gordon Wenham (Genesis 1–15, Word Biblical Commentary) takes them as divine beings. That is the reading taken here.

What It Means for the Reader

The passage does not tell the reader how the union happened or what was born of it, and it does not build a doctrine of angels on the point. It tells the reader why the earth’s wickedness was “great” before the flood, and that God judged it.
