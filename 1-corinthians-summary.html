1 Corinthians

Paul applies the message of Christ crucified to division, holiness, marriage, liberty, worship, spiritual gifts, resurrection, and orderly life in the local assembly.

Purpose

Paul writes to correct serious disorders reported from Corinth and to answer questions raised in the assembly’s letter. He brings every issue under the lordship of Christ: factionalism, sexual immorality, lawsuits, marriage, food offered to idols, public worship, gifts, and denial of bodily resurrection.

The letter does more than solve isolated problems. It shows how the gospel of Christ crucified reshapes wisdom, identity, freedom, relationships, worship, and hope within God’s assembly.

Historical and Biblical Context

Corinth was a prosperous Roman colony marked by commerce, social competition, religious plurality, and pronounced status divisions. Paul had established the assembly during an extended ministry recorded in Acts 18. After leaving, he received reports and correspondence requiring pastoral and apostolic response.

The letter belongs to Paul’s ministry during the Acts 9–28 overlap. Its direct recipients are members of the Body of Christ, yet individual sections still address particular questions and circumstances. Paul’s authorship does not eliminate the need to identify the subject, audience, and purpose of each passage.

Structure and Outline

  1. Divisions and the wisdom of the cross — 1 Corinthians 1–4
    Paul confronts party spirit and redefines wisdom, ministry, and boasting through Christ crucified.
  2. Holiness, discipline, and embodied life — 1 Corinthians 5–7
    The assembly must address immorality, disputes, sexual conduct, marriage, singleness, and calling.
  3. Liberty governed by love — 1 Corinthians 8–10
    Knowledge, conscience, apostolic self-denial, idolatry, and edification govern Christian freedom.
  4. Gathered worship and spiritual gifts — 1 Corinthians 11–14
    Paul addresses order, the Lord’s Supper, the one Body, love, intelligibility, and edification.
  5. The resurrection and final matters — 1 Corinthians 15–16
    Christ’s resurrection guarantees the believer’s resurrection and shapes steadfast service.

Major Themes

The Cross and Divine Wisdom

God’s wisdom overturns human boasting. The crucified Christ exposes status-seeking and supplies the pattern for ministry.

Holiness in the Body

Believers belong to Christ in embodied life. Sexual conduct, discipline, marriage, and mutual responsibility are gospel matters.

Liberty, Conscience, and Love

Freedom is not self-assertion. Mature knowledge willingly limits itself to avoid idolatry, protect conscience, and seek another’s good.

One Body and Ordered Edification

Diverse gifts come from one Spirit and serve one Body. Love and understandable instruction govern gathered ministry.

Bodily Resurrection

Christ’s historical resurrection is foundational. His resurrection guarantees the future transformation of those who belong to Him.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Follow Paul’s movement from reported problems to the assembly’s questions rather than treating every paragraph as an unrelated rule.
  • Distinguish the gospel facts in 15:1–4 from the full range of doctrines Paul connects to Christ’s death and resurrection elsewhere.
  • Israel’s wilderness history in chapter 10 functions as admonition; its later use does not reassign Israel’s covenant identity to the Body of Christ.
  • Read chapters 12–14 together. The existence of a gift, its proper exercise during the Acts overlap, and its present governing status are related but distinct questions.
  • Do not isolate chapter 13 from its assembly context: love governs gifted service and endures beyond partial modes of knowledge.
  • Meaning first, assignment second, and application third remains necessary even in an epistle written directly to a Body-of-Christ assembly.

Study Questions

  1. How does the cross answer Corinthian factionalism?
  2. What principles govern discipline and restoration in chapters 5–6?
  3. How does Paul balance liberty, knowledge, conscience, and love?
  4. What disorders at the Lord’s Supper contradict the meaning of the gathering?
  5. How do chapters 12–14 connect diversity, unity, love, and edification?
  6. Why is bodily resurrection essential to Paul’s gospel argument and practical appeal?
