Sanctification Under Grace

Positional Holiness, the Call to Holy Conduct, and the Spirit's Ongoing Transformation

A Biblical Examination of Positional, Practical, and Progressive Sanctification Under the Mystery Revealed Through Paul

Believers use the word "sanctification" often and define it loosely — usually as a synonym for getting better, trying harder, or growing up spiritually. Scripture uses the word more precisely than that, and for more than one reality at once. Paul calls the notoriously immature Corinthian church "sanctified in Christ Jesus" in the very letter where he confronts their sin (1 Cor. 1:2) — a statement that cannot be describing their present conduct.

This study isolates three distinct senses in Paul's sanctification vocabulary — positional, practical, and progressive — and traces each through its own grammar: a perfect-tense fact already true in Christ, a present command with concrete moral content, and an ongoing transformation worked by the Spirit. It also distinguishes the Body's sanctification under grace from Israel's holiness under the Mosaic Law, so the two programs are never merged.

ABOUT THIS STUDY

One Word, Three Senses, and a Program It Does Not Belong To

Scripture uses "sanctify" and its related words for three distinct realities in the life of the Body of Christ, each with its own grammar: positional sanctification is a perfect-tense fact, already true in Christ; practical sanctification is a present imperative with concrete moral content; and progressive sanctification is a present passive process, the Spirit's ongoing work as the believer beholds Christ's glory. Collapsing any two of these into one is what produces most confusion about sanctification.

This study is not a study of the believer's whole identity in Christ, which Study 16 surveys broadly, nor of the moment-by-moment conflict between the flesh and the Spirit, which Study 44 examines from Galatians 5 and Romans 8, nor of the believer's new-creation standing, which Study 37 examines on its own terms. It assumes each of those and asks a narrower question: how many distinct things is Paul actually saying when he uses sanctification language, and how does each relate to Israel's holiness under the Law?

How These Studies Relate

Study 45 isolates Paul's three senses of sanctification — positional, practical, and progressive — and distinguishes each from Israel's holiness under the Mosaic covenant.

Study 16 — The Believer's Identity in Christ Start with the believer's settled identity and standing, of which positional sanctification is one accomplished member; Study 45 isolates that member and asks what it specifically means and where its limits lie.

Study 44 — Walking in the Spirit Read the moment-by-moment conflict between flesh and Spirit that produces the yielding Romans 6 commands; this study uses Romans 6 only for its practical-sanctification outcome, "unto holiness," not as a general exposition of that conflict.

Study 37 — What Does Paul Mean by "New Creation"? Examine the believer's new-creation standing on its own terms; this study distinguishes that standing from the three senses of sanctification without repeating its argument.

KEY SCRIPTURES

Examine the Passages for Yourself

1 Corinthians 1:2, 30

"Sanctified in Christ Jesus" as an accomplished fact, grouped with righteousness and redemption.

1 Corinthians 6:11

Washed, sanctified, and justified — the same tense, describing the same past transaction.

1 Thessalonians 4:3–5

"This is the will of God, even your sanctification" — a present will with concrete content.

1 Thessalonians 4:6–8

Grounded in apostolic instruction and the gift of the Spirit, not in Israel's covenant Law.

Romans 6:19, 22

"Fruit unto holiness" — the outcome of a yielded life, not a repeated re-acquisition of standing.

2 Corinthians 3:18

Beholding the Lord's glory, "changed into the same image from glory to glory."

Leviticus 20:7–8

Israel's holiness commanded on its own covenant ground, bound to the Mosaic statutes.

1 John 1:8–10

No believer, at any stage of this transformation, may rightly claim to be without sin.

FROM THE STUDY

Three Verb Forms, Three Distinct Realities

Positional sanctification is a perfect-tense fact; practical sanctification is a present imperative; progressive sanctification is a present passive process.

Each of Paul's three senses carries its own grammar, and collapsing any two into one — treating position as something still to be earned, or treating progressive transformation as identical to either the standing or the command — produces the very confusion these distinct verb forms were written to prevent.
