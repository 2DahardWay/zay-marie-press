Nahum

Nineveh’s fall and divine justice.

TestamentOld Testament
GenreMinor Prophets
Primary SettingJudah before Nineveh’s destruction in 612 BC
Key MovementFrom the LORD’s avenging character to the collapse of Assyria
On This PagePurposeHistorical and Biblical ContextStructure and OutlineMajor ThemesKey PassagesInterpretive ConsiderationsStudy Questions

Purpose

Nahum announces the fall of Nineveh and comforts Judah with the end of Assyrian oppression. The prophecy holds together divine patience, power, justice, refuge, and judgment against a violent empire that once received mercy but returned to cruelty.

Historical and Biblical Context

Assyria had destroyed the northern kingdom and terrorized surrounding nations. Nahum addresses Nineveh at the height or memory of imperial power while foretelling its irreversible ruin.

Evidence and inference: Prophetic texts join historical setting, covenant proclamation, symbolic imagery, and future promise. This summary distinguishes explicit textual claims from debated conclusions about date, structure, chronology, or fulfillment.

Structure and Outline

  1. The LORD comes in judgment — Nahum 1
    A hymn portrays divine jealousy, patience, power, wrath, and refuge.
  2. Nineveh besieged — Nahum 2
    The destroyer advances, defenses fail, and the lion’s den is emptied.
  3. The bloody city exposed — Nahum 3
    Violence, exploitation, and arrogance bring shame and final collapse.

Major Themes

Divine Justice

God opposes imperial violence and will not clear persistent guilt.

Refuge

The same LORD who judges is good and knows those who trust Him.

Imperial Pride

Military power and wealth cannot secure Nineveh against God.

Good News for Judah

Assyria’s fall ends a yoke and permits renewed worship.

Key Passages

Nahum 1:2–8God’s avenging justice is joined to patience and refuge.
Nahum 1:12–15Judah receives assurance that Assyrian oppression will end.
Nahum 2:1–13Nineveh’s siege and plunder are vividly portrayed.
Nahum 3:1–7The bloody city’s violence is exposed.
Nahum 3:8–19Past empires and present defenses cannot avert the incurable wound.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Read divine vengeance as judicial opposition to violent evil, not as private human retaliation.
  • Nahum complements rather than contradicts Jonah: mercy after repentance does not guarantee immunity for later wickedness.
  • Poetic taunt and battle imagery serve the oracle against a historical empire.
  • Comfort is addressed to Judah within the Prophecy Program.

Study Questions

  1. How does Nahum 1 unite justice and refuge?
  2. Why is Nineveh called the bloody city?
  3. How does the book reverse Assyria’s lion imagery?
  4. In what sense is Nineveh’s fall good news?
