                          ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 1
Preface
This volume argues a position. It does not collect what churches say about the
prophets and sacrifice and leave the reader to choose. It says what the author
believes the prophets themselves teach about the worship of Israel, it gives the
evidence from their own sentences, and it answers the strongest objections in the
objectors’ words, so that a reader who disagrees knows exactly where.
The question is narrow, and the prophets raise it. Samuel asks it of a king: “Hath the
LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the
LORD?” (1 Samuel 15:22). Isaiah has the LORD say of a crowded temple, “To what
purpose is the multitude of your sacrifices unto me?” (Isaiah 1:11). Amos has him say,
“I hate, I despise your feast days” (Amos 5:21). Hosea has him say, “For I desired
mercy, and not sacrifice” (Hosea 6:6). Many readers have taken these sentences to
mean that the LORD, through his prophets, set aside the sacrifices he had
commanded. Others have taken them to mean that he cared only for the heart and
nothing for the altar. The volume asks what each prophet condemns, in whose
worship, and what he leaves standing.
The position is this. The prophets condemn the worshipers and the way they worship,
and they never condemn the worship the LORD commanded. In every indictment the
offerings are named as Israel’s own, brought to the LORD’s house at the LORD’s
appointment. The charge falls on what the worshiper does outside the house and on
what he thinks the offering buys. The sentence is judgment on the house and the
people, and not abolition of the order. The remedy the prophets name is turning back,
and the offerings reappear in their own hope of a purified people. The sentences that
seem to say otherwise, “mercy, and not sacrifice” and “I spake not unto your fathers …
concerning burnt offerings,” are statements of rank. They say what the LORD asked
first and what he asks above all.
Three things about the method. First, the volume reads the King James Version only,
and every Scripture quotation is checked word for word against it. Second, each
chapter opens with a claim, reads its passages in order, and closes with a short table
and a list of what the volume takes from the passage, so that the reader can see what
has been argued and test it. Third, where modern writers are named, they are named
for a position the author has read in a free text, and where an objection is stated, it is
stated as the objector would state it and then answered from the verses.
The volume does not take up what its neighbours argue. The Kingdom temple of
Ezekiel 40–48 and the sacrifices written there are read in Typology Commentary
Volume 1; the priesthood of Aaron and Melchizedek in Typology Commentary Volume
3; the scope of the phrase the law or the prophets in Study 62 and the Law’s
jurisdiction in Study 59; the Sabbath of Isaiah 58:13 and 66:23 in Study 78; Malachi
3:8–12 on giving in Study 47; and Stephen’s use of Amos 5:25–27 in Acts
Commentary Volume 1. This volume cites them and does not repeat them. It reads
Hebrews 10:5–10 inside Israel’s line, on the letter’s own audience markers and the
covenant it quotes, and builds no doctrine for the Body of Christ from the prophets’
charges. The charges were spoken to Israel about Israel’s worship, and the volume
keeps them there.

A reader may test the thesis in one move. Take any prophetic condemnation of
worship and ask four questions of it in order: whose offering is it, what does the LORD
say against it, what does he do about it, and what does he leave standing? If a prophet
anywhere says the order itself is wrong, or that the LORD never commanded it, the
thesis fails. If each indictment instead names the offering as Israel’s own, charges the
worshiper, and sentences the house and people while holding the way back open, the
thesis stands, and the reader will have seen why the prophets can say hard things
about sacrifice and yet speak of the offerings of a purified people.


1. The Earlier Voices
Claim: before any writing prophet speaks against the sacrifices, a prophet and a king
speak from inside the Law, and what they say sets the pattern the later prophets
follow. Samuel sets obedience above sacrifice and then goes back with the king to
the place of worship. David says the LORD did not desire sacrifice and offering, and
then says what he delights to do. Asaph hears the LORD say he will not reprove Israel
for its sacrifices, and then hears him reprove the worshipers. In none of the passages
is the order abolished.

1.1 Saul at Gilgal
The earliest sentence in the series is spoken to a king who has kept part of a
command. The LORD had sent Saul to “utterly destroy the sinners the Amalekites” (1
Samuel 15:18). Saul comes back with the best of the sheep and oxen, and Samuel
hears them: “What meaneth then this bleating of the sheep in mine ears, and the
lowing of the oxen which I hear?” (1 Samuel 15:14). Saul says the people spared the
best “to sacrifice unto the LORD thy God” (1 Samuel 15:15), and again, of the chief of
the spoil, “to sacrifice unto the LORD thy God in Gilgal” (1 Samuel 15:21).
The offering Saul describes is real. It is a sacrifice to the LORD, at a place of sacrifice,
from the best of the flock. The text does not say the offering would have been
wrongly made if it had been made in obedience. It says the animals were taken
against a command, and the pretext for the taking was the offering. Saul’s sentence is
the first form of a mistake the prophets will name again and again: that the offering
can be brought as a payment to cover what the worshiper has done.
1.2 To obey is better than sacrifice
Samuel answers with a question and a statement. “Hath the LORD as great delight in
burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is
better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams” (1 Samuel 15:22). The
grammar of the sentence is a comparison. Burnt offerings and sacrifices are named
as things in which the LORD has delight. Obedience is set above them. The verse
does not say that the LORD has no delight in sacrifice. It says he has more delight in
the voice being obeyed.
The next verse names what Saul has done in the terms of the Law’s gravest sins. “For
rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft, and stubbornness is as iniquity and idolatry.
Because thou hast rejected the word of the LORD, he hath also rejected thee from
being king” (1 Samuel 15:23). The charge is the rejection of the word of the LORD. The
sacrifice is not what is rejected. Saul himself confesses in the following verse, “I have
sinned: for I have transgressed the commandment of the LORD, and thy words” (1
Samuel 15:24), and he asks Samuel to “turn again with me, that I may worship the
LORD” (1 Samuel 15:25).
Samuel first refuses, then goes. “I will not return with thee: for thou hast rejected the
word of the LORD, and the LORD hath rejected thee from being king over Israel” (1
Samuel 15:26). But the chapter says a few verses later that “Samuel turned again
after Saul; and Saul worshipped the LORD” (1 Samuel 15:31). The prophet who has
just said obedience is better than sacrifice goes back with the king to the place of
worship. He does not take the king’s worship to be void. He takes the king’s rule to be
forfeited. The sentence of 15:22 is therefore a statement of rank inside a worship the
prophet shares, and the pattern is already set: the offering stands, the man who
brings it in disobedience is judged.
1.3 David: sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire
The Psalms add two voices from within the same order. In the fortieth psalm David
says, “Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire; mine ears hast thou opened: burnt
offering and sin offering hast thou not required” (Psalm 40:6). The sentence names
four kinds of offering, sacrifice, offering, burnt offering and sin offering, and says the
LORD did not desire or require them. The next two verses give what he did desire.

“Then said I, Lo, I come: in the volume of the book it is written of me, I delight to do thy
will, O my God: yea, thy law is within my heart” (Psalm 40:7, 8).
The psalm sets the offerings beside the law. What is placed within the heart is the
law, and the delight is in doing the will of God. The singer does not repudiate the
book. He says it is written of him, and the law is within his heart. The sentence of
verse 6 is the “not … but” that the volume will meet again at Hosea 6:6 and Jeremiah
7:22: not these offerings as the thing the LORD wants, but the will done from the
heart. The psalm goes on, in verses 9 and 10, to the public declaration of what the
singer has done: “I have preached righteousness in the great congregation: lo, I have
not refrained my lips, O LORD, thou knowest” (Psalm 40:9). The great congregation is
Israel assembled for worship, and the psalmist speaks there.
The wording of verse 6, “mine ears hast thou opened,” is the sentence Hebrews 10
will quote in a different form. Chapter 8 reads that quotation. For now it is enough to
say that Psalm 40:6 in the King James Version says what it says, and that the volume
builds on no difference of wording.
1.4 Asaph: I will not reprove thee for thy sacrifices
The fiftieth psalm is the clearest of the earlier voices on what the LORD leaves
standing. God speaks as judge (Psalm 50:6), and he begins with his people: “Gather
my saints together unto me; those that have made a covenant with me by sacrifice”
(Psalm 50:5). The people are defined by a covenant made by sacrifice, and the LORD
says so before he reproves them. Then comes the address: “Hear, O my people, and I
will speak; O Israel, and I will testify against thee: I am God, even thy God” (Psalm
50:7).
The first thing the LORD says is what he does not charge. “I will not reprove thee for
thy sacrifices or thy burnt offerings, to have been continually before me” (Psalm 50:8).
The sentence is an explicit statement that the sacrifices and burnt offerings,
continually before him, are not the ground of the reproof. What follows is a rebuke of
a mistaken idea of what the sacrifices are. “I will take no bullock out of thy house, nor
he goats out of thy folds. For every beast of the forest is mine, and the cattle upon a
thousand hills” (Psalm 50:9, 10). “If I were hungry, I would not tell thee: for the world is
mine, and the fulness thereof. Will I eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats?”
(Psalm 50:12, 13).
The LORD does not say he has given no sacrifices. He says he does not need them as
a man needs food, and that the people must not think of the offering as something
they supply to him. In its place he names thank-offering and vow: “Offer unto God
thanksgiving; and pay thy vows unto the most High: and call upon me in the day of
trouble: I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify me” (Psalm 50:14, 15). Thanksgiving
and vows are the language of the sacrifices of peace and of vows in Israel’s worship,
and the LORD asks for them. The closing verse of the psalm says the same: “Whoso
offereth praise glorifieth me: and to him that ordereth his conversation aright will I
shew the salvation of God” (Psalm 50:23).
The second half of the psalm turns to the worshiper who recites the covenant and
lives otherwise. “But unto the wicked God saith, What hast thou to do to declare my
statutes, or that thou shouldest take my covenant in thy mouth? Seeing thou hatest
instruction, and castest my words behind thee” (Psalm 50:16, 17). The list is of
conduct, theft, adultery, deceit and slander (Psalm 50:18–20), and the LORD adds,



                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 5
“These things hast thou done, and I kept silence; thou thoughtest that I was altogether
such an one as thyself: but I will reprove thee, and set them in order before thine eyes”
(Psalm 50:21). The charge is a misjudgment of the LORD, that he is such a one as the
worshiper, and it rests on the worshiper’s life, not on the altar.
1.5 David again: a broken spirit, then the walls, then the offerings
The fifty-first psalm is David’s confession after his sin, and it contains the sentence
most often quoted against the altar. “For thou desirest not sacrifice; else would I give
it: thou delightest not in burnt offering” (Psalm 51:16). It follows a prayer for
cleansing, “Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin”
(Psalm 51:2), and an acknowledgment that the sin is “against thee, thee only” (Psalm
51:4). The singer says that if sacrifice would answer, he would give it. He comes with
no offering. What he brings is stated in the next verse. “The sacrifices of God are a
broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise” (Psalm
51:17).
Verses 18 and 19 are the sentences that decide how verse 16 is read. “Do good in thy
good pleasure unto Zion: build thou the walls of Jerusalem. Then shalt thou be
pleased with the sacrifices of righteousness, with burnt offering and whole burnt
offering: then shall they offer bullocks upon thine altar” (Psalm 51:18, 19). The psalm
that said the LORD does not delight in burnt offering ends by saying that when Zion is
blessed and Jerusalem’s walls are built, the LORD will be pleased with burnt offering
and whole burnt offering, and bullocks will be offered on his altar. The same singer, in
the same psalm, says both. The only reading that keeps both is the reading of rank
and of order: the offering is not what covers a sin of this kind, and it is received with
pleasure when it is offered by those who have come with a broken heart and in a
restored city.
The volume reports verses 18 and 19 as written. It does not say when the walls are
built or which altar is meant, and it builds nothing on the time. It says what the psalm
says: the singer expects burnt offering and bullocks upon the altar to please the
LORD.

1.6 What the volume takes from the passages
 • Samuel, David and Asaph speak from inside the Law and the worship of Israel;
   none of them stands outside it to condemn it.
 • Each sentence against sacrifice is a sentence of rank or of misuse: obedience
   above the fat of rams, the will done from the heart above the four offerings, the
   thank-offering and the vow above the supposed food of the altar, a broken spirit
   above a sacrifice that cannot cover this sin.
 • Psalm 50:8 states in so many words what the LORD does not charge: “I will not
   reprove thee for thy sacrifices.”
 • Psalm 51:18, 19 and 1 Samuel 15:31 show the offerings and the worship still
   standing in the same passages.
 • The pattern is set before the writing prophets: the sentence falls on the one who
   brings, in disobedience or in the thought that the LORD is such an one as himself.


2. Isaiah 1: The Crowded House
Claim: the first chapter of Isaiah is the longest and plainest indictment of worship in
the prophets, and it condemns neither the offerings nor the appointed times. It
condemns a people whose hands are full of blood coming to a house the LORD has
already said he is weary of seeing them tread. It names the remedy in nine
commands, offers to reason with them, and ends with a purged and restored city. The
order of worship is the setting of the charge from the first verse to the last.
2.1 The setting: Judah and Jerusalem
The chapter says whom it is about before it says anything about worship. “The vision
of Isaiah the son of Amoz, which he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem in the days
of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah” (Isaiah 1:1). The LORD has
“nourished and brought up children, and they have rebelled against me” (Isaiah 1:2).
The nation is “laden with iniquity,” a people who “have forsaken the LORD” (Isaiah
1:4). The land is desolate and the cities burned (Isaiah 1:7). Verse 9 says that “except
the LORD of hosts had left unto us a very small remnant,” Judah would have been like
Sodom and Gomorrah.
The next verse takes the names from the verse before. “Hear the word of the LORD, ye
rulers of Sodom; give ear unto the law of our God, ye people of Gomorrah” (Isaiah
1:10). Judah’s rulers are called rulers of Sodom, and the prophet’s message is called


                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 7
the law of our God. The word the prophet speaks is set beside the law, not against it.
What follows is not a new teaching about what God wants in place of what he
commanded. It is the application of what God commanded to those who handle it
wrongly.
2.2 The multitude of your sacrifices
The first question is about quantity. “To what purpose is the multitude of your
sacrifices unto me? saith the LORD: I am full of the burnt offerings of rams, and the
fat of fed beasts; and I delight not in the blood of bullocks, or of lambs, or of he goats”
(Isaiah 1:11). Three things in the sentence are worth marking. The sacrifices are
called “your sacrifices unto me,” offerings brought to him. The sentence speaks of a
multitude: the problem is the pile, and the idea in the pile. And the verb is “delight,” the
same verb Samuel used when he asked whether the LORD had as great delight in
burnt offerings as in obeying his voice (1 Samuel 15:22). The comparison of rank is in
the background again. The LORD says he has more than enough of these and delights
in none of them as they are brought.
The second question is about the one who requires. “When ye come to appear before
me, who hath required this at your hand, to tread my courts?” (Isaiah 1:12). The
question can be misread as a denial that the LORD commanded anything. It is not
that. The Law had appointed the courts, the offerings and the coming to appear. The
question is who has required this at their hand: this treading, this coming, in this state.
The sentence asks whether the LORD requires of men the visit of men with blood on
their hands. The courts are “my courts.” The LORD does not disown the house. He
speaks of it as his own.
2.3 The appointed times named and refused
Verses 13 and 14 run through the calendar. “Bring no more vain oblations; incense is
an abomination unto me; the new moons and sabbaths, the calling of assemblies, I
cannot away with; it is iniquity, even the solemn meeting. Your new moons and your
appointed feasts my soul hateth: they are a trouble unto me; I am weary to bear them”
(Isaiah 1:13, 14). The oblation, the incense, the new moons, the sabbaths, the
assemblies and the feasts are the Law’s own list. The LORD calls them vain, an
abomination, a trouble and a weariness in the hands of this people. The phrase “your
appointed feasts” is the plainest evidence in the passage that these are the appointed
times. The word “appointed” belongs to the Law’s own vocabulary for the feasts, and
it is used here against the way the people keep them.
The text gives the reason in verse 13: “it is iniquity, even the solemn meeting.” The
meeting and the iniquity are joined. The LORD says he cannot away with the assembly
of those who bring iniquity into it. The prophet is not saying that assembling is
iniquity. He is saying that a solemn meeting held by those who hold on to wickedness
is itself made iniquity by the joining. The next verses say what the iniquity is.
2.4 The hands and the prayers
“And when ye spread forth your hands, I will hide mine eyes from you: yea, when ye
make many prayers, I will not hear: your hands are full of blood” (Isaiah 1:15). The
offering is joined here to prayer, and the LORD hides his eyes from the one and does
not hear the other. The reason is in the last clause: “your hands are full of blood.” The
hands that are spread forth in prayer and that bring the sacrifice are the hands that
have shed it in the street. Verse 17 names the shape of the sin in the city: the


                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 8
oppressed, the fatherless and the widow are the ones whose cause is neglected, and
verse 23 says that the princes “judge not the fatherless, neither doth the cause of the
widow come unto them.”
The text offers two things the prophet could have said and did not say. He did not say
that the hands should stop offering. He did not say that prayer is useless. He said that
these hands and these prayers are not heard, and then he told them what to do about
it.
2.5 Wash you: the nine commands
Verses 16 and 17 are the turn of the chapter. “Wash you, make you clean; put away
the evil of your doings from before mine eyes; cease to do evil; learn to do well; seek
judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow” (Isaiah
1:16, 17). Count the verbs: wash, make clean, put away, cease, learn, seek, relieve,
judge, plead. They are commands of conduct, and none of them is a command to
stop sacrificing or to stop keeping the feasts. The washing is a washing of the doings
“from before mine eyes.” The prophet joins the language of cleansing to the language
of justice: what makes the worshiper clean is the putting away of evil and the doing of
justice for those with no one to speak for them.
The remedy is also an offer. “Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD:
though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow; though they be red like
crimson, they shall be as wool” (Isaiah 1:18). The LORD invites argument and
promises cleansing. Then the two ways are set out: “If ye be willing and obedient, ye
shall eat the good of the land: but if ye refuse and rebel, ye shall be devoured with the
sword: for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it” (Isaiah 1:19, 20). The conditional
word is the one Volume 1 traced in the prophets’ rule for announced judgment and
blessing. The people’s response decides what the LORD does.
2.6 The city purged and restored
The chapter ends with a verdict on the city and a promise for it. “How is the faithful
city become an harlot! it was full of judgment; righteousness lodged in it; but now
murderers” (Isaiah 1:21). The silver has become dross and the wine is mixed with
water (Isaiah 1:22). The LORD will “turn my hand upon thee, and purely purge away thy
dross, and take away all thy tin” (Isaiah 1:25). Then he will “restore thy judges as at
the first, and thy counsellors as at the beginning: afterward thou shalt be called, The
city of righteousness, the faithful city” (Isaiah 1:26). The chapter ends where it began,
with Zion: “Zion shall be redeemed with judgment, and her converts with
righteousness” (Isaiah 1:27).
The sentence of the chapter on the house is therefore a purging and a restoring, and
the pattern is the one the earlier voices set. The offerings are not the dross. The
judges, the counsellors and the princes who love gifts are. When the city is restored it
is called the city of righteousness, and nothing in the chapter says that the house, the
feasts or the offerings will not be part of that city. The chapter is silent on that, and
the volume does not fill the silence. It says only that nothing in Isaiah 1 proposes an
end of the order, and that the whole chapter is addressed to Judah and Jerusalem, in
the days of four named kings.

 • Isaiah 1:10 calls the prophet’s message the law of our God; the condemnation is

 • The chapter ends with the LORD purging the city and restoring it, and it says
   nothing of abolishing the order within it.


3. Amos: Bethel, Gilgal and the Feast Days
Claim: Amos is the sharpest of the prophets on feasts and offerings, and he speaks to
the northern kingdom at the sanctuaries its king had set up. His charge is double. The
altar at Bethel and the feast Jeroboam devised were not the worship the LORD
commanded, and the people who kept the feasts and brought the offerings, wherever
they brought them, trampled the poor. His remedy is to seek the LORD and let justice
run down like waters, and he gives no sentence that the order the LORD gave Israel
was a mistake.

3.1 A man of Tekoa speaking to the north
The book opens with the man and the place. “The words of Amos, who was among
the herdmen of Tekoa, which he saw concerning Israel in the days of Uzziah king of
Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash king of Israel, two years before
the earthquake” (Amos 1:1). Tekoa is in Judah and the king named first is Judah’s.
The subject is Israel, the northern kingdom. The first thing Amos says in the LORD’s
name points south: “The LORD will roar from Zion, and utter his voice from
Jerusalem” (Amos 1:2). The prophet sends his word from Jerusalem to the north.
That direction matters for what follows, because the sanctuary he goes against is not
Jerusalem’s.
The origin of the northern sanctuaries is given in the First Book of Kings. Jeroboam
feared that if the people went up “to do sacrifice in the house of the LORD at
Jerusalem,” their hearts would turn back to Rehoboam (1 Kings 12:27). So the king
“made two calves of gold,” saying, “It is too much for you to go up to Jerusalem” (1
Kings 12:28). He set one in Bethel and the other in Dan (1 Kings 12:29). He “made an
house of high places, and made priests of the lowest of the people, which were not of
the sons of Levi” (1 Kings 12:31). He “ordained a feast in the eighth month, on the
fifteenth day of the month, like unto the feast that is in Judah” and offered upon the
altar at Bethel, “even in the month which he had devised of his own heart” (1 Kings
12:32, 33). Every element of the northern worship is named as the king’s own: the
place, the calves, the priests, the month. The text says in so many words that the
month was devised of his own heart.
Amos meets the result in the last chapters of the book. Amaziah, the priest of Bethel,
tells him, “prophesy not again any more at Bethel: for it is the king’s chapel, and it is
the king’s court” (Amos 7:13). The priest’s own words say what Bethel was: the king’s
chapel. The LORD had foretold it: “I will also visit the altars of Bethel: and the horns of
the altar shall be cut off, and fall to the ground” (Amos 3:14). The volume reads the
altars of Bethel as the altars Jeroboam made, not as the altar at Jerusalem, and the
prophet’s sentence against them is a sentence against a worship the LORD did not
command.
3.2 Come to Bethel, and transgress
Amos 4:4, 5 is the prophet’s irony. “Come to Bethel, and transgress; at Gilgal multiply
transgression; and bring your sacrifices every morning, and your tithes after three
years: and offer a sacrifice of thanksgiving with leaven, and proclaim and publish the
free offerings: for this liketh you, O ye children of Israel, saith the Lord GOD.” The
commands are in the form of a call to worship and mean the opposite. At Bethel, the
call is to transgress. At Gilgal, to multiply transgression. The sacrifices, the tithes, the
thank-offering and the free offerings are real, and the people bring them with zeal,
every morning and on the third year. The reason is in the last clause: “for this liketh
you.” The worship is what pleases the worshipers.
The next verses show what the LORD did while they offered. “And I also have given
you cleanness of teeth in all your cities, and want of bread in all your places: yet have
ye not returned unto me, saith the LORD” (Amos 4:6). The sentence is repeated
across the chapter as a refrain of judgments that did not turn them. The offering was
brought and the LORD was not returned to. Verse 12 closes the chapter: “prepare to




                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 11
meet thy God, O Israel.” The offering at the wrong altar and the return of the heart are
set side by side, and the first is no substitute for the second.
3.3 Seek me, and not Bethel
Chapter 5 turns the irony into command. “For thus saith the LORD unto the house of
Israel, Seek ye me, and ye shall live: but seek not Bethel, nor enter into Gilgal, and pass
not to Beersheba: for Gilgal shall surely go into captivity, and Bethel shall come to
nought” (Amos 5:4, 5). The LORD names the places he does not want sought and sets
himself in their place. The sentence is not a command to stop worshiping. It is a
command to seek him, and it names three sanctuaries of the north and the south of
the divided country, Bethel, Gilgal and Beersheba, as the places that are not the
seeking. The same call is repeated: “Seek the LORD, and ye shall live; lest he break out
like fire in the house of Joseph” (Amos 5:6).
The charge that goes with it is of conduct. The people “turn judgment to wormwood,
and leave off righteousness in the earth” (Amos 5:7). They “hate him that rebuketh in
the gate” (Amos 5:10). Their “treading is upon the poor,” and they take “burdens of
wheat” from him (Amos 5:11). They “afflict the just, they take a bribe, and they turn
aside the poor in the gate from their right” (Amos 5:12). The remedy is stated twice:
“Seek good, and not evil, that ye may live: and so the LORD, the God of hosts, shall be
with you, as ye have spoken” (Amos 5:14), and “Hate the evil, and love the good, and
establish judgment in the gate: it may be that the LORD God of hosts will be gracious
unto the remnant of Joseph” (Amos 5:15).
3.4 I hate, I despise your feast days
Verses 18 to 20 speak to those who desire the day of the LORD and are told it is
darkness, not light. Immediately after comes the most quoted sentence of the
prophet. “I hate, I despise your feast days, and I will not smell in your solemn
assemblies. Though ye offer me burnt offerings and your meat offerings, I will not
accept them: neither will I regard the peace offerings of your fat beasts. Take thou
away from me the noise of thy songs; for I will not hear the melody of thy viols. But let
judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream” (Amos 5:21–
24).
The list is complete: feast days, solemn assemblies, burnt offerings, meat offerings,
peace offerings, songs and viols. Most are named with the pronoun: “your” feast days,
“your” meat offerings, “your” fat beasts. The pronoun does the work in the same way
as in Isaiah’s “your appointed feasts.” These are the feasts as this people keep them.
The LORD does not say that burnt offerings are a sin. He says that he will not accept
them from these hands, and that what he wants instead is a flow: “let judgment run
down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream.” A stream is a thing that
keeps running. The prophet asks that justice be as constant in Israel as the water in a
river, and he sets that constancy against the constancy of the feasts.
The Sabbath and the new moon appear again in chapter 8. Amos reports the speech
of the merchants. “When will the new moon be gone, that we may sell corn? and the
sabbath, that we may set forth wheat, making the ephah small, and the shekel great,
and falsifying the balances by deceit? That we may buy the poor for silver, and the
needy for a pair of shoes” (Amos 8:5, 6). The merchants keep the new moon and the
sabbath, and they do not like it. They count the hours until they can cheat again. The
days are observed, and the heart is elsewhere. The sentence is a charge against the



                ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 12
dealers, and it takes the days of rest for granted as days the people kept. The Sabbath
is not argued here; its place in the Law and in the prophets is left to Study 78.
3.5 Have ye offered unto me sacrifices in the wilderness?
The question of Amos 5:25 is the one most often quoted to show that the LORD never
wanted sacrifices. “Have ye offered unto me sacrifices and offerings in the wilderness
forty years, O house of Israel? But ye have borne the tabernacle of your Moloch and
Chiun your images, the star of your god, which ye made to yourselves. Therefore will I
cause you to go into captivity beyond Damascus, saith the LORD, whose name is The
God of hosts” (Amos 5:25–27).
The question has the form of a rhetorical question, and readers differ on the answer it
expects. The volume reads it with the verse that follows, which says that the people
carried the tabernacle of Moloch and the images of their own star-god in the
wilderness. The question does not ask whether the sacrifices were commanded in the
wilderness. It asks whether the offerings were offered “unto me,” to the LORD, by a
people who carried other gods in the same camp. The sentence pairs the offering
with the idols, and the pairing is the charge. The captivity “beyond Damascus” is the
sentence on the whole worship, the offerings and the idols together, and the sentence
falls on the worshipers.
Stephen quotes Amos 5:25–27 in Acts 7:42, 43 in a speech to the council, and the use
is read in Acts Commentary Volume 1; this volume does not repeat it. What Amos
himself says is enough for the present argument. He puts the wilderness sacrifices in
question because of the idols that went with them. He does not say that the Law’s
order was a mistake, and he does not say the offerings had been forbidden.

4. Hosea and Micah: Mercy, and Not Sacrifice
Claim: Hosea 6:6 and Micah 6:6–8 are the two sentences most often read as the
prophets’ rejection of sacrifice, and each is a statement of rank inside a charge
against the worshipers. Hosea’s verse is built as a pair of lines that interpret each
other, and the second line says “more than.” Micah’s verse answers a worshiper who
asks how much he must bring by naming what the LORD has already shown, and the
verses after it return to false weights and deceit. Neither prophet proposes that the
offerings cease. Hosea lists the loss of sacrifice among the judgments, and Micah
asks for justice, mercy and humility as the walk of a man who comes.
4.1 Hosea’s controversy: no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God
Hosea opens his indictment with a word that will return in chapter 6. “Hear the word
of the LORD, ye children of Israel: for the LORD hath a controversy with the inhabitants
of the land, because there is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land”
(Hosea 4:1). The three missing things are truth, mercy and the knowledge of God. The
next verse lists the conduct: “By swearing, and lying, and killing, and stealing, and
committing adultery, they break out, and blood toucheth blood” (Hosea 4:2). The
LORD’s controversy is the same lawsuit Isaiah and Micah bring, and its first count is
the absence of mercy and of the knowledge of God, the pair that stands again in
Hosea 6:6.
The priests are charged next. “My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge:
because thou hast rejected knowledge, I will also reject thee, that thou shalt be no
priest to me: seeing thou hast forgotten the law of thy God, I will also forget thy
children” (Hosea 4:6). The prophet does not say the priesthood is wrong. He says this
priest has rejected knowledge and forgotten the law and will be no priest to the LORD.
The next verses speak of the priests in the same terms: “They eat up the sin of my
people, and they set their heart on their iniquity” (Hosea 4:8). Many readers take the
verse to mean that the priests profit from the sin offerings and so want the people to



                ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 14
sin. The volume reads it so, with the note that the verse does not itself name the sin
offering, and in either case it is a charge against the priests.
The worship at the high places is charged separately. “They sacrifice upon the tops of
the mountains, and burn incense upon the hills, under oaks and poplars and elms,
because the shadow thereof is good” (Hosea 4:13). The reason the verse gives is that
the shadow is good. The place is chosen for the worshipers’ comfort, and it is a place
the LORD had not appointed. Hosea 5:6 adds a sentence that no later reading can
soften: “They shall go with their flocks and with their herds to seek the LORD; but they
shall not find him; he hath withdrawn himself from them.” The LORD is not found by
the flocks and herds that are brought to seek him. The offerings are not what he is
withholding.
4.2 Hosea 6:1–7: the sentence in its place
Chapter 6 opens with Israel’s own words of return. “Come, and let us return unto the
LORD: for he hath torn, and he will heal us; he hath smitten, and he will bind us up.
After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up, and we shall live in
his sight” (Hosea 6:1, 2). The LORD does not take the words as a turning. “O Ephraim,
what shall I do unto thee? O Judah, what shall I do unto thee? for your goodness is as
a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away” (Hosea 6:4). The word the King
James Version renders “goodness” in this verse is the same word it renders “mercy”
in verse 6. The people’s goodness vanishes like the morning mist. What the LORD
wants is a goodness that does not.
Verse 6 follows. “For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God
more than burnt offerings” (Hosea 6:6). The verse is two lines, and they are a pair. The
first says mercy and not sacrifice. The second says the knowledge of God more than
burnt offerings. The second line is the key to the first. It uses “more than,” the
language of comparison, and so shows how the pair is to be read: mercy over
sacrifice, the knowledge of God over burnt offerings. The same pairing of “not” and
“more than” appears in the earlier voices (1 Samuel 15:22, “to obey is better than
sacrifice”). The volume reads the verse as a statement of rank and not of abolition.
Verse 7 states the reason. “But they like men have transgressed the covenant: there
have they dealt treacherously against me” (Hosea 6:7). The offence is the broken
covenant. The sacrifices are named as the thing mercy outranks; the covenant is
named as the thing broken. Hosea 8:12 adds, “I have written to him the great things of
my law, but they were counted as a strange thing.” The people have the law in writing
and treat it as foreign.
4.3 My offerings, and the loss of sacrifice
Two further sentences in Hosea show what the LORD leaves standing. The first is in
chapter 8. “Because Ephraim hath made many altars to sin, altars shall be unto him to
sin. … They sacrifice flesh for the sacrifices of mine offerings, and eat it; but the LORD
accepteth them not; now will he remember their iniquity, and visit their sins: they shall
return to Egypt” (Hosea 8:11, 13). The LORD calls them “the sacrifices of mine
offerings.” He does not disown the offerings, and he does not accept the worshipers.
The charge is the making of many altars to sin, and the sentence is a return to Egypt.
The second is in chapter 3, in the account of what the exile will take. “For the children
of Israel shall abide many days without a king, and without a prince, and without a



                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 15
sacrifice, and without an image, and without an ephod, and without teraphim:
afterward shall the children of Israel return, and seek the LORD their God, and David
their king; and shall fear the LORD and his goodness in the latter days” (Hosea 3:4, 5).
The list mixes things the LORD gave Israel and things he condemned, and the volume
does not sort it. It reports one thing without comment: sacrifice is named among the
losses, with king and prince, and the return that follows is a return to the LORD and to
David their king. The prophet who says the LORD desired mercy and not sacrifice also
counts the absence of sacrifice as part of the judgment.
The last chapter gives the people their words. “O Israel, return unto the LORD thy God;
for thou hast fallen by thine iniquity. Take with you words, and turn to the LORD: say
unto him, Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously: so will we render the
calves of our lips” (Hosea 14:1, 2). The word “calves” is the offering, and the offering
is lips: the praise of a pardoned people. Even here, the language is the language of
sacrifice.
4.4 The words in Matthew
Matthew twice has the Lord quote Hosea 6:6. When the Pharisees ask why he eats
with publicans and sinners, he answers, “go ye and learn what that meaneth, I will
have mercy, and not sacrifice: for I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to
repentance” (Matthew 9:13). Again, when the disciples pluck ears of corn on the
sabbath, he says, “if ye had known what this meaneth, I will have mercy, and not
sacrifice, ye would not have condemned the guiltless” (Matthew 12:7). In both, the
Lord is speaking to Pharisees in Israel about how the Law is to be kept, and in both he
sends them to Hosea. In the second, he has just said that “the priests in the temple
profane the sabbath, and are blameless” (Matthew 12:5), and that “in this place is one
greater than the temple” (Matthew 12:6). The Lord takes the temple service for
granted as a service in which priests are blameless.
The volume reports both quotations in their settings and argues neither. Study 78
reads Matthew 12 for the Sabbath and Typology Commentary Volume 5 reports it in
its treatment of the kingdom. For the present argument the point is small and
sufficient: in both places the Lord quotes the verse the way the prophet used it, as a
rule of rank, and he does not use it to end the temple service.
4.5 Micah’s lawsuit
Micah’s sixth chapter is another controversy. “Hear ye, O mountains, the LORD’s
controversy, and ye strong foundations of the earth: for the LORD hath a controversy
with his people, and he will plead with Israel” (Micah 6:2). The LORD’s first words are a
question to the people: “O my people, what have I done unto thee? and wherein have I
wearied thee? testify against me” (Micah 6:3). He recites what he did: “I brought thee
up out of the land of Egypt, and redeemed thee out of the house of servants; and I
sent before thee Moses, Aaron, and Miriam” (Micah 6:4). The case rests on the
LORD’s faithfulness, and the people are asked to remember Balak and Balaam, “that
ye may know the righteousness of the LORD” (Micah 6:5).
Then the worshiper speaks. “Wherewith shall I come before the LORD, and bow
myself before the high God? shall I come before him with burnt offerings, with calves
of a year old? Will the LORD be pleased with thousands of rams, or with ten
thousands of rivers of oil? shall I give my firstborn for my transgression, the fruit of
my body for the sin of my soul?” (Micah 6:6, 7). The questions climb: a burnt offering,



                ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 16
a calf of a year old, thousands of rams, ten thousands of rivers of oil, a firstborn. The
worshiper asks how much will be enough. The question assumes that the offering is a
payment sized to the offence, and that more of it will purchase more.
The prophet’s answer does not say the offerings should stop. “He hath shewed thee,
O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to
love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?” (Micah 6:8). The answer is that the
LORD has already shown what is good, and that what he requires is three things in the
walk of a man. The verse is silent about the offerings. It does not say they are not
required. It says that the thing the worshiper is asking for, the price, is not in the
account. The walk is. The verses after it return to the city: “Are there yet the treasures
of wickedness in the house of the wicked, and the scant measure that is abominable?
Shall I count them pure with the wicked balances, and with the bag of deceitful
weights?” (Micah 6:10, 11). The answer of verse 8 is followed at once by the false
weights, and the offerings of verses 6 and 7 are the thing meant to cover them.

5. Jeremiah: The Temple Sermon and the
Hardest Sentence
Claim: Jeremiah’s sermon at the gate of the LORD’s house is the fullest statement of
what the prophets say about worship by those who trust the house, and it holds a
sentence that seems to deny that the LORD ever commanded sacrifices. Read with
the verses around it and with Exodus 19, that sentence is a statement of rank: the first
word at the Exodus was obedience. Jeremiah himself speaks of burnt offerings,
sacrifices of praise and sacrifices continually in the house of the LORD in the days of
restoration. His judgment falls on a house trusted as a charm, and the pattern of
Shiloh is the sentence.
5.1 Incense from Sheba
The first sentence is in chapter 6. “Hear, O earth: behold, I will bring evil upon this
people, even the fruit of their thoughts, because they have not hearkened unto my
words, nor to my law, but rejected it” (Jeremiah 6:19). The charge is first of all that the
people have not hearkened and have rejected the law. Then comes the question about
the offerings: “To what purpose cometh there to me incense from Sheba, and the
sweet cane from a far country? your burnt offerings are not acceptable, nor your
sacrifices sweet unto me” (Jeremiah 6:20).
The incense came from far away and was costly, and the sweet cane was a rare
spice. The people spared no expense for the house. The LORD says the burnt
offerings are not acceptable and the sacrifices are not sweet to him. The offerings are
the people’s: “your burnt offerings,” “your sacrifices.” The reason is in verse 19, which
comes before the verse and gives the ground of it: the law rejected. The next verse
says what follows, “I will lay stumblingblocks before this people” (Jeremiah 6:21). The
sentence on the offerings is a sentence on those who reject the law in the same
house in which they bring incense.
5.2 The sermon at the gate
Chapter 7 gives the setting of the sermon in the opening verses. “Stand in the gate of
the LORD’s house, and proclaim there this word, and say, Hear the word of the LORD,
all ye of Judah, that enter in at these gates to worship the LORD” (Jeremiah 7:2). The
audience is the worshipers entering to worship. The message is conditional: “Amend
your ways and your doings, and I will cause you to dwell in this place” (Jeremiah 7:3).
The next verse names the false confidence. “Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The
temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, are these”
(Jeremiah 7:4). The repetition is the prophet’s way of quoting a chant. The people say
the name of the house three times as if the name protected them. The prophet calls it
“lying words, that cannot profit” (Jeremiah 7:8). The condition is repeated: “if ye


                ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 18
throughly amend your ways and your doings; if ye throughly execute judgment
between a man and his neighbour; if ye oppress not the stranger, the fatherless, and
the widow, and shed not innocent blood in this place, neither walk after other gods to
your hurt: then will I cause you to dwell in this place, in the land that I gave to your
fathers, for ever and ever” (Jeremiah 7:5–7).
The LORD does not say the house is wrong, and he does not say the people should
stay away from it. He says that dwelling in the place depends on how they live in it.
The “for ever and ever” belongs to the conditional word of Jeremiah 18:7–10, read in
Volume 1, and it is not a sworn word.
5.3 A den of robbers
Then the charge: “Will ye steal, murder, and commit adultery, and swear falsely, and
burn incense unto Baal, and walk after other gods whom ye know not; and come and
stand before me in this house, which is called by my name, and say, We are delivered
to do all these abominations?” (Jeremiah 7:9, 10). The picture is of a worshiper who
sins and then comes to stand in the house, and who says that the standing there
delivers him to go on. The house is “called by my name” twice in two verses. The
LORD claims it. Then the question: “Is this house, which is called by my name,
become a den of robbers in your eyes? Behold, even I have seen it, saith the LORD”
(Jeremiah 7:11). A den is where robbers go to hide between robberies. The house has
become the place where the people hide from the consequences of what they do
outside it.
5.4 Shiloh
The sentence is in the pattern of a precedent. “But go ye now unto my place which
was in Shiloh, where I set my name at the first, and see what I did to it for the
wickedness of my people Israel” (Jeremiah 7:12). Shiloh was the place where the
LORD had set his name first. The ark was there in the days of Eli and Samuel. The
LORD says it was destroyed for the wickedness of his people. Therefore, “will I do
unto this house, which is called by my name, wherein ye trust, and unto the place
which I gave to you and to your fathers, as I have done to Shiloh. And I will cast you
out of my sight, as I have cast out all your brethren, even the whole seed of Ephraim”
(Jeremiah 7:14, 15).
The precedent shows what the sentence on a house is. A house in which the LORD’s
name was set at the first can be abandoned for the wickedness of the people who
trust it. The sentence is not that houses of worship are wrong. It is that the house
cannot protect those who use it as a shelter. Jeremiah 26 gives the same sermon in a
narrative form: “Then will I make this house like Shiloh, and will make this city a curse
to all the nations of the earth” (Jeremiah 26:6). The priests and prophets and all the
people take Jeremiah and say, “Thou shalt surely die” (Jeremiah 26:8). Volume 1
reads the end of that chapter, where the elders recall Micah and the princes say that
Jeremiah is not worthy to die (Jeremiah 26:16).
5.5 Put your burnt offerings unto your sacrifices, and eat flesh
The next section is the one that has been read most often as a denial of the
sacrifices. It begins with a command that is plainly ironic. “Thus saith the LORD of
hosts, the God of Israel; Put your burnt offerings unto your sacrifices, and eat flesh”
(Jeremiah 7:21). A burnt offering is wholly burned and not eaten. A sacrifice, a peace
offering, is partly eaten by the offerer. The LORD says to put the burnt offerings with


                ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 19
the sacrifices and eat all the flesh. The command takes away the line between what is
the LORD’s and what is the offerer’s. He tells the people that if their offerings are no
more than meat, they may eat it. The sentence is the same as Isaiah’s: nothing the
LORD sees in them is a gift to him.
Then verses 22 and 23. “For I spake not unto your fathers, nor commanded them in
the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, concerning burnt offerings or
sacrifices: but this thing commanded I them, saying, Obey my voice, and I will be your
God, and ye shall be my people: and walk ye in all the ways that I have commanded
you, that it may be well unto you” (Jeremiah 7:22, 23).
Three observations. First, the sentence says what the LORD commanded the fathers:
“Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and ye shall be my people.” That is the
covenant formula, and it is found at the first meeting at Sinai: “Now therefore, if ye will
obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be a peculiar treasure unto
me above all people” (Exodus 19:5). The words “obey my voice” are the opening
condition of the covenant. The sacrifices, the tabernacle and the feasts come after
them. Jeremiah 7:22 says that in the day of the Exodus and in the first words the
LORD spoke to the fathers, the word was obedience. The offerings came as the ways
of the obedient people. Jeremiah is saying what the LORD said first.
Second, a flat reading is impossible. The prophet’s own book speaks of burnt
offerings, sacrifices and meat offerings in the city of the LORD’s promise. The first
passage is conditional on the city’s hearkening and on its keeping the sabbath
(Jeremiah 17:24), and it reads: “And they shall come from the cities of Judah …
bringing burnt offerings, and sacrifices, and meat offerings, and incense, and bringing
sacrifices of praise, unto the house of the LORD” (Jeremiah 17:26). In another chapter
the LORD promises that the voice of joy shall be heard again, “and of them that shall
bring the sacrifice of praise into the house of the LORD” (Jeremiah 33:11), and that
“the priests the Levites” shall not want a man “to offer burnt offerings, and to kindle
meat offerings, and to do sacrifice continually” (Jeremiah 33:18). The same prophet
cannot mean that the LORD never wanted burnt offerings and sacrifices. The volume
reports these verses as written and does not argue the covenants they sit beside,
which are read in the Covenantal and Prophetic Identity series.
Third, the idiom is the same as in Hosea and Psalm 40. A not-but sentence of rank
can say not this but that when it means this above all, and not this alone. The
comparison here is with the fathers at the Exodus: the first word to them was not a
code of sacrifice but a call to obey. The phrase “in the day that I brought them out”
covers the season of the Exodus. The Passover kept in that season was a sacrifice
(Exodus 12), and so the sentence, taken as a flat denial, would contradict the story it
appeals to. It is the rank of the first word that the prophet states.
A contrast in the same chapter fixes the usage. At Tophet in the valley of Hinnom the
people “burn their sons and their daughters in the fire; which I commanded them not,
neither came it into my heart” (Jeremiah 7:31). Here the LORD says he did not
command a thing, and means that it is foreign to him. If verse 22 said the same of
burnt offerings, it would put the sacrifices of Leviticus on a level with the burning of
children, and no one reads Jeremiah that way. The two sentences are different. In
verse 22 the LORD says the first word was obedience, and in verse 31 he says the
thing never came into his heart.

6. The Fast, the House and the Question from
Bethel
Claim: three late passages test the thesis against the observances that are not
sacrifices. Isaiah 58 condemns a fast kept by people who exact all their labours.
Isaiah 66:1–4 condemns the offerings of those who chose their own ways, and Isaiah
56:7 and 66:20 say the offerings of a gathered people shall be accepted on his altar



                  ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 21
and brought to his holy mountain. Zechariah 7 answers men who ask whether to go
on with a fast, and the answer is that the fast was not kept to the LORD, and chapter 8
turns the fasts into feasts.
6.1 Isaiah 58: a fast the LORD has not chosen
Isaiah 58 begins by telling the prophet to cry aloud and “shew my people their
transgression” (Isaiah 58:1). What follows is a description of religious people. “Yet
they seek me daily, and delight to know my ways, as a nation that did righteousness,
and forsook not the ordinance of their God: they ask of me the ordinances of justice;
they take delight in approaching to God” (Isaiah 58:2). The description is not ironical
about the seeking. They seek daily and delight in approaching God. The LORD says
they are a nation that appears to do righteousness. The people complain: “Wherefore
have we fasted, say they, and thou seest not? wherefore have we afflicted our soul,
and thou takest no knowledge?” (Isaiah 58:3).
The LORD’s answer takes up the fast. “Behold, in the day of your fast ye find pleasure,
and exact all your labours. Behold, ye fast for strife and debate, and to smite with the
fist of wickedness” (Isaiah 58:3, 4). The fast is a day on which they afflict themselves
and press their workers. The LORD asks, “Is it such a fast that I have chosen? a day
for a man to afflict his soul? is it to bow down his head as a bulrush, and to spread
sackcloth and ashes under him?” (Isaiah 58:5). The sentence uses the forms of the
fast, the bowed head, the sackcloth and the ashes, and asks whether this is what he
chose. The answer is no, and the next verse says what he chose. “Is not this the fast
that I have chosen? to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, and
to let the oppressed go free, and that ye break every yoke? Is it not to deal thy bread to
the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house?” (Isaiah 58:6,
7).
The pattern is the same as in the first chapter of the book. The observance is real and
the people are zealous. The LORD asks for justice to the oppressed as the thing the
observance was meant to carry. The promise follows: “Then shall thy light break forth
as the morning, and thine health shall spring forth speedily” (Isaiah 58:8), and “Then
shalt thou call, and the LORD shall answer; thou shalt cry, and he shall say, Here I am”
(Isaiah 58:9). The people who complained that the LORD did not see are told that he
will answer when they do justice. The chapter then turns to the sabbath in verses 13
and 14. Study 78 reads those verses with the other passages on the Sabbath, and this
volume leaves them there.
6.2 Isaiah 66:1–4: the house and the man who trembles
The last chapter of Isaiah asks a question about the house. “Thus saith the LORD, The
heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool: where is the house that ye build
unto me? and where is the place of my rest? For all those things hath mine hand
made, and all those things have been, saith the LORD: but to this man will I look, even
to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit, and trembleth at my word” (Isaiah 66:1, 2).
The LORD does not need a house, because heaven is his throne and earth his
footstool. He looks to a man, and the man is poor, contrite and one who trembles at
his word. Stephen quotes verses 1 and 2 in Acts 7:49, 50, and that use is read in Acts
Commentary Volume 1.
Verse 3 is the hardest sentence in the book on offerings. “He that killeth an ox is as if
he slew a man; he that sacrificeth a lamb, as if he cut off a dog’s neck; he that offereth



                ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 22
an oblation, as if he offered swine’s blood; he that burneth incense, as if he blessed an
idol. Yea, they have chosen their own ways, and their soul delighteth in their
abominations” (Isaiah 66:3). The sentence has four pairs, an ox and a man, a lamb
and a dog’s neck, an oblation and swine’s blood, incense and an idol. In each pair the
offering is set beside an abomination. The next clause says who the offerers are.
“Yea, they have chosen their own ways.” Verse 4 completes it: “because when I called,
none did answer; when I spake, they did not hear: but they did evil before mine eyes,
and chose that in which I delighted not” (Isaiah 66:4).
The volume reads the verse as a statement about the man who offers while he has
chosen his own ways. The four pairs do not say the offering is itself a killing; they say
the offering of such a man is as the abomination set beside it. The same book, ten
chapters earlier, says of the sons of the stranger who join themselves to the LORD
that “their burnt offerings and their sacrifices shall be accepted upon mine altar; for
mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people” (Isaiah 56:7). The altar is
named as “mine altar,” and the offerings are accepted. The two verses cannot be read
as the same man at the same altar. The man of 56:7 has joined himself to the LORD
and keeps his covenant (Isaiah 56:6). The man of 66:3 has chosen his own ways.
The close of the chapter confirms the point. The LORD says of a day to come that
“they shall bring all your brethren for an offering unto the LORD out of all nations … to
my holy mountain Jerusalem, saith the LORD, as the children of Israel bring an
offering in a clean vessel into the house of the LORD. And I will also take of them for
priests and for Levites, saith the LORD” (Isaiah 66:20, 21). The offering in a clean
vessel, the house of the LORD, priests and Levites are named in the same chapter, as
part of what the LORD will do. Verse 23 speaks of new moon and sabbath, and it is
left to Study 78. The volume reports verses 20 and 21 as written. It does not say when
they are fulfilled, and it leaves the temple of Ezekiel 40–48 to Typology Commentary
Volume 1.
6.3 Zechariah 7: did ye at all fast unto me?
Zechariah treats the question after the exile. The setting is given exactly: “in the fourth
year of king Darius, … the fourth day of the ninth month” (Zechariah 7:1). Men have
been sent “unto the house of God” with a question for the priests and the prophets:
“Should I weep in the fifth month, separating myself, as I have done these so many
years?” (Zechariah 7:2, 3). They ask whether to continue a fast for the destruction of
the house. The question is an honest one, and the answer is not a yes or a no.
The word comes back as a question to all the people and to the priests. “When ye
fasted and mourned in the fifth and seventh month, even those seventy years, did ye
at all fast unto me, even to me? And when ye did eat, and when ye did drink, did not ye
eat for yourselves, and drink for yourselves?” (Zechariah 7:5, 6). The question puts the
fast and the meal on the same footing: both were for themselves. The LORD does not
say that fasting is wrong. He says the fast was not unto him. Then he points back to
the former prophets: “Should ye not hear the words which the LORD hath cried by the
former prophets, when Jerusalem was inhabited and in prosperity?” (Zechariah 7:7).
The words of the former prophets are quoted. “Execute true judgment, and shew
mercy and compassions every man to his brother: and oppress not the widow, nor the
fatherless, the stranger, nor the poor; and let none of you imagine evil against his
brother in your heart” (Zechariah 7:9, 10). The charge is that they refused to hearken:



                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 23
“they made their hearts as an adamant stone, lest they should hear the law, and the
words which the LORD of hosts hath sent in his spirit by the former prophets:
therefore came a great wrath from the LORD of hosts” (Zechariah 7:12). The prophet
reads the whole line of the former prophets as one message, and the message is
justice, mercy and truth to the brother, joined to the law. The sentence on the people
was exile.
Zechariah 8 gives the outcome. “The fast of the fourth month, and the fast of the fifth,
and the fast of the seventh, and the fast of the tenth, shall be to the house of Judah
joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts; therefore love the truth and peace” (Zechariah
8:19). The fasts are not abolished; they are turned into feasts. The transformation is
the LORD’s doing, and the condition is the same as in every prophet: love the truth and
peace. The prophet who answers the question about the fast closes by promising that
the fasts will turn into gladness.

7. Malachi: The Priests and the Pleasant Offering
Claim: Malachi is the last of the prophets, and he is the one who complains most that
the offerings are poor. His charge against the priests is that they bring the blind, the
lame and the sick to an altar the LORD calls his own. He asks for a better offering, not
for none. He ends with a promise that the sons of Levi will be purified so that they
may offer an offering in righteousness, and that the offering of Judah and Jerusalem
shall be pleasant to the LORD as in the days of old. If any prophet were going to say
that the order of sacrifice had come to an end, it would be here, and it is the prophet
who says the opposite.
7.1 O priests, that despise my name
The charge opens with a question about honour. “A son honoureth his father, and a
servant his master: if then I be a father, where is mine honour? and if I be a master,
where is my fear? saith the LORD of hosts unto you, O priests, that despise my name”
(Malachi 1:6). The audience is the priests, and the charge is contempt for the name.
The priests reply, “Wherein have we despised thy name?” The word of the LORD is
that they offer “polluted bread upon mine altar” (Malachi 1:7). The altar is “mine altar.”
The table is “the table of the LORD,” which the priests call contemptible. The house,
the altar and the table are the LORD’s, and the complaint is about what the priests put
on them.
7.2 The blind, the lame and the sick
The offence is stated in detail. “And if ye offer the blind for sacrifice, is it not evil? and
if ye offer the lame and sick, is it not evil? offer it now unto thy governor; will he be
pleased with thee, or accept thy person? saith the LORD of hosts” (Malachi 1:8). The
comparison is with a governor. A man would not bring a blind animal to the governor
of the province and expect to be received. The priests bring to the altar what they
would not bring to a man. The LORD is a great King (Malachi 1:14). The weight of the
charge is that the offering is treated as a thing of no account, to be filled with
whatever is to hand.
Verse 13 supplies the attitude. “Ye said also, Behold, what a weariness is it! and ye
have snuffed at it, saith the LORD of hosts; and ye brought that which was torn, and
the lame, and the sick; thus ye brought an offering: should I accept this of your hand?
saith the LORD” (Malachi 1:13). The priests find the service a weariness and sniff at it.
They bring what is torn, lame and sick. The LORD asks whether he should accept it.
Verse 14 turns to the worshiper: “But cursed be the deceiver, which hath in his flock a
male, and voweth, and sacrificeth unto the LORD a corrupt thing: for I am a great King,
saith the LORD of hosts, and my name is dreadful among the heathen” (Malachi 1:14).


                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 25
A man who has a male in his flock, vows it, and then offers a corrupt thing instead is a
deceiver. The vow is the language of Psalm 50:14, and the curse falls on the man who
does not pay it.
7.3 Who would shut the doors?
The strongest sentence is in verse 10. “Who is there even among you that would shut
the doors for nought? neither do ye kindle fire on mine altar for nought. I have no
pleasure in you, saith the LORD of hosts, neither will I accept an offering at your hand”
(Malachi 1:10). The LORD wishes that someone among the priests would shut the
doors of the house. He says they do not kindle fire on his altar for nothing: they take a
living by it. The sentence does not say the doors should be shut for ever. It says that
offerings of this kind, from these priests, would be better not offered, and it is an
appeal to the priests to stop.
The next verse is the most difficult in the book for any reader. “For from the rising of
the sun even unto the going down of the same my name shall be great among the
Gentiles; and in every place incense shall be offered unto my name, and a pure
offering: for my name shall be great among the heathen, saith the LORD of hosts”
(Malachi 1:11). The volume reports the verse as written and draws three small
conclusions. The sentence is a statement of what shall be, as the King James Version
puts it, in the future. It contrasts a pure offering with the polluted bread of verse 7.
And it speaks of the LORD’s name among the Gentiles in language like that of Isaiah
66:19–21. The volume does not say when this is, and it does not read the verse as a
description of the Body of Christ. It stands in Israel’s prophetic line, with the hope of a
purified offering, and says what the LORD says: his name will be great among the
nations, and a pure offering will be given.
7.4 The covenant of Levi
Chapter 2 repeats the charge to the priests and sets it in the covenant. “And now, O ye
priests, this commandment is for you. If ye will not hear, and if ye will not lay it to
heart, to give glory unto my name, saith the LORD of hosts, I will even send a curse
upon you, and I will curse your blessings” (Malachi 2:1, 2). The sentence is severe: “I
will corrupt your seed, and spread dung upon your faces, even the dung of your
solemn feasts” (Malachi 2:3). The solemn feasts are named as the priests’ own, and
the sentence turns them to shame.
The covenant explains why the offence is so great. “My covenant was with him of life
and peace; and I gave them to him for the fear wherewith he feared me, and was
afraid before my name. The law of truth was in his mouth, and iniquity was not found
in his lips: he walked with me in peace and equity, and did turn many away from
iniquity” (Malachi 2:5, 6). The priest at his best is a teacher: “the priest’s lips should
keep knowledge, and they should seek the law at his mouth: for he is the messenger
of the LORD of hosts” (Malachi 2:7). The priests of Malachi’s day have departed out of
the way, caused many to stumble at the law, and corrupted the covenant of Levi
(Malachi 2:8). The charge is not that Levi’s office is false. It is that the sons of Levi
have fallen from it, and the covenant is the standard by which they are measured.
7.5 The refiner, and an offering in righteousness
The third chapter begins with the coming of a messenger and the LORD to his temple;
Volume 4 reads those two messengers and the verses around them. The verses that
follow belong to this volume. The coming is a day of testing: “But who may abide the


                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 26
day of his coming? and who shall stand when he appeareth? for he is like a refiner’s
fire, and like fullers’ soap” (Malachi 3:2). Then the purpose is stated. “And he shall sit
as a refiner and purifier of silver: and he shall purify the sons of Levi, and purge them
as gold and silver, that they may offer unto the LORD an offering in righteousness.
Then shall the offering of Judah and Jerusalem be pleasant unto the LORD, as in the
days of old, and as in former years” (Malachi 3:3, 4).
Three things are plain in the two verses. The object of the refining is the sons of Levi,
the priests whose offerings were polluted. The purpose of the refining is “that they
may offer unto the LORD an offering in righteousness.” And the result is that the
offering of Judah and Jerusalem shall be “pleasant unto the LORD, as in the days of
old, and as in former years.” The prophet who charged the priests with offering the
blind and the lame ends by promising the offering that pleases. He does not promise
an end to the order. He promises its purification. The verse that follows lists the sins
that bring him near to judgment: sorcerers, adulterers, false swearers, those who
oppress the hireling, the widow, the fatherless and the stranger (Malachi 3:5). The list
is conduct. The sins of verse 5 are the sins of the earlier prophets.
Malachi 3:6 gives the ground on which the sons of Jacob are not consumed: “For I am
the LORD, I change not; therefore ye sons of Jacob are not consumed.” Volume 1 read
that verse among the sworn words. It is cited here only to mark that Malachi’s hope
for the offering and his word about the LORD’s constancy stand together in the same
chapter.
The book ends with an instruction. “Remember ye the law of Moses my servant, which
I commanded unto him in Horeb for all Israel, with the statutes and judgments”
(Malachi 4:4). The last charge of the prophets is to remember the law given at Horeb,
with its statutes and judgments. Volume 4 reads the verse that follows. In this volume
it is enough to note what the final prophet asks: not that Israel leave the Law behind,
but that it remember it.

8. Hebrews 10: The Same Verdict from the Other
Side
Claim: the letter to the Hebrews takes up the sentence of Psalm 40 and says, in its
own argument, what the prophets had been saying about the offerings: they were
never the means by which sins are taken away. It does not say the prophets abolished
them. It says that the offerings of the Law were a shadow and could not take away
sins, that the psalm sets the will done against them, and that Christ’s offering of his
body once for all does what they could not. The letter is read inside Israel’s line, on its
own audience markers and the covenant it quotes, and builds nothing for the Body
from it.
8.1 Where the letter stands
This reading rests on the letter’s own audience markers and on the covenant it quotes
(Jeremiah 31:31–34), which belongs to the house of Israel and the house of Judah,
the Body benefiting from Christ’s blood without being the covenant party. The letter
speaks of the priests who “offer gifts according to the law” (Hebrews 8:4), of Moses
and the tabernacle (Hebrews 8:5), and of a new covenant made with Israel (Hebrews
8:8–12 quotes it). It is addressed to men who know the sacrifices and have a high
priest in view. Study 24 reads the letter’s warning and its sacrificial argument, and
Typology Commentary Volumes 1 and 3 read the shadow and the priesthood. This
chapter asks one narrower question: how does the letter take up the psalm’s
sentence on the offerings, and what does it make of it?




                  ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 28
8.2 The Law’s sacrifices: a shadow that could not take away sins
The tenth chapter begins with a verdict on the Law’s offerings. “For the law having a
shadow of good things to come, and not the very image of the things, can never with
those sacrifices which they offered year by year continually make the comers
thereunto perfect. For then would they not have ceased to be offered? because that
the worshippers once purged should have had no more conscience of sins. But in
those sacrifices there is a remembrance again made of sins every year. For it is not
possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins” (Hebrews 10:1–
4).
The sentence is flat, and it does not say the sacrifices were wrong. It says what they
could not do. The repetition of the sacrifices, year by year, is the proof: a thing that
took sin away would not need to be repeated. The writer’s argument is that the Law’s
sacrifices were a shadow and a yearly remembrance of sins. This is a statement
about their power, and it is made inside the covenant the sacrifices belonged to. The
same writer says elsewhere that the Law’s sacrifices were a figure “imposed on them
until the time of reformation” (Hebrews 9:10), and that the high priests of Israel “serve
unto the example and shadow of heavenly things” (Hebrews 8:5).
The prophets had said much the same from the other side. When Isaiah asks, “To
what purpose is the multitude of your sacrifices unto me?” (Isaiah 1:11), and Psalm
51:16 says, “thou delightest not in burnt offering,” the prophets are saying that the
offerings do not do the thing the worshipers hope they do. Hebrews 10:4 says it in a
sentence: the blood of bulls and goats cannot take away sins. The two statements
agree, and they are made for different reasons. The prophets say it to people who are
using the offering to cover their sins. The letter says it to people who are asking what
the offerings were for.
8.3 The quotation of Psalm 40
The letter then quotes the psalm. “Wherefore when he cometh into the world, he saith,
Sacrifice and offering thou wouldest not, but a body hast thou prepared me: in burnt
offerings and sacrifices for sin thou hast had no pleasure. Then said I, Lo, I come (in
the volume of the book it is written of me,) to do thy will, O God” (Hebrews 10:5–7).
Psalm 40:6–8, as chapter 1 read it, says: “Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire;
mine ears hast thou opened: burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required.
Then said I, Lo, I come: in the volume of the book it is written of me, I delight to do thy
will, O my God.”
Two things differ in the English. Where the psalm has “mine ears hast thou opened,”
the letter has “a body hast thou prepared me.” And the psalm’s “burnt offering and sin
offering hast thou not required” stands in the letter as “in burnt offerings and
sacrifices for sin thou hast had no pleasure.” The volume reports both wordings as
they stand in the King James Version and builds no theory on the difference. The
speaker is the same in both: the one who says, Lo, I come, and says that it is written
of him in the volume of the book. The letter introduces the words with “when he
cometh into the world, he saith,” and so reads the psalm as the speech of the one
who comes. The volume reports that reading.
What the quotation shares with the psalm is the sentence of rank. The psalm sets the
offerings against the will done, and so does the letter. In the letter’s next verses the
four named offerings are those of the psalm: “Above when he said, Sacrifice and



                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 29
offering and burnt offerings and offering for sin thou wouldest not, neither hadst
pleasure therein; which are offered by the law” (Hebrews 10:8). The phrase “which are
offered by the law” is the letter’s gloss on the four. It tells the reader that the offerings
the psalm names are the Law’s offerings, and that the “not” of the psalm applies to
them.
8.4 The first and the second
Verse 9 is the verse that causes the difficulty. “Then said he, Lo, I come to do thy will,
O God. He taketh away the first, that he may establish the second.” The first is the set
of offerings that the psalm said the LORD did not desire. The second is the will done.
The writer reads the psalm as announcing that what the LORD wanted was never the
offerings in themselves and that the one who comes does the will. Verse 10 then says
what the will accomplished: “By the which will we are sanctified through the offering
of the body of Jesus Christ once for all.”
The volume reads the verse as follows. The letter says that Christ’s offering does
what the offerings of the Law could not do: it takes sins away. In that sense he takes
away the first, as the means by which sin is dealt with, and establishes the second,
the will done and the body offered once for all. The verse does not say that the
prophets set the offerings aside. It says that what the psalm already said, that the
offerings were not the thing the LORD desired and required, comes to its point in the
one who says, Lo, I come. The prophets’ sentences are not abolished by the letter, and
they are not made false. They are brought to the one they pointed toward.
The verses that follow say the same thing in the letter’s own terms. “And every priest
standeth daily ministering and offering oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can
never take away sins: but this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever,
sat down on the right hand of God” (Hebrews 10:11, 12). “For by one offering he hath
perfected for ever them that are sanctified” (Hebrews 10:14). The letter calls the Holy
Ghost a witness to this, and it quotes the covenant of Jeremiah 31: “This is the
covenant that I will make with them after those days, saith the Lord, I will put my laws
into their hearts, and in their minds will I write them; and their sins and iniquities will I
remember no more” (Hebrews 10:15–17). Verse 18 ends the argument: “Now where
remission of these is, there is no more offering for sin.” Study 24 reads that finality in
detail, and the volume cites it.
8.5 What the letter does not say
It does not say that the prophets condemned the Law’s sacrifices as sinful. The
prophets’ sentences were about how the worshipers used them, and the letter’s are
about what they could do. It does not say that burnt offerings will never be offered
again. Hebrews 10:18 speaks of offering for sin, and the sentence is about remission
of sins. Typology Commentary Volume 1 reads Ezekiel 40–48 as a Kingdom temple
with memorial offerings, and nothing in this volume argues against that reading:
offerings that never took away sins can be offered as a remembrance without taking
them away. The volume does not argue the point, and cites the Typology volume.
Nor does the letter say anything of the Body of Christ in these verses. It speaks of
those whom the covenant of Jeremiah 31 concerns, and it holds the Body at a
distance in the standing sentence given above. The blood of Christ is the ground, and
the Body remembers it. The Body is not the party to the covenant the letter quotes.
The prophets’ charges are in this volume read as charges brought against Israel’s

worship; the letter’s verdict is read as a verdict inside Israel’s line, and nothing in
either is made a charge against, or a rule for, the worship of the Body.

8.6 What the volume takes from the passages
 • Hebrews 10:1–4 says what the Law’s sacrifices could not do: they were a
   shadow, repeated year by year, and could not take away sins.
 • The letter quotes Psalm 40 and says the offerings named are those “offered by
   the law”; the volume reports the two wordings of the clause on the body and the
   ears without a theory.
 • Hebrews 10:9 is read as the letter’s argument that the will done takes the place of
   the offerings as the means of dealing with sin; it does not say the prophets
   abolished the offerings.
 • The letter and the prophets agree that the offerings were never the thing that took
   sins away, and they say it to different people for different reasons.
 • The letter is read inside Israel’s line; nothing in it is made a charge against, or a
   rule for, the Body, and Ezekiel 40–48 is left to Typology Commentary Volume 1.


9. Objections and Replies
Claim: the volume’s reading, that the prophets condemn the worshipers and the way
they worship and never the worship the LORD commanded, answers the objections
best made against it, and where an objector is right the volume says so. Modern
writers who support parts of the argument are named beside those who press against
it. A writer is quoted only where the author has read the words in a free text. Where a
position is known to the author only because another writer or an abstract reports it,
the volume says so, names the report, and puts no quotation marks around the
position.




                   ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 31
9.1 Who agrees, and where the volume differs
Four modern writers hold, in their own ways, that the prophets did not reject sacrifice
as such. Göran Eidevall, who teaches Old Testament at Uppsala, writes in an article of
2018 that his study “refutes the view that prophets like Amos and Jeremiah rejected
all sacrifices” (Religions 9, no. 12, article titled Reciprocity and the Risk of Rejection).
He reads their cult-critical oracles as bound to particular situations. In a short piece of
2017 for Yale University Press he calls the opposite picture of the prophets
“anachronistic” Blessing Boloje, writing on Micah 6:6–8 in Old Testament Essays in
2019, concludes that “Micah’s message is not a rejection of ritual but maintaining the
right priorities.” Alphonso Groenewald, in HTS Teologiese Studies in 2019, reads Amos
5:21–24 and Isaiah 1:10–20 in the same direction, and quotes another scholar’s
sentence that “The problem indeed is the lack of social ethics of the community of
the faithful and not with the rituals themselves.” Matt Lynch, in a blog post of 2017 on
the WTC Theology blog, writes that “It’s important to remember that the prophets
were rhetoricians” and he notes that Jeremiah himself says sacrifices will be restored
(Jeremiah 33:18).
The volume agrees with them on the conclusion and differs on the method. Eidevall
and Lynch read the strong sentences as situational or rhetorical: said for an occasion,
or to provoke. The volume reads them, wherever the wording allows, as the Hebrew
idiom of rank, this more than that, and finds in each the same two things: the
offerings are named as the LORD’s own appointment, and the charge falls on the
worshiper. That is a narrower claim than rhetoric, and a reader who prefers the
rhetorical reading loses nothing in the conclusion.
9.2 Objection: the prophets rejected sacrifice itself
This is the strongest opposing position, and it has a long history in the study of the
prophets. John Barton, in The Prophets and the Cult (in Temple and Worship in Biblical
Israel, edited by John Day, 2007), holds, according to an abstract of the chapter
published by the Temple Studies Group, that a handful of passages, among them
Micah 6:6, Amos 5:25, Jeremiah 2:1–3, Jeremiah 7:22 and Psalm 50, are hostile to
sacrificial activity in itself and not only to sacrifice offered by the unrepentant. The
volume knows Barton’s chapter only through that abstract and describes it in its own
words. Eidevall reports that Barton also describes Amos as an anti-ritualist (in The
Theology of the Book of Amos, 2012), and he reports that Paul Volz, in 1937, argued
from five observations that Amos and Jeremiah wanted the cult abolished.
Groenewald reports that William McKane (1982) and Ronald Hendel (1995) read the
prophets as setting the ethical against the ritual. The volume has read none of these
works; it describes the positions as these two writers report them.
Reply. Barton’s five passages are each read in the chapters before this one, and the
reply is the reading. Micah 6:6–8 is a series of questions put by the worshiper,
answered by “what doth the LORD require of thee” (Micah 6:8), with no sentence
abolishing the offerings (chapter 4). Amos 5:25 is a question that is itself contested
(chapter 3). Jeremiah 7:22 is read against Exodus 19:5, Jeremiah 7:23 and Jeremiah
33:18 (chapter 5). Jeremiah 2:1–3 praises Israel’s love in the wilderness and calls
Israel “holiness unto the LORD, and the firstfruits of his increase”; it does not mention
an offering and so cannot be hostile to one. Psalm 50 says, “I will not reprove thee for
thy sacrifices or thy burnt offerings, to have been continually before me” (Psalm 50:8),
and then says what he does not need and what he asks (chapter 1). The volume



                 ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 32
grants that the sentences read alone can sound like abolition. It holds that they do not
read so in their contexts.
Volz’s five observations, as Eidevall reports them, can each be met from the text. That
the critique reached the central sanctuaries is true of Amos’s Bethel, which was the
king’s own chapel (Amos 7:13), and Eidevall notes that Amos never charges the
Jerusalem cult; Jeremiah’s charge falls on those who trust the house, not on the
house’s offerings (chapter 5). That the prophets proposed no reform fits a message
that said the day of judgment was fixed; but Jeremiah 7:3 sets the condition, “Amend
your ways and your doings, and I will cause you to dwell in this place,” and Isaiah
1:16–20 offers the same. That they idealized the past is what Jeremiah 7:22, 23 and
Amos 5:25 are said to show, and chapters 3 and 5 read both. That priests persecuted
them is true, but the charge against Amos was conspiracy and the foretelling of the
king’s death and the people’s exile (Amos 7:10, 11), not the abolition of offerings. That
some oracles imply total rejection is the very point at issue, and the chapters read
each of them.
9.3 Objection: ritual has little value; ethics is the whole matter
A second objection grants that the prophets did not abolish the cult and holds that
they saw little worth in it. Boloje and Groenewald, in Scriptura in 2015, propose that
“these prophets could not see the importance of rituals for the improvement of ethical
life,” and say this criticism “lies at the heart of the controversy between the prophets
and the priest.” They add that the cult was acceptable where it served ethical living.
Groenewald, in 2019, reports that Hendel holds the classical prophets contrasted the
ethical with the ritual. The objection is that the prophets valued the offerings so little
that the volume’s emphasis on what they left standing makes too much of it.
Reply. The volume grants that the prophets demanded conduct and that offerings
without it are refused: Boloje himself says rituals without ethical behaviour are
worthless, and the volume has said the same of Isaiah 1, Amos 5 and Jeremiah 7. But
the prophets do not say the offerings are of little value. They call them “mine
offerings” (Hosea 8:13), they say the LORD will “require” them in the land (Ezekiel
20:40), and Malachi charges the priests for offering what a governor would refuse,
which treats the offering as a matter of weight (Malachi 1:8). A man who thinks an
offering of little value does not object to a blind lamb. The charge is that the offering
was brought by the unrepentant and brought in a state that dishonored the one it was
brought to. That makes them of great value, not little.
9.4 Objection: Ezekiel 20:25–26 says the LORD gave bad laws
The hardest text comes from a prophet. “Wherefore I gave them also statutes that
were not good, and judgments whereby they should not live; and I polluted them in
their own gifts, in that they caused to pass through the fire all that openeth the womb,
that I might make them desolate, to the end that they might know that I am the LORD”
(Ezekiel 20:25, 26). Hyam Maccoby, in an article in the Journal of Textual Reasoning in
1999, reports that modern Jewish commentators led by Moshe Greenberg have taken
the verses to say that Ezekiel accuses God of giving bad laws, among them a law of
firstborn sacrifice. Maccoby rejects that. “The notion of a God who deliberately gives
bad laws is surely nonsensical,” he writes, and he reads the verses as Ezekiel’s report
of what the rebels said: “Ezekiel represents the rebels as attributing the new teaching
to Yahweh himself.”



                ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 2 • 33
Reply. The volume and Maccoby differ on how the verses work and agree on what
they cannot do. Maccoby takes the statutes that were not good as the rebels’ claim;
the volume reads them as a judicial giving-over, in the order of the chapter. The
statutes by which a man lives are the ones given first, and Israel “walked not in my
statutes” (Ezekiel 20:11, 13, 21). Then comes “Wherefore I gave them also statutes
that were not good” (Ezekiel 20:25), a consequence of the refusal. The next verse
names the practice, the burning of the firstborn, and Jeremiah 7:31 says of the
burning of children that the LORD “commanded them not, neither came it into my
heart.” Whichever reading is taken, the verses are not a repudiation of the offerings,
because the same chapter promises that in his holy mountain “there will I require your
offerings, and the firstfruits of your oblations, with all your holy things” (Ezekiel
20:40). The volume does not rest its case on choosing between Maccoby’s reading
and its own.
9.5 Objection: Hebrews 10 shows the prophets were right to reject sacrifice
The objection takes Hebrews 10:8, 9 as a ruling that the offerings were taken away,
and concludes that the prophets were announcing their end. No writer is named for it;
it is stated in the author’s words. Reply. Chapter 8 read the passage. The letter says
the Law’s sacrifices could not take away sins and that Christ’s offering does. It does
not say the prophets abolished the offerings, and it does not say their charges were
charges against the offerings. What it shares with the prophets is a verdict about their
power, and what it adds is a person.
9.6 Objection: are the churches’ rites condemned too?
The objection asks whether those who keep a rite today stand under the prophets’
charge. No writer is named for it. Reply. The volume does not say so. The prophets’
charges are brought against Israel’s worship, in Israel’s land, at the house the LORD
appointed. Nothing in the chapters was a rule for the Body, and nothing was a charge
against it. The volume holds that the Body is in no Israelite covenant and states no
critique of Christian ritual. A reader who wants to ask what the prophets’ pattern
shows about any worship is free to ask it, and the volume has not answered.

9.7 What the volume takes from the replies
 • Eidevall, Boloje, Groenewald and Lynch agree that the prophets did not reject
   sacrifice as such; the volume differs from the first and last in reading the
   sentences as rank rather than situation or rhetoric.
 • The strongest opposing voice, Barton, is answered passage by passage; the
   volume knows his chapter only through an abstract and says so.
 • The volume grants that the prophets demanded conduct and refused offerings
   without it, and holds that they never treated the offerings as of little worth.
 • Ezekiel 20:25, 26 is read in the chapter’s order, with its closing promise of the
   offerings; the volume differs from Maccoby on how the verses work and agrees
   with him that they do not repudiate the offerings.
 • The volume states no critique of Christian ritual and assigns the prophets’
   charges to the worship they were brought against.


Conclusion
The volume began with a plain statement: it argues a position. The position is that the
prophets condemn the worshipers and the way they worship, and never the worship
the LORD commanded. It has tried to show that from the prophets’ own sentences.
Saul is told that to obey is better than sacrifice (1 Samuel 15:22), and the same
chapter shows him offering what the LORD had said to destroy. The psalms say the
LORD did not desire the offerings and still look to the day when bullocks are offered
on his altar (Psalm 51:19). Isaiah names the sacrifices, the new moons and the
appointed feasts as the Law’s own and refuses them from hands full of blood, and his
remedy is nine commands of conduct (Isaiah 1:10–17). Amos charges Bethel and
Gilgal, which Jeroboam devised, and the feasts that “your” hands keep. Hosea and
Micah set mercy and the knowledge of God above the offering. Jeremiah stands at
the gate of the house and tells the people that the house will go as Shiloh went if they
do not amend their ways. Isaiah, Zechariah and Malachi bring the fast, the house and
the priests under the same charge. And each of them, in his own hope, names the
offerings among the things restored.

The volume asks to be tested. A reader who disagrees may take any row of the table

The volume has tried to be fair to the readings it opposes. It has granted that the
prophets’ sentences are strong, that Ezekiel 20:25, 26 is hard, and that Amos 5:25 can
be read more than one way. It has named Barton as the strongest voice against it and
answered his passages one by one, and it has said where it knows a writer’s position
only through a report. Eidevall, Boloje, Groenewald and Lynch agree with its
conclusion, and the volume has said where it differs from them in method. If the
volume has pressed past an objection unfairly, the verses will show it. The volume
has stated no critique of Christian ritual and has made no rule for the Body from the
charges it reads.
