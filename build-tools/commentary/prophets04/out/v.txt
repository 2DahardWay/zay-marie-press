ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 1

Preface
This volume argues a position. It does not gather what churches say about Elijah and
leave the reader to choose. It says what the author believes the Scriptures teach
about the prophet who was promised before the day of the LORD, it gives the
evidence from the verses in the order the verses stand, and it answers the strongest
objections in the objectors’ own words, so that a reader who disagrees knows exactly
where.
The question is narrow, and the prophet who closes the Old Testament raises it. The
last words of Malachi are a promise and a warning: “Behold, I will send you Elijah the
prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD: and he shall
turn the heart of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children to their
fathers, lest I come and smite the earth with a curse” (Malachi 4:5, 6). The Old
Testament ends on that verse, and when the Gospels begin, the promise is on
everyone’s lips. Priests and Levites ask John, “Art thou Elias?” (John 1:21). The
disciples ask the Lord, “Why then say the scribes that Elias must first come?”
(Matthew 17:10). The crowd at Caesarea Philippi says that some think Jesus himself
is Elias (Matthew 16:14). At the cross, someone hears the cry and says, “Let be, let us
see whether Elias will come to save him” (Matthew 27:49).
Readers have answered the question in three ways. Some say the promise was kept
in John the Baptist and is finished. Some say it was not kept at all, and that John had
nothing to do with it. Some say it was kept in part and still waits. Each reading can
quote a verse. The first quotes “Elias is come already” (Matthew 17:12), the second
quotes John’s “I am not” (John 1:21), and the third quotes “Elias truly shall first come,
and restore all things” (Matthew 17:11). This volume holds that all three verses stand,
that they were spoken by different persons to different hearers for different ends, and
that none of them is allowed to cancel the others.
The position is this. Malachi promises one Elijah the prophet, sent by the LORD,
before the great and dreadful day. The Gospels answer in three voices. Gabriel tells
Zacharias that his son will go before the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elias” (Luke
1:17). John, asked to his face whether he is Elias, says, “I am not” (John 1:21). The
Lord says of John, “And if ye will receive it, this is Elias, which was for to come”
(Matthew 11:14), and then, after John is dead, says both that “Elias truly shall first
come, and restore all things” and that “Elias is come already, and they knew him not”
(Matthew 17:11, 12). The volume reads the three voices together as an offer made to
Israel in John’s day and conditional on its reception. The offer was refused, the
restoring of all things is not reported done, the day of the LORD has not come, and the
text therefore points to a further coming of Elijah before that day.
Three things about the method. First, the volume reads the King James Version only,
and every Scripture quotation is checked word for word against it. Second, each
chapter opens with a claim, reads its passages in order, and closes with a short table
and a list of what the volume takes from the passages, so that the reader can see
what has been argued and test it. Third, where modern writers are named, they are
named for a position the author has read in a free text, and where an objection is
stated, it is stated as the objector would state it and then answered from the verses.

One boundary runs through the whole volume. Every passage here is addressed to
Israel. Malachi speaks to Israel by the hand of a prophet (Malachi 1:1), Gabriel to a
priest of Israel in the temple, John to the people at the Jordan, and the Lord to his
disciples about Israel’s expectation. The Body of Christ is not named in any of these
passages and is not read into them. The volume draws nothing from them for the
Body’s calling, hope or conduct.

Elias shall first come and restore all things.

A reader may test the thesis in one move. Put the three statements of John’s relation
to Elijah side by side, and ask whether the text lets any one of them be dropped. If
Gabriel’s sentence is dropped, John’s “I am not” and the Lord’s “this is Elias” stand
against each other with nothing to join them. If John’s denial is dropped, the Lord’s
words are heard as simple identity and the question of the priests and Levites is left
without an answer. If the Lord’s second sentence on the mountain is dropped, he said
only that Elias had come, and Matthew 17:11 has nothing to say. If the first is
dropped, the Lord’s own “truly shall first come” has nothing to mean. The thesis
stands if the four stand together; the volume argues that they do.

1. Malachi’s Close: The Law, Horeb and the Curse
Claim: the promise of Elijah is the last paragraph of the Old Testament, and it cannot
be read apart from the paragraph around it. Malachi 4:4–6 joins three things: a
command to remember the law of Moses given at Horeb, a promise to send Elijah the
prophet, and a warning of a curse. The prophet who is promised is promised to Israel,
before a day, and for a work done on hearts. The volume reads the three verses in
order and asks what each says and what each leaves unsaid.
1.1 The book and its audience
The book opens, “The burden of the word of the LORD to Israel by Malachi” (Malachi
1:1). The audience is named in the first verse and is never changed. The book that
follows is a series of disputes between the LORD and Israel in which the people ask,
“Wherein hast thou loved us?” (Malachi 1:2), “Wherein have we despised thy name?”
(Malachi 1:6) and “Wherein have we wearied him?” (Malachi 2:17). The priests are
charged with offering polluted bread, the people with unfaithfulness to the wife of
their youth, and all with robbing God in tithes and offerings (Malachi 1:6–8; 2:14–16;
3:8). The book is the LORD’s indictment of his people and a promise to them at the
same time. The second volume of this series reads the charges against the priests
and the offerings; this chapter needs only the point that the audience of the last
paragraph is the audience of the first.
1.2 The day that burns
The last paragraph begins in the verses before it. Malachi 3:1–5 announced a
messenger, the sudden coming of the LORD to his temple, and a judgment on
sorcerers, adulterers and false swearers. Malachi 3:13–18 records the faithful
speaking to one another, a book of remembrance, and the promise, “And they shall be
mine, saith the LORD of hosts, in that day when I make up my jewels” (Malachi 3:17).
Then chapter 4 opens with the day itself: “For, behold, the day cometh, that shall burn
as an oven; and all the proud, yea, and all that do wickedly, shall be stubble: and the

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 4

day that cometh shall burn them up, saith the LORD of hosts, that it shall leave them
neither root nor branch” (Malachi 4:1).
Two outcomes are set side by side. The proud and the wicked are stubble. “But unto
you that fear my name shall the Sun of righteousness arise with healing in his wings;
and ye shall go forth, and grow up as calves of the stall” (Malachi 4:2). The speaker
turns from the day in the third person to a “you” who fear his name, and the “you” is
Israel in the book’s own address. “And ye shall tread down the wicked; for they shall
be ashes under the soles of your feet in the day that I shall do this, saith the LORD of
hosts” (Malachi 4:3). The day is a day of fire and a day of healing, and the same day
does both. It is the day the next three verses are about.
1.3 Remember the law of Moses
Between the day and the prophet stands a command: “Remember ye the law of
Moses my servant, which I commanded unto him in Horeb for all Israel, with the
statutes and judgments” (Malachi 4:4). The sentence is easily passed over, and it
should not be. It names the law as Moses’ and as the LORD’s, it names Horeb as the
place it was given, and it names “all Israel” as the people it was given to. Malachi is
the last of the prophets in the English Old Testament, and his last act is to point back
to the first foundation of Israel’s life with God. The book ends looking at Sinai and
looking forward to a day.
The order of the three verses matters. The command to remember the law is not a
charge against a particular sin. It is a call to the whole people to hold what was given.
It stands before the promise of the prophet, and nothing in the paragraph says why
the command stands there. The volume notes the placement and builds nothing on it,
except one thing: the promise that follows is a promise in Israel’s line, to a people
with a law, a land and a temple, and not a promise addressed to anyone outside that
line.
1.4 Elijah the prophet
“Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful
day of the LORD” (Malachi 4:5). Eight things can be read out of the verse without
adding anything. The speaker is the LORD: “I will send.” The sending is future. It is
addressed to a plural “you,” the same Israel of the book. The one sent is named,
“Elijah,” and given a title, “the prophet.” The title is the one the book of Kings gives him
when he comes near the altar on Carmel: “Elijah the prophet came near, and said,
LORD God of Abraham, Isaac, and of Israel” (1 Kings 18:36). The sending is set before
an event, “the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD.” The day is called
great and dreadful, and the verse does not say what happens in it. And the verse says
nothing of his baptizing, preaching in a wilderness or announcing a person. Those
come from other texts.
The volume draws one conclusion at this point and no more. The verse names a man.
A reader may say that the name is a title for an office or a type, and that is the position
of some writers who are answered in the last chapter. The verse as written says
“Elijah the prophet,” and the same name and title are used in the narrative of Kings of
a man who lived and spoke. Nothing in the verse itself marks the name as figurative.
The volume therefore reads the promise as a promise of Elijah, and tests that reading
against every passage that follows.

1.5 What the prophet does, and the curse
“And he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children
to their fathers, lest I come and smite the earth with a curse” (Malachi 4:6). The work
is a work on hearts. The “fathers” and the “children” are not further identified by
Malachi, and the volume does not guess. The purpose clause, “lest I come and smite
the earth with a curse,” gives the work its reason. If the hearts are not turned, the
LORD comes and smites. The last word of the Old Testament in the English Bible is
“curse,” and the word is the sign of the Mosaic covenant’s own sanctions:
Deuteronomy 28 sets blessings and curses before Israel (Deuteronomy 28:15). The
promise of Elijah is a promise of a work meant to avert the curse.
Two things follow, and the later chapters will test them. The first is that the work is
conditional in its end. The prophet turns hearts so that the LORD does not have to
smite, and the verse states no guarantee that all the hearts are turned. The second is
that the work comes before the day and is aimed at it. The day is not the prophet’s
coming; the prophet’s coming is a mercy before the day.

1.6 What the volume takes from the passage
• Malachi is addressed to Israel from its first verse to its last, and the promise of
Elijah is a promise to that people.
• The command to remember the law of Moses given at Horeb stands between the
day and the prophet, and the promise belongs to Israel’s line.
• Malachi 4:5 names a man, Elijah the prophet, and gives him the title the book of
Kings gives him; nothing in the verse marks the name as a figure.
• The prophet’s work is on hearts and has a purpose, that the LORD not come and
smite the earth with a curse.
• The promise is a promise of a mercy before the day, and the day is not the
prophet’s coming.

2. Elijah in Kings: The Man the Promise Names
Claim: if Malachi 4:5 names a man, the first thing to ask is who the man was and what
he did, and the book of Kings answers in five scenes. Each scene is read here as
written, and each is tied to the later text that takes it up, so that a reader can see

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 6

which of Elijah’s works the New Testament recalls and which it does not. The scenes
also contain two links to Malachi’s own paragraph that are easy to miss: Elijah’s
prayer on Carmel uses the very phrase of Malachi 4:6, and his one great journey ends
at the mountain named in Malachi 4:4.
2.1 The shut heaven
Elijah enters the record without introduction. “And Elijah the Tishbite, who was of the
inhabitants of Gilead, said unto Ahab, As the LORD God of Israel liveth, before whom I
stand, there shall not be dew nor rain these years, but according to my word” (1 Kings
17:1). He is named, he is given a place, he stands “before” the LORD, and he sets a
term on the heaven: no dew or rain “but according to my word.” The word is his only
because it is first the LORD’s, for the next verse says, “And the word of the LORD
came unto him, saying” (1 Kings 17:2), and the brook, the ravens and the widow of
Zarephath follow at the LORD’s command (1 Kings 17:3, 4, 9).
Three later texts take up the shut heaven, and each gives a number or a name. The
Lord says in the synagogue at Nazareth that there were many widows in Israel “in the
days of Elias, when the heaven was shut up three years and six months, when great
famine was throughout all the land” (Luke 4:25). James writes that Elias “prayed
earnestly that it might not rain: and it rained not on the earth by the space of three
years and six months” (James 5:17). The book of Kings itself says only that the word
came to Elijah “in the third year” (1 Kings 18:1). James adds that “Elias was a man
subject to like passions as we are” (James 5:17), which is the New Testament’s own
statement that the man was a man. Gospel Commentary Volume 2 reads the
Nazareth sermon as the Lord’s account of Israel’s history, and this volume cites it and
does not repeat it. Revelation 11:6 speaks of “power to shut heaven, that it rain not in
the days of their prophecy,” and the verse is cited here and read in chapter 11, where
the figures of the witnesses are set out and not named.
2.2 The widow’s son
At Zarephath the widow’s son falls sick “and his sickness was so sore, that there was
no breath left in him” (1 Kings 17:17). The woman’s first thought is of her sin, and
Elijah’s is of the LORD’s name: “O LORD my God, hast thou also brought evil upon the
widow with whom I sojourn, by slaying her son?” (1 Kings 17:20). He stretches himself
on the child three times and prays, “let this child’s soul come into him again,” and “the
LORD heard the voice of Elijah; and the soul of the child came into him again, and he
revived” (1 Kings 17:21, 22). The woman’s verdict is the verdict of the scene: “Now by
this I know that thou art a man of God, and that the word of the LORD in thy mouth is
truth” (1 Kings 17:24).
The scene is the first raising of the dead recorded in the Bible, and the volume names
it for one reason only. The Lord’s words at Nazareth about Elias say that he “was
sent” to a widow of Sidon, “unto Sarepta, a city of Sidon” (Luke 4:26), the same
Zarephath. The sending is the Lord’s own word for the errand of 1 Kings 17:9, and it
will matter later that the Lord uses the verb of sending for Elijah, as Malachi does.
2.3 Carmel: the heart turned back
The third year brings the meeting with Ahab. “Art thou he that troubleth Israel?” the
king asks, and Elijah answers, “I have not troubled Israel; but thou, and thy father’s
house, in that ye have forsaken the commandments of the LORD, and thou hast
followed Baalim” (1 Kings 18:17, 18). The contest is set on Carmel with “all Israel” and

four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18:19). Elijah’s first word is to the
people: “How long halt ye between two opinions? if the LORD be God, follow him: but
if Baal, then follow him. And the people answered him not a word” (1 Kings 18:21). He
says of himself, “I, even I only, remain a prophet of the LORD” (1 Kings 18:22).
Two details of the altar bear on Malachi. “And he repaired the altar of the LORD that
was broken down. And Elijah took twelve stones, according to the number of the
tribes of the sons of Jacob, unto whom the word of the LORD came, saying, Israel
shall be thy name” (1 Kings 18:30, 31). The altar is Israel’s altar and is rebuilt for all
twelve tribes, in a day when the kingdom was divided. It is the work of one man at an
altar for the whole people. And at the evening sacrifice he prays: “Hear me, O LORD,
hear me, that this people may know that thou art the LORD God, and that thou hast
turned their heart back again” (1 Kings 18:37). The fire falls, the people fall on their
faces, and they say, “The LORD, he is the God; the LORD, he is the God” (1 Kings 18:38,
39).
The phrase in the prayer is “turned their heart back again.” Malachi’s is “turn the heart
of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children to their fathers” (Malachi
4:6). The English words are the same word, to turn the heart, and the direction in
Kings is toward the LORD, as Luke 1:16 says of John: “And many of the children of
Israel shall he turn to the Lord their God.” The volume does not say that Malachi 4:6 is
only the Carmel work repeated. It says that the one scene in which the Bible shows
Elijah turning a whole people’s heart is a scene in which he uses Malachi’s phrase,
and that a reader who asks what the promised prophet’s work will look like has the
answer in Kings.
2.4 Horeb
After Carmel, Jezebel’s threat sends Elijah south to Beer-sheba and into the
wilderness, where he sits under a juniper tree and asks to die (1 Kings 19:2–4). An
angel feeds him twice and he goes “in the strength of that meat forty days and forty
nights unto Horeb the mount of God” (1 Kings 19:8). He lodges in a cave, and the
LORD asks the question twice: “What doest thou here, Elijah?” (1 Kings 19:9, 13). The
answer is the same both times: “I have been very jealous for the LORD God of hosts:
because the children of Israel have forsaken thy covenant, thrown down thine altars,
and slain thy prophets with the sword; and I, even I only, am left; and they seek my life,
to take it away” (1 Kings 19:14).
Horeb is the mountain named in Malachi 4:4: “the law of Moses my servant, which I
commanded unto him in Horeb for all Israel.” Elijah is the one prophet whose
recorded journey ends at that mountain, and he goes there to say that Israel has
forsaken the covenant given there. The wind, the earthquake and the fire pass, and the
LORD is not in them, “and after the fire a still small voice” (1 Kings 19:11, 12). Then
the LORD commissions him: Hazael, Jehu and Elisha, and an answer to his loneliness:
“Yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel, all the knees which have not bowed unto
Baal, and every mouth which hath not kissed him” (1 Kings 19:15–18). Elijah’s own
report is that all Israel is against him; the LORD’s is that a seven thousand remain. The
volume notes the contrast and leaves the seven thousand to the places that read
them.
Elisha’s call comes in the same chapter: Elijah “passed by him, and cast his mantle
upon him,” and Elisha “arose, and went after Elijah, and ministered unto him” (1 Kings

19:19, 21). The mantle, which falls again in 2 Kings 2, is the first sign that the
prophet’s work can pass to another.
2.5 Naboth’s vineyard
The last scene in 1 Kings is a word of judgment. Ahab has taken Naboth’s vineyard,
and the LORD sends Elijah: “Hast thou killed, and also taken possession?” (1 Kings
21:19). Ahab answers, “Hast thou found me, O mine enemy?” and the prophet says, “I
have found thee: because thou hast sold thyself to work evil in the sight of the LORD”
(1 Kings 21:20). The sentence on the house is set out. Then comes the moment that
matters for the volume: “And it came to pass, when Ahab heard those words, that he
rent his clothes, and put sackcloth upon his flesh, and fasted” (1 Kings 21:27). The
LORD says to Elijah, “Seest thou how Ahab humbleth himself before me? because he
humbleth himself before me, I will not bring the evil in his days: but in his son’s days
will I bring the evil upon his house” (1 Kings 21:29).
The scene shows what the first volume of this series reads as the conditional word of
a prophet. A judgment spoken by Elijah is delayed when the man judged humbles
himself. The volume draws one point from it for the question of Malachi’s Elijah. The
prophet who is to come before the day is a prophet whose word may be answered,
and the LORD’s answer to a turned heart is mercy for a time.
2.6 Ahaziah and the fire
The book of Kings closes its first volume with Ahaziah, who falls through a lattice and
sends to Baal-zebub of Ekron. The angel of the LORD tells Elijah to meet the
messengers: “Is it not because there is not a God in Israel, that ye go to enquire of
Baal-zebub the god of Ekron?” (2 Kings 1:3). The king asks what manner of man met
them, and they answer, “He was an hairy man, and girt with a girdle of leather about
his loins. And he said, It is Elijah the Tishbite” (2 Kings 1:8). The text does not say
whether the “hairy” is the man’s or his garment’s. Mark’s description of John is that he
“was clothed with camel’s hair, and with a girdle of a skin about his loins” (Mark 1:6),
and the likeness is in the wording and is noted without being pressed.
Fire falls twice on captains of fifty, “If I be a man of God, then let fire come down from
heaven, and consume thee and thy fifty” (2 Kings 1:10, 12), and the third captain falls
on his knees and begs, “let my life now be precious in thy sight,” and the angel says,
“Go down with him: be not afraid of him” (2 Kings 1:13, 15). The disciples will recall
the fire when a Samaritan village refuses the Lord, and their words, “wilt thou that we
command fire to come down from heaven, and consume them, even as Elias did?”
(Luke 9:54), are the New Testament’s only direct reference to this scene. The Lord’s
answer is read in chapter 10.

• Kings gives Elijah a name, a place, a title and a life, and the New Testament

3. The Whirlwind and What Came After
Claim: Elijah’s departure is the second fact the promise rests on. He did not die. He
“went up by a whirlwind into heaven” (2 Kings 2:11), and the text of Kings shows at
once that the prophets of the day treated the matter as open and that his spirit was
seen on another man. Both facts bear on Malachi 4:5. The first says that the man
named in the promise has not been reported dead. The second says that a man may
bear Elijah’s spirit and not be Elijah, which is the point on which the Gospels’ account
of John turns. A writing from Elijah is also recorded in Chronicles, and the volume
reports the passage and builds nothing on its timing.

The chapter opens with its own announcement: “when the LORD would take up Elijah
into heaven by a whirlwind, that Elijah went with Elisha from Gilgal” (2 Kings 2:1).

3.2 A double portion
On the farther bank Elijah says, “Ask what I shall do for thee, before I be taken away
from thee,” and Elisha asks, “let a double portion of thy spirit be upon me” (2 Kings

3.3 The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha
Elisha takes up the mantle that fell, returns to Jordan and asks, “Where is the LORD
God of Elijah?” (2 Kings 2:13, 14). The waters part for him, and the sons of the
prophets at Jericho, who had watched from the other side, say, “The spirit of Elijah
doth rest on Elisha” (2 Kings 2:15). They bow to the ground before him. A man other
than Elijah is recognized as bearing Elijah’s spirit, and the recognition is made by the
prophets themselves. He is not called Elijah, and the narrator does not call him Elijah.
He is Elisha, the son of Shaphat, “which poured water on the hands of Elijah” (2 Kings
3:11), and Jehoshaphat says of him, “The word of the LORD is with him” (2 Kings
3:12).
This is the precedent the volume wants. The Old Testament itself shows that the
spirit of Elijah can rest on a man who is not Elijah, that the prophets can say so in
those words, and that the man is received in that character. When Gabriel says that a
son of Zacharias will go before the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elias” (Luke 1:17),
the form of the sentence is the form of 2 Kings 2:15. The sentence does not say that
the man will be Elijah, and the volume reads it so in chapter 6.

4. The Messenger Before the Lord: Malachi 3:1
Claim: Malachi speaks of a forerunner twice, and the two sayings are not the same
saying. In 3:1 the LORD says, “I will send my messenger,” and the one who follows is
the LORD himself, coming suddenly to his temple. In 4:5 the LORD says, “I will send
you Elijah the prophet,” and what follows is the great and dreadful day. The Gospels
quote the first and leave the second where Malachi put it. A reader who keeps the two
apart can see why the Gospels can say that John fulfils the one and still leave the
other open.
4.1 The first saying
“Behold, I will send my messenger, and he shall prepare the way before me: and the
LORD, whom ye seek, shall suddenly come to his temple, even the messenger of the
covenant, whom ye delight in: behold, he shall come, saith the LORD of hosts”
(Malachi 3:1). The sentence has two persons in it. The first is “my messenger,” who
prepares the way “before me,” and the speaker is the LORD of hosts. The second is
“the LORD, whom ye seek,” who comes “to his temple,” and who is called “the
messenger of the covenant.” The sentence promises a forerunner and the coming of
the one he goes before, in one breath, and it names the place of the coming as the
temple.
The Hebrew behind “my messenger” is the same word as the name of the book.
Malachi 1:1 reads, “The burden of the word of the LORD to Israel by Malachi,” and the
name is the Hebrew form of “my messenger.” The volume notes the fact and does not
build on it. The book does not say that Malachi is the messenger of 3:1, and the
Gospels do not say so either; they apply the sentence to John.

refining, a purifying of the sons of Levi, and a judgment, and that the Gospels do not
report these things done when John came.
4.3 The Gospels quote the first half
Three Gospels quote Malachi 3:1, and each stops in the same place. Mark opens: “As
it is written in the prophets, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face, which shall
prepare thy way before thee” (Mark 1:2). The Lord says of John, “For this is he, of
whom it is written, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face, which shall prepare
thy way before thee” (Matthew 11:10; Luke 7:27). In all three the quotation ends at
“before thee.” None of them carries on to “and the LORD, whom ye seek, shall
suddenly come to his temple.”
Two differences from the Hebrew are visible in the Gospels’ wording. The Gospels
have “before thy face” and “thy way before thee,” where Malachi has “before me.” The
volume reports the difference and does not explain it. What the volume takes from it
is only that the speaker in the Gospels is addressing the Lord Jesus, and the sentence
the Gospels quote is the half of Malachi 3:1 that is about the forerunner. The half that
is about the coming to the temple is not quoted in any of the three places, and the
volume does not add it for them.
4.4 The two sayings side by side

about two. The text does not decide it on its face, and both readings have been held.

calls the figure “my messenger” and the figure prepares “the way before me.” In 4:5 he
calls him “Elijah the prophet,” and he comes “before the coming of the great and
dreadful day of the LORD.” In 3:1 what follows is the coming of the LORD to his
temple. In 4:5 what follows is the day.

5. The Voice in the Wilderness: Isaiah 40
Claim: the Gospels give John three descriptions drawn from Scripture, and they are
different descriptions. One is Malachi’s messenger, one is Isaiah’s voice, and one is
the spirit and power of Elias. Isaiah’s voice is the only one John applies to himself in
the Gospels, and he applies it without the title of Elijah. The chapter reads Isaiah
40:1–5 in its own place and then reads how each Gospel brings it to John, so that the
reader can see what is claimed for John by Isaiah and what is not.
5.1 Isaiah’s comfort
Isaiah 40 opens with a command to comfort: “Comfort ye, comfort ye my people,
saith your God. Speak ye comfortably to Jerusalem, and cry unto her, that her warfare
is accomplished, that her iniquity is pardoned: for she hath received of the LORD’s
hand double for all her sins” (Isaiah 40:1, 2). Then the voice: “The voice of him that
crieth in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the LORD, make straight in the desert a
highway for our God” (Isaiah 40:3). The preparation is of a highway, and the highway
is for “our God.” The valleys are lifted, the mountains made low, the crooked made

straight, and “the glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it
together: for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it” (Isaiah 40:4, 5).
The speaker of the passage is not named. It is “the voice of him that crieth,” and the
next verses show other voices: “The voice said, Cry. And he said, What shall I cry?”
(Isaiah 40:6). The prophet gives no name to the one who cries in the wilderness, and
he does not say that he is Elijah or that he is anyone but a voice. The passage is
addressed to Jerusalem, its comfort is Jerusalem’s comfort, and its end is the glory
of the LORD revealed.
5.2 How the Gospels bring it to John
All four Gospels apply Isaiah 40:3 to John. Mark joins it to Malachi: “As it is written in
the prophets, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face … The voice of one crying
in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make his paths straight” (Mark 1:2,
3). The King James text prints “in the prophets,” and the quotation that follows is from
two of them, Malachi and Isaiah. Matthew writes, “For this is he that was spoken of by
the prophet Esaias, saying, The voice of one crying in the wilderness, Prepare ye the
way of the Lord, make his paths straight” (Matthew 3:3). Luke carries on further into
Isaiah: “Every valley shall be filled, and every mountain and hill shall be brought low;
and the crooked shall be made straight, and the rough ways shall be made smooth;
and all flesh shall see the salvation of God” (Luke 3:5, 6). Luke’s setting is dated by
rulers: the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar, Pontius Pilate governor, “Annas and
Caiaphas being the high priests,” and then, “the word of God came unto John the son
of Zacharias in the wilderness” (Luke 3:1, 2).
John himself, in the fourth Gospel, uses the passage for himself when he is asked
who he is: “I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness, Make straight the way of the
Lord, as said the prophet Esaias” (John 1:23). This is the only place in the Gospels
where John answers the question of who he is with a text of Scripture, and the text is
Isaiah’s. He does not answer with Malachi 3:1 and he does not answer with Malachi
4:5.
5.3 What John preached and who came
The Gospels describe the message in the same terms. “In those days came John the
Baptist, preaching in the wilderness of Judaea, and saying, Repent ye: for the kingdom
of heaven is at hand” (Matthew 3:1, 2). Mark says, “John did baptize in the wilderness,
and preach the baptism of repentance for the remission of sins” (Mark 1:4), and then,
“there went out unto him all the land of Judaea, and they of Jerusalem, and were all
baptized of him in the river of Jordan, confessing their sins” (Mark 1:5). Matthew
says, “Then went out to him Jerusalem, and all Judaea, and all the region round about
Jordan, and were baptized of him in Jordan, confessing their sins” (Matthew 3:5, 6).
The message and its audience are Israel’s. “All the land of Judaea, and they of
Jerusalem” is the audience of Isaiah’s “comfort ye my people” and “speak ye
comfortably to Jerusalem.” The call is to repent because the kingdom of heaven is at
hand, and the kingdom is the one the prophets had announced to Israel. The volume
reads the passages in this setting and does not carry the message of John over to
anyone else. It is a message to Israel from a prophet of Israel about the kingdom, and
its baptism is “the baptism of repentance.”

6.1 A priest’s house
Luke opens in the days of Herod, “a certain priest named Zacharias, of the course of
Abia: and his wife was of the daughters of Aaron, and her name was Elisabeth” (Luke

without” (Luke 1:10), and the angel stands “on the right side of the altar of incense”

The angel’s words are these: “Fear not, Zacharias: for thy prayer is heard; and thy wife
Elisabeth shall bear thee a son, and thou shalt call his name John” (Luke 1:13). The
son is to be “great in the sight of the Lord,” abstaining from wine and strong drink,
“filled with the Holy Ghost, even from his mother’s womb” (Luke 1:15). The filling with
the Spirit is said of John from the womb, and the volume reports it and leaves it there.
6.2 Many of the children of Israel
Then the work: “And many of the children of Israel shall he turn to the Lord their God”
(Luke 1:16). The audience is Israel, and the number is “many.” Gabriel does not say

is “to the Lord their God,” which is the direction of Elijah’s prayer on Carmel in chapter

him,” and the pronoun’s antecedent is “the Lord their God” of the verse before. He
goes “in the spirit and power of Elias,” which is the form of 2 Kings 2:15, “the spirit of
Elijah doth rest on Elisha.” His work is “to turn the hearts of the fathers to the
children,” which is half of Malachi 4:6, with “the disobedient to the wisdom of the just”
in place of the second half. And the purpose is “to make ready a people prepared for
the Lord.”
6.3 What Gabriel leaves out
Three omissions are plain. Gabriel does not say that John is Elias. He does not quote
the second half of Malachi 4:6, “lest I come and smite the earth with a curse,” and he
does not mention the day. And he does not say that John restores all things. The
sentence is a statement of John’s office in terms of Elijah’s spirit and power, and the
aim is a prepared people. A reader who hears in it the whole of Malachi 4:5, 6 hears
more than the sentence says. A reader who hears in it nothing of Malachi hears less.
The Elisha precedent of chapter 3 is the clearest guide to the form. In Kings the
prophets said of Elisha, “The spirit of Elijah doth rest on Elisha” (2 Kings 2:15), and
Elisha was not called Elijah. In Luke the angel says of John that he will go “in the spirit
and power of Elias,” and John is not called Elias. The volume does not say that the
two cases are the same. It says that the form of words is one the Old Testament had
already used of a man who was not the man named, and that the form tells a reader
to expect a man who bears Elijah’s spirit and power.

7. John’s Answer: “I Am Not”
Claim: John the Baptist, asked directly whether he is Elias, says, “I am not.” The
statement is in the Gospels and it is John’s own. The volume does not set it aside and
does not set it against the Lord’s words in chapter 8. It reads the scene in John 1 as
written, with its questioners, its three denials and the text John gives for himself, and
then reads the second scene in John 3 where John speaks of his own place and his
decrease. The aim is to record exactly what John says, to whom, and what he does
not say.
7.1 The deputation
“And this is the record of John, when the Jews sent priests and Levites from
Jerusalem to ask him, Who art thou?” (John 1:19). The questioners are priests and
Levites, and the sender is “the Jews” from Jerusalem. The text adds a detail a few
verses on: “And they which were sent were of the Pharisees” (John 1:24). The
deputation is official and from the temple city. Its question is short, and the answers
come one at a time.
John’s first answer is a denial of the highest title: “And he confessed, and denied not;
but confessed, I am not the Christ” (John 1:20). The verbs are emphatic, “confessed,
and denied not,” and the answer is to a question that has not yet been asked in so
many words. The next question is the one this volume follows: “And they asked him,
What then? Art thou Elias? And he saith, I am not. Art thou that prophet? And he
answered, No” (John 1:21). Three questions, three denials. The Christ, Elias and “that
prophet” are three expected figures, and John is none of them. The questioners
reached for the second title on their own.
7.2 What the questioners expected
The priests and Levites ask, “Art thou Elias?” and the question shows that Elias was
an expected figure in Jerusalem. The question fits Malachi 4:5, and the questioners’
expectation, in the form the text records, is that Elias is a figure who is to come and
may be recognized. The question does not say how they conceived him, a returned
Tishbite or a prophet like him. The text records the question and the answer and gives
no more. The volume does not supply what they thought.
When they ask again, “Who art thou? that we may give an answer to them that sent
us. What sayest thou of thyself?” (John 1:22), John does not offer another title from
Malachi. He answers, “I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness, Make straight
the way of the Lord, as said the prophet Esaias” (John 1:23). The text he gives for
himself is Isaiah’s, as chapter 5 showed. The deputation then asks the question that
follows from his answers: “Why baptizest thou then, if thou be not that Christ, nor
Elias, neither that prophet?” (John 1:25). They treat baptizing as a right of one of the
three, and John does not dispute the connection. He points past himself: “I baptize
with water: but there standeth one among you, whom ye know not; he it is, who
coming after me is preferred before me, whose shoe’s latchet I am not worthy to
unloose” (John 1:26, 27).
7.3 What “I am not” says and does not say
The words “I am not” answer the question the priests asked, in the sense they asked
it. They are John’s statement about himself. Taken as written they say that John,

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 20

asked whether he is Elias, says no. They do not say that Malachi’s promise has no
further fulfilment, and they do not say that John’s ministry had nothing to do with
Elijah. John does not discuss Malachi 4:5. He does not say that Elias is yet to come,
and he does not say that Elias has come in him. The scene carries a single question
and a single answer, and the volume reads it with the same restraint.
The volume sets one more fact beside it. Gabriel’s sentence in Luke 1:17 does not call
John Elias, and John’s answer in John 1:21 does not either. The two statements
agree in this: neither uses the title for John. What the Lord says of him in Matthew
11:14 is read in the next chapter in its own setting. The volume does not reconcile
John 1:21 with Matthew 11:14 beyond what the passages state, and the reader is told
so here so as not to expect it.
7.4 John 3: the friend of the bridegroom
A second scene shows John speaking of his place. A question arises between John’s
disciples and a Jew about purifying, and the disciples come to John with a grievance:
“Rabbi, he that was with thee beyond Jordan, to whom thou barest witness, behold,
the same baptizeth, and all men come to him” (John 3:26). John answers, “A man can
receive nothing, except it be given him from heaven. Ye yourselves bear me witness,
that I said, I am not the Christ, but that I am sent before him” (John 3:27, 28). The
phrase “sent before him” is the nearest John comes to the language of Malachi 3:1, “I
will send my messenger.”
Then the figure of the wedding: “He that hath the bride is the bridegroom: but the
friend of the bridegroom, which standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth greatly because
of the bridegroom’s voice: this my joy therefore is fulfilled. He must increase, but I
must decrease” (John 3:29, 30). The bridegroom and the bride are read in Prophetic
Identity Volume 1, and this volume does not repeat the reading. The point here is
John’s own account of his office. He is the friend who stands and hears, and his joy is
full when the bridegroom’s voice is heard. His decrease is the other side of the Lord’s
increase.
7.5 For a season
The Lord himself speaks of John’s work as bounded. “Ye sent unto John, and he bare
witness unto the truth. But I receive not testimony from man: but these things I say,
that ye might be saved. He was a burning and a shining light: and ye were willing for a
season to rejoice in his light” (John 5:33–35). The verb is past, “he was,” and the time
is limited, “for a season.” The audience is the Jerusalem leadership that had sent to
John, the same company John 1:19 describes. The volume notes the Lord’s phrase
because it frames what John’s ministry was: a light the leaders were willing to rejoice
in for a season. It was not all they were told it could be.

8. The Lord’s Answer About John
Claim: when the Lord speaks of John, he says three things that the volume holds side
by side. John is “more than a prophet” and the messenger of Malachi 3:1. John is
Elias “if ye will receive it.” And this generation refused both John and the Son of man.
The first two are said in one sitting, and the third follows at once. The chapter reads
Matthew 11 and Luke 7 in order, with the question that John asked from prison, the
answer the Lord gave to John’s disciples, and the words the Lord then spoke to the
crowds.
8.1 The question from prison
“Now when John had heard in the prison the works of Christ, he sent two of his
disciples, and said unto him, Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?”
(Matthew 11:2, 3). Luke tells the same story at greater length, with the disciples
repeating the question to the Lord (Luke 7:19, 20). The question is John’s, from
prison, about the Lord. The words “he that should come” echo the language of the
coming one, and the volume does not interpret John’s motive. The text gives the
question and nothing of why it was asked.
The Lord’s answer is a list of works: “Go and shew John again those things which ye
do hear and see” (Matthew 11:4), and the works follow: “The blind receive their sight,
and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up,
and the poor have the gospel preached to them” (Matthew 11:5). Luke adds that in
that same hour he “cured many of their infirmities and plagues, and of evil spirits”
(Luke 7:21). The list is drawn from Isaiah’s pictures of the coming one: the blind

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 22

seeing and the lame walking (Isaiah 35:5, 6) and good tidings to the poor (Isaiah
61:1). The answer to “art thou he” is to point to what Isaiah said would be done. Then
a word to John: “And blessed is he, whosoever shall not be offended in me” (Matthew
11:6).
8.2 More than a prophet
When the messengers have gone, the Lord turns to the multitudes. Three times he
asks what they went out to see: “A reed shaken with the wind?” “A man clothed in soft
raiment?” “A prophet?” (Matthew 11:7–9). The answer to the third question is “yea, I
say unto you, and more than a prophet.” Then the text: “For this is he, of whom it is
written, Behold, I send my messenger before thy face, which shall prepare thy way
before thee” (Matthew 11:10). Luke’s form is “and much more than a prophet,” with
the same quotation (Luke 7:26, 27).
The Lord’s application of Malachi 3:1 to John is complete and plain. The volume
follows it. John is the messenger of whom Malachi wrote, and he prepared the way. In
the sentence that follows the Lord says, “Among them that are born of women there
hath not risen a greater than John the Baptist: notwithstanding he that is least in the
kingdom of heaven is greater than he” (Matthew 11:11). The saying about “the least in
the kingdom” and the saying that follows about violence, “from the days of John the
Baptist until now the kingdom of heaven suffereth violence, and the violent take it by
force” (Matthew 11:12), are read in Prophecy & Mystery Volume 3 and in the first
volume of the Readers Question Series, and this volume cites them and does not
argue them.
8.3 “If ye will receive it”
Then the sentences on which the whole question turns. “For all the prophets and the
law prophesied until John. And if ye will receive it, this is Elias, which was for to come.
He that hath ears to hear, let him hear” (Matthew 11:13–15). Luke’s account does not
carry these sentences. The statement is conditional, “if ye will receive it,” and it is
addressed to the multitudes. The clause “which was for to come” is in the past tense
of expectation: Elias was the one who was to come. The Lord says that John is that
Elias, on the condition that the hearers will receive it.
The condition is not decoration. A statement made on the condition of a hearer’s
reception is a statement about an offer. If the offer is received, John is Elias in the
sense the Lord names. If it is not, the statement does not take effect in that sense,
and the text will show in chapter 9 that the Lord speaks of Elias as one who “shall first
come” after John has been put to death. The volume reads Matthew 11:14 as the
Lord’s offer to the nation, made in John’s day, of the identification of John as the Elias
of Malachi. It reads the offer as one the nation did not accept, and the next sentences
confirm that.
8.4 This generation
The Lord goes on: “But whereunto shall I liken this generation? It is like unto children
sitting in the markets, and calling unto their fellows, and saying, We have piped unto
you, and ye have not danced; we have mourned unto you, and ye have not lamented.
For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, He hath a devil. The Son of
man came eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a
winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners” (Matthew 11:16–19). The generation

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 23

refused John for his austerity and the Son of man for his ease. Both messages were
rejected.
Luke adds a verse on the division within the people: “And all the people that heard
him, and the publicans, justified God, being baptized with the baptism of John. But the
Pharisees and lawyers rejected the counsel of God against themselves, being not
baptized of him” (Luke 7:29, 30). The people and the publicans justified God, and the
Pharisees and lawyers rejected his counsel. The Pharisees are the company from
which the deputation of John 1:24 came. The Gospel record shows a split: some
received John, and the leaders refused him.
8.5 Two words, one scene
The chapter has now two statements about John from the Lord within a few lines.
John is the messenger of Malachi 3:1, and John is Elias if the hearers will receive it.
They are not the same sentence and the Lord does not merge them. The first is a
statement of fact grounded in a text, “this is he, of whom it is written.” The second is a
conditional about the hearers. The volume holds them as the Lord states them. It
does not say that John failed or that the Lord withdrew an offer. It says that the Lord
made one, and that the generation he describes in verses 16–19 did not take it.

9. The Mountain and the Question: Matthew 17
and Mark 9
Claim: on the way down from the mountain the disciples ask about Elias, and the Lord
answers with two sentences that look in opposite directions. “Elias truly shall first
come, and restore all things” is in the future. “Elias is come already, and they knew
him not” is in the past. Mark gives the same pair in the present and the past. The
chapter reads the whole scene in order, the mountain, the question and the answer,
and records what each sentence says before the next chapter asks what the pair
means together.
9.1 Elias on the mountain
Six days after the confession at Caesarea Philippi, “Jesus taketh Peter, James, and
John his brother, and bringeth them up into an high mountain apart, and was
transfigured before them: and his face did shine as the sun, and his raiment was white
as the light” (Matthew 17:1, 2). Then: “And, behold, there appeared unto them Moses
and Elias talking with him” (Matthew 17:3). Mark gives the order the other way: “And
there appeared unto them Elias with Moses: and they were talking with Jesus” (Mark
9:4). Moses and Elias are named, they are seen and they talk with the Lord. The text
does not say how the disciples knew them, and it does not say what was said.
Peter answers, “Lord, it is good for us to be here: if thou wilt, let us make here three
tabernacles; one for thee, and one for Moses, and one for Elias” (Matthew 17:4). Mark
adds, “For he wist not what to say; for they were sore afraid” (Mark 9:6). A voice out of
the cloud says, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased; hear ye him”
(Matthew 17:5), and when the disciples lift their eyes, “they saw no man, save Jesus
only” (Matthew 17:8). Moses stands for the law and Elias for the prophets in the
common reading, and the text does not say so. The volume notes what is said: two
named men, one of whom is Elias, appeared with the Lord on the mountain, and the
voice named the Lord alone as the one to hear.
9.2 The charge and the question
“And as they came down from the mountain, Jesus charged them, saying, Tell the
vision to no man, until the Son of man be risen again from the dead” (Matthew 17:9).
Mark adds that “they kept that saying with themselves, questioning one with another
what the rising from the dead should mean” (Mark 9:10). The charge is silence until
the resurrection, and the disciples puzzle over the words. Then the question comes:
“Why then say the scribes that Elias must first come?” (Matthew 17:10). Mark’s form
is “Why say the scribes that Elias must first come?” (Mark 9:11).
The question has a premise and a puzzle. The premise is that the scribes teach that
Elias must first come, and the Lord does not dispute the teaching. The puzzle is the
word “then.” The disciples have just seen Elias with the Lord, and they have heard the
Lord speak of rising from the dead. Their question asks how the scribes’ teaching,

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 25

that Elias must first come, fits what they have seen. The text does not tell us what
they had in mind, and the volume does not guess.
9.3 The two answers
“And Jesus answered and said unto them, Elias truly shall first come, and restore all
things” (Matthew 17:11). The verb is future, “shall … come,” and the sentence carries
the word “truly” and a second work, “restore all things.” Mark’s form is present: “Elias
verily cometh first, and restoreth all things” (Mark 9:12). In both, the Lord affirms the
scribes’ teaching. He does not say they are mistaken. He says that Elias comes first
and restores all things.
Then: “But I say unto you, That Elias is come already, and they knew him not, but have
done unto him whatsoever they listed. Likewise shall also the Son of man suffer of
them” (Matthew 17:12). Mark’s form: “But I say unto you, That Elias is indeed come,
and they have done unto him whatsoever they listed, as it is written of him” (Mark
9:13). The sentence changes the tense and adds an adversative, “But I say unto you.”
Elias “is come already.” The verb is past, the people “knew him not,” and what they did
to him is what the Son of man will suffer. Then Matthew adds: “Then the disciples
understood that he spake unto them of John the Baptist” (Matthew 17:13).
9.4 What the sentences say
Five features of the text are plain. First, the Lord affirms the scribes’ teaching: Elias
“truly shall first come.” Second, he adds a work that is not in Malachi’s wording of 4:5,
6, “restore all things.” Third, he says that Elias “is come already,” and the people “knew
him not.” Fourth, what was done to Elias is what the Son of man will suffer. Fifth, the
disciples understood the second sentence to be about John the Baptist, and Matthew
places that understanding after verse 12, not after verse 11.
The two sentences are joined by “But,” and the volume notes what Matthew 17:13
attaches to John. It says that the disciples “understood that he spake unto them of
John the Baptist.” The “he spake” has the whole of what the Lord said as its object,
and the verse can be read as attaching John to both sentences or to the second. The
volume does not decide that on grammar. The difference between the two readings is
the subject of the next chapter. Mark’s “as it is written of him” (Mark 9:13) points to
Scripture that records what was done to Elias. The nearest written account of
suffering for Elijah is in Kings, where Jezebel seeks his life and he says, “I, even I only,
am left; and they seek my life, to take it away” (1 Kings 19:10, 14). Mark does not
name the passage, and the volume reports the nearest and builds no more.
9.5 Restitution in Acts
The Lord’s phrase “restore all things” has a counterpart in Peter’s sermon in the
temple. “Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out,
when the times of refreshing shall come from the presence of the Lord; and he shall
send Jesus Christ, which before was preached unto you: whom the heaven must
receive until the times of restitution of all things, which God hath spoken by the mouth
of all his holy prophets since the world began” (Acts 3:19–21). The word “restitution”
and the word “restore” are related in the Greek, and the volume reports that fact and
does not rest an argument on it. The first volume of the Acts Commentary Series
reads the sermon, and this volume cites it. What the passage says is that “the times
of restitution of all things” lie ahead of Peter’s hearers, that heaven must receive
Jesus until then, and that the prophets spoke of it.

10. Who Do Men Say? The Crowds, Herod and the
Cross
Claim: the Gospels keep three lists of what people said about Jesus, and in every list
John and Elias appear as two separate persons. The lists come after John’s death. At
the cross the bystanders think the Lord calls for Elias and wait to see whether he will
come. Between those two scenes the disciples ask the Lord to bring down fire as
Elias did, and he rebukes them. The chapter reads these passages as written, in order.
It does not use them to settle the question; it records what the texts show about how
the people thought of Elias.
10.1 Herod and the three lists
Herod the tetrarch hears of the Lord’s fame and says, “This is John the Baptist; he is
risen from the dead; and therefore mighty works do shew forth themselves in him”
(Matthew 14:1, 2). Mark records the same, and adds what others said: “Others said,
That it is Elias. And others said, That it is a prophet, or as one of the prophets” (Mark
6:14, 15). Luke has it the same way: Herod “was perplexed, because that it was said

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 27

of some, that John was risen from the dead; and of some, that Elias had appeared;
and of others, that one of the old prophets was risen again” (Luke 9:7, 8). Matthew
has already told of John’s end: “And he sent, and beheaded John in the prison”
(Matthew 14:10). Herod’s own verdict is, in Mark, “It is John, whom I beheaded: he is
risen from the dead” (Mark 6:16).
Three positions stand side by side in the lists: John risen, Elias appeared, and one of
the prophets. Elias is not John in this list. The people who say “Elias” are not the
people who say “John,” and Herod’s group, who say John, are not the same as those
who say Elias. The lists come after John’s death, and the opinion that Elias had
“appeared” stands as a separate opinion even though the one the Lord had called
Elias in Matthew 11:14 had already been killed.
10.2 Caesarea Philippi
The Lord asks his disciples what men say. “Whom do men say that I the Son of man
am? And they said, Some say that thou art John the Baptist: some, Elias; and others,
Jeremias, or one of the prophets” (Matthew 16:13, 14). Mark: “John the Baptist: but
some say, Elias; and others, One of the prophets” (Mark 8:28). Luke: “John the Baptist;
but some say, Elias; and others say, that one of the old prophets is risen again” (Luke
9:19). The disciples report the list, and the Lord goes on to ask what they say. Peter
answers, “The Christ of God” (Luke 9:20).
The wording is steady across the three accounts: John, Elias, a prophet. The disciples
give the people’s list without comment. The volume notes only that the list names
Elias as a separate figure from John a second time, in a scene the Gospels place after
John’s death, and that the Lord does not correct the list in the passage.
10.3 The prophet and the Prophet
Two other titles appear in John. After the feeding of the multitude, “those men, when
they had seen the miracle that Jesus did, said, This is of a truth that prophet that
should come into the world” (John 6:14). At the feast, “Many of the people therefore,
when they heard this saying, said, Of a truth this is the Prophet” (John 7:40). “That
prophet” is the figure the deputation of John 1:21 asked John about, and he said, No.
The volume names the figure and does not take up whom the title names. For the
question of Elias, the point is that the people of the Gospels kept a list of expected
figures, and Elias was on it.
10.4 At the cross
When the Lord cries from the cross, “Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani?” the bystanders
mishear: “Some of them that stood there, when they heard that, said, This man calleth
for Elias” (Matthew 27:46, 47). Mark has it, “Behold, he calleth Elias” (Mark 15:35).
Then one runs with a sponge, and “the rest said, Let be, let us see whether Elias will
come to save him” (Matthew 27:49). Mark’s form is “let us see whether Elias will
come to take him down” (Mark 15:36).
The bystanders’ words are the last mention of Elias in the Gospels. They show that a
crowd at the cross thought of Elias as a figure who might come to a man in distress,
to save him, and who might be called. The text records their expectation and records
that no one came. The volume does not interpret the crowd's belief. It reports that a
belief in Elias’s coming was present at the cross, in the days after John’s death, in the
mouths of those who stood by.

10.5 The fire refused
The last passage is the Samaritan village. “And it came to pass, when the time was
come that he should be received up, he stedfastly set his face to go to Jerusalem, and
sent messengers before his face: and they went, and entered into a village of the
Samaritans, to make ready for him. And they did not receive him, because his face
was as though he would go to Jerusalem” (Luke 9:51–53). Then: “And when his
disciples James and John saw this, they said, Lord, wilt thou that we command fire to
come down from heaven, and consume them, even as Elias did?” (Luke 9:54).
The disciples name Elias as a precedent, and the precedent is 2 Kings 1. The Lord
“turned, and rebuked them, and said, Ye know not what manner of spirit ye are of. For
the Son of man is not come to destroy men’s lives, but to save them” (Luke 9:55, 56).
The answer does not deny that Elias called fire. It says that the disciples do not know

11. “Shall First Come” and “Is Come Already”
Claim: the Lord’s two sentences in Matthew 17:11 and 17:12 are not a contradiction
and are not one statement said twice. The first is about a coming still ahead when he
spoke it, by a man who restores all things. The second is about a coming already past,
by a man whom the people did not know and who suffered. The chapter holds that the
text leads to a further coming of Elijah before the day of the LORD, in the line of Israel,
and sets out the reasons in order: the order of events, the words of the Lord, the form
of Gabriel’s sentence, the man on the mountain, and the passage in Revelation 11 that
the volume cites and does not name.
11.1 What Malachi 4:5 fixes
Malachi’s promise has one time mark, and it is “before the coming of the great and
dreadful day of the LORD” (Malachi 4:5). Elijah is sent before the day. The promise
does not say how long before, and it does not say that the sending is fulfilled in one
lifetime or in one visit. The test the volume applies is the test of Malachi’s own
sentence: what is it that must come first, and has the day come? The Gospels record
that John came and preached and was put to death. They do not record that the great
and dreadful day followed. A promise that Elijah comes before the day is met by a
coming that precedes the day, and the day, in the Gospels and after them, has not
come.
11.2 The order of events
Matthew tells of John’s death in chapter 14, three chapters before the Lord says
“Elias truly shall first come” (Matthew 14:10; 17:11). The sentence is spoken after
John has been beheaded. A speaker who says “shall first come” after the man in
question has died is, on its plain reading, speaking of a coming still to be. If the Lord
meant that John’s ministry had already accomplished the coming, the future tense
would have no work to do, and the disciples, who had watched John’s ministry end,
would have had no reason to ask. The question the disciples ask is also in the
present, “say the scribes that Elias must first come,” and the Lord does not say that
the scribes’ teaching has been met. He says that it is so.
The second sentence does not take back the first. It adds a fact: “Elias is come
already, and they knew him not.” The adversative “But I say unto you” sets the second
sentence against what the disciples might conclude from the first, that nothing has
yet been done. The Lord corrects that conclusion. Something has been done, and it
was not recognized. The volume holds both sentences in the order in which the Lord
gave them, the first in the future and the second in the past.
11.3 The words “restore all things”
The work the Lord names is “restore all things.” Malachi 4:6 speaks of turning hearts,
and the Lord’s phrase is wider. It speaks of everything. The Gospels do not report that
John’s ministry restored all things. They report the opposite: a ministry of repentance
that drew the crowds, that the leaders refused, and that ended in a prison and a
beheading (Matthew 14:3–10). The Lord himself says of the generation that it refused
both John and the Son of man (Matthew 11:16–19). The nation was not turned and
the land was not restored. The Lord’s phrase has a counterpart in Peter’s word in the
temple that the heaven must receive Jesus “until the times of restitution of all things,
which God hath spoken by the mouth of all his holy prophets since the world began”

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 30

(Acts 3:21). That restitution is spoken of as still ahead after the cross and the
resurrection, and it is Israel’s: Peter speaks to “ye men of Israel” (Acts 3:12) and to
those who “are the children of the prophets, and of the covenant” (Acts 3:25). The
volume reads the Lord’s “restore all things” as the work of the coming that is still
ahead.
11.4 The form of Gabriel’s sentence
Gabriel’s sentence has been read in chapter 6. Its form is the form the Old Testament
used of Elisha, a man who bears another’s spirit and is not that man. The sentence is
“in the spirit and power of Elias,” and not “as Elias.” If the angel had meant that John
would be Elijah, the Old Testament’s own precedent for the form is against him; if he
meant that John would do Elijah’s work and bear Elijah’s spirit, the form fits. The
sentence thus implies a distinct man: the Elias whose spirit and power John bore. The
volume reads Luke 1:17 as consistent with Matthew 17:11 for this reason. A man who
goes before the Lord in Elijah’s spirit stands in an order of such men, and the Elias
himself is not that man.
The same point stands behind John’s “I am not.” John says that he is not Elias, and
the Lord says that Elias is come already. Chapter 7 held that the volume does not
reconcile the two statements beyond the text. What the volume adds here is that the
statements fit a coming of Elias in the person of one who bore his spirit, and that this
coming is not the whole of what the Lord calls “shall first come.” John is Elias in the
sense of Matthew 11:14, if the nation will receive it. John is not Elias in the sense of
John 1:21, the person the priests asked about. Neither sentence needs to be rewritten
for the reading, and neither is made to say more than it does.
11.5 The man on the mountain
On the mount of transfiguration, “there appeared unto them Moses and Elias talking
with him” (Matthew 17:3). The text names a person, Elias, who appears, and in the
same scene, a few verses later, the Lord speaks of Elias as one who “shall first come”
and as one who “is come already.” The disciples saw Elias. They did not take him for
John, for John was dead and they were told afterwards that the Lord spoke of John.
The volume does not build a theory on the appearance. It notes that in the same
scene the Gospels name Elias as a person who appears, as one who comes first, and
as one who has come, and that the three are not the same use of the name.
11.6 Revelation 11
The last text the volume sets beside the question is Revelation 11. “And I will give
power unto my two witnesses, and they shall prophesy a thousand two hundred and
threescore days, clothed in sackcloth” (Revelation 11:3). Then: “if any man will hurt
them, fire proceedeth out of their mouth, and devoureth their enemies” (Revelation
11:5), and “These have power to shut heaven, that it rain not in the days of their
prophecy” (Revelation 11:6). The next verses tell that the beast shall overcome them
and kill them, that after three days and a half “the Spirit of life from God entered into
them, and they stood upon their feet,” and that they heard “a great voice from heaven
saying unto them, Come up hither. And they ascended up to heaven in a cloud”
(Revelation 11:7–12). The passage opens with the measuring of the temple and the
holy city given to the Gentiles “forty and two months” (Revelation 11:1, 2).
The volume sets out the features and does not name the witnesses. The fire from the
mouth is the fire of 2 Kings 1. The power to shut heaven is the power of 1 Kings 17:1.

The days, one thousand two hundred and threescore, are three years and a half by the
count of thirty days to the month, the same span James gives to the shutting of the
heaven in Elijah’s day (James 5:17) and the Lord gives in Luke 4:25. The ascent into
heaven in a cloud after a death is the end of a prophet’s ministry, as the whirlwind was
Elijah’s. The setting is a temple at Jerusalem and a city given to the Gentiles, the

passage does not call anyone Elijah, and the volume does not add the name. The
Lord’s two sentences say that Elias shall first come, and Revelation 11 shows
prophets of Elijah’s works in the days of the beast. The volume lays them side by side.
11.7 What the volume holds, and what it does not say
The position is this. Malachi 4:5 promises Elijah the prophet before the day of the
LORD. John went before the Lord in his spirit and power and was, if the nation would
receive it, Elias. The nation did not receive it, John was put to death, and the Lord
spoke afterwards of Elias as one who shall first come and restore all things. The
restoring is not reported done and the day has not come. The text therefore points to
a further coming of Elijah before the day, in Israel’s line, as the restorer. The Body of
Christ is not read into any of these passages.
The volume does not say when this coming is. It does not say where. It does not say
that the person who comes is the Tishbite of Kings in the flesh, and it does not say
that he is another man in Elijah’s spirit. It does not identify the witnesses of Revelation
11. It does not set a date for the day of the LORD, which is read in Study 7 and in the
fifth volume of the Prophecy & Mystery Commentary Series. It does not say that every
sentence about Elijah in the Gospels is about the further coming. The position is as
large as the text allows and no larger.

12. Objections and Replies
Claim: the volume’s reading, that Malachi promises one Elijah, that John came in his
spirit and power and was Elias if the nation would receive it, and that the text points to
a further coming before the day, answers the objections best made against it, and
where an objector is right the volume says so. Modern writers who hold parts of the
position are named beside those who press against it. A writer is quoted only where
the author has read the words in a free text. A writer’s view is described in the
volume’s own words when only a summary of it was read, and no quotation marks are
used for it.
12.1 Who agrees, and where the volume differs
Three modern writers hold that Malachi’s promise has a coming still ahead. Thomas
Ice, in an article titled “Elijah is Coming” published by the Pre-Trib Research Center in
2018, writes, “So Elijah is still to come.” He holds that the promise was offered to
Israel in John’s day and not taken up. His sentence, “John the Baptist fulfilled a
different prediction, that of Malachi 3:1 and Isaiah 40:3-5,” keeps the two sayings of
chapter 4 apart, as this volume does. He also writes that “because of Israel’s
rejection, John the Baptist was John the Baptist (My messenger) and not Elijah.” The
volume agrees on the offer and its refusal and on the two sayings. It differs in two
respects. Ice places the coming in the tribulation, and this volume says only that the
coming is before the day of the LORD, and it does not name the period. And Ice’s
article reads the coming of Elijah as a visit by Elijah himself, while the volume leaves
open whether the one who comes is the Tishbite or a man in his spirit.
Walter C. Kaiser Jr., in an article in Grace Theological Journal in 1982 titled “The
Promise of the Arrival of Elijah in Malachi and the Gospels,” sets out three ways to
read the promise. The first is that John fully fulfilled it. The second is that the Tishbite
returns in person. The third is that John came in Elijah’s spirit and power as one of a
series of forerunners. Kaiser holds the third. He writes that “John then was Elijah as
an earnest, but we still await the other Elijahs.” He also argues that a present-tense
verb in the Lord’s sentence is, in his words, a “futuristic present,” because “this
present is coupled with a future tense.” The volume agrees that John came as an
earnest, to use Kaiser’s word, and that the Lord’s sentences look ahead. It differs in
not adopting his series. The volume asks what the Gospels say about the Elijah of

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 33

Malachi 4:5 and holds one further coming before the day; it does not say that
Scripture lists a line of forerunners.
Paul Henebury, in an article titled “John the Baptist and Elijah” on the SharperIron site
in 2021, grants the point many make and denies the conclusion drawn from it. He
writes, “Many scholars have argued that the fulfillment of the Elijah prophecy (of Mal.
4:5), is to be confined to John the Baptist.” His answer is, “But this does not force us
to conclude that there is no future work for Elijah to do.” He goes on to write, “At the
second coming of Messiah as Conqueror and King, Elijah himself returns to prepare
the way.” The volume agrees that a work remains. It differs by not tying the coming to
the Messiah’s return as a date, since no passage says so, and by not saying that the
person is Elijah himself. The three writers support the shape of the position and none
of them is quoted for more than they say.
12.2 Objection: Malachi speaks of an office, and John filled it
The strongest opposing reading holds that Malachi’s Elijah is John. Angus Stewart, in
an address titled “The Coming of Elijah” given at the Covenant Protestant Reformed
Church in 2004, writes, “Elijah in our text is John the Baptist, not the literal Tishbite
revived.” His reason is that “Malachi 4:5 speaks of Elijah’s office rather than His
person,” and he adds, “Similarly, Malachi is not prophesying that Elijah will return and
labour again.” His sentence of the pattern is “Rather, one like Elijah will do a work like
Elijah.” An unsigned devotional in the Ligonier Ministries series Tabletalk takes the
same side. It writes, “However, Malachi did not predict a return of the very same Elijah
in the flesh.” The objection is that the promise is met by a man in Elijah’s office, and
John was that man.
Reply. The volume grants three things. First, a man in Elijah’s office or in his spirit and
power is what Luke 1:17 says of John, and the volume has read it so. Second, the
promise does not require that the Tishbite himself come, and the volume does not say
that he does. Third, the devotional is right about the people of John’s day: it writes,
“The Jews were expecting a literal return of Elijah, and in that respect, John the
Baptist certainly was not Elijah,” which is what John says in John 1:21. The volume
parts from the objection at one point. The office reading says that the promise is
closed because John held the office. The Lord’s sentence, “Elias truly shall first come,
and restore all things” (Matthew 17:11), is spoken after John’s death, and the
restoring is not reported done. If the office was filled by John and the promise closed,
the future tense of the Lord’s sentence has no work to do. A man may fill an office in
the sense of Matthew 11:14 and the office may be filled again before the day.
12.3 Objection: “Elias is come already” settles it
The objection reads the second sentence of Matthew 17:12 as the Lord’s last word:
Elias is come already, the disciples understood that he spake of John, and the matter
is closed. Mark’s form, “Elias is indeed come” (Mark 9:13), is read the same way.
Reply. The sentence is the second of a pair, and the pair begins, “Elias truly shall first
come, and restore all things.” The Lord does not say that Elias has come and that the
scribes are therefore wrong. He says that the scribes’ teaching is true, and then he
says “But I say unto you” that Elias is come already and they knew him not. A reader
who takes only the second sentence takes half of what the Lord said, and a reader
who takes only the first takes the other half. The volume holds both in the order in
which they were spoken. Matthew 17:13 says that the disciples understood that he

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 4 • 34

spake unto them of John, and the volume reports that the verse comes after both
sentences.
12.4 Objection: a further coming adds what Jesus did not say
A reader may say that the volume adds to the Gospels a coming the Lord never
described. No writer is named for this; it is the volume’s own statement of the worry.
Reply. The Lord said that Elias “shall first come, and restore all things,” and the
volume takes the sentence at its word. What the volume adds is two observations
drawn from the text: the sentence was spoken after John’s death, and the restoring is
not reported done. It adds no date, no place, no name, no description of the coming
beyond what Malachi and the Lord say. The charge of adding applies with equal force
to a reading that says the promise is closed, for that reading adds that the future
tense of Matthew 17:11 is a figure for something already past.
12.5 Objection: “restore all things” is John’s preaching
Another reader may say that “restore all things” describes what John’s preaching did,
calling the nation to repentance. No writer is named. Reply. The Gospels describe
John’s preaching as a call to repent, with crowds going out to him (Matthew 3:5, 6),
and they describe the leaders refusing it (Luke 7:30), and the Lord says that “this
generation” refused both John and the Son of man (Matthew 11:16–19). They do not
report that all things were restored. Peter, after the cross, speaks of restitution as
ahead, “until the times of restitution of all things” (Acts 3:21). The volume takes the
Lord’s phrase to be as wide as it sounds, and reports that the Gospels do not say it
was met.
12.6 Objection: John 1:21 and Matthew 11:14 contradict
A last objection says that if John says “I am not” and the Lord says “this is Elias,” one
of them is wrong. No writer is named. Reply. The two statements are each made in a
setting. John answers priests and Levites of the Pharisees who ask a question about
a figure they expected (John 1:19–25). The Lord speaks to the multitudes and makes
his statement conditional: “if ye will receive it” (Matthew 11:14). The volume has not
argued that the two statements are the same statement. It has said that neither is
made to say more than it says, and that neither needs to be rewritten for the reading
to stand. If the reader holds that they conflict, the reading of this volume does not
depend on resolving the conflict, since the further coming does not rest on either
sentence alone.

12.7 What the volume takes from the replies
• Ice, Kaiser and Henebury hold that a work of Elijah remains; the volume agrees in
shape and differs on the period, the person and any series of forerunners.
• Stewart and the Tabletalk devotional are right that Luke 1:17 speaks of an office
and a spirit, and right that John was not the returned Tishbite in the people’s
sense.
• The office reading does not account for the future tense of Matthew 17:11 spoken
after John’s death.
• “Elias is come already” is the second of a pair; the first sentence is “Elias truly
shall first come, and restore all things.”
• John 1:21 and Matthew 11:14 are each read in their own setting, and the position
does not rest on resolving them.

Conclusion
The volume began with a plain statement: it argues a position. The position is that
Malachi promises one Elijah the prophet before the day of the LORD, that the Gospels
answer in three voices, and that the text points to a further coming. Gabriel says that
John will go before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elias. John, asked by priests
and Levites whether he is Elias, says, “I am not.” The Lord says, “if ye will receive it,
this is Elias, which was for to come,” and after John’s death says, “Elias truly shall first
come, and restore all things,” and then, “Elias is come already, and they knew him
not.” The three voices are three statements made in three settings, and the volume
has held each in its own place.
It has read the offer of Matthew 11:14 as a conditional one made to Israel in John’s
day. It has read the refusal in the verses that follow, where this generation is likened
to children who would neither dance nor mourn, and in the end of John in the prison. It
has read the restoring of all things as work not reported done, and the day of the
LORD as not yet come. The text therefore points to a further coming of Elijah before
the day, in Israel’s line, as the restorer. The volume has set Revelation 11 beside the
position and listed what the chapter itself says, and it has not named the witnesses. It
has not read the Body of Christ into any of the passages.
