Preface
This volume argues a position. It does not gather what churches say about the
oracles against the nations and leave the reader to choose. It says what the author
believes the prophets themselves teach about the nations they judged, it gives the
evidence from their own sentences, and it answers the strongest objections in the
objectors’ words, so that a reader who disagrees knows exactly where.
The question is narrow, and the prophets raise it. Jeremiah is told at his calling, “I
have this day set thee over the nations and over the kingdoms, to root out, and to pull
down, and to destroy, and to throw down, to build, and to plant” (Jeremiah 1:10).
Amos opens his book with a roar from Zion and six sentences, each beginning “For
three transgressions,” against Damascus, Gaza, Tyrus, Edom, Ammon and Moab
(Amos 1:3–2:3). Isaiah has a “burden” for Babylon, Moab, Damascus, Egypt and Tyre
(Isaiah 13:1; 15:1; 17:1; 19:1; 23:1). Ezekiel and Jeremiah lay out whole chapters of
them. Readers have taken these oracles in three ways. Some say they were all fulfilled
long ago and are now history. Some say they are all pictures of the church and its
enemies. Some say they are all still to come. The volume asks a question that the
three answers skip: which of these oracles does Scripture itself report as fulfilled,
which does it hold open, and what does each say it rests on?
The position is this. The oracles against the nations are not one prophecy on one
timeline. Each carries its own ground, which is what that nation did; its own term,
which is how long or how soon; and its own ending, which is what the text says
becomes of it. The text sorts them into three classes. In the closed class the
sentence is carried out and no restoration follows. In the part-fulfilled class the text
itself reports what happened and what remains. In the open class a clause of
restoration is attached that no later passage reports as fulfilled, and the volume holds
those clauses open: the nations are blessed through Israel’s restored line in the
Kingdom, and not through the Body of Christ or through the church.
Three things about the method. First, the volume reads the King James Version only,
and every Scripture quotation is checked word for word against it. Second, each
chapter opens with a claim, reads its passages in order, and closes with a short table
and a list of what the volume takes from the passage, so that the reader can see what
has been argued and test it. Third, where modern writers are named, they are named
for a position the author has read in a free text, and where an objection is stated, it is
stated as the objector would state it and then answered from the verses.
The volume does not take up what its neighbours argue. Babylon, with Revelation 17
and 18, is read in Prophetic Commentary Volume 3, and Edom, with Isaiah 63, in
Prophetic Commentary Volume 5; this volume cites them and does not repeat them.
The conditional word of Jeremiah 18:7–10 is argued in the first volume of this series,
and the sins of Israel’s worship in the second. The day of the LORD is read in Study 7,
the court of the living nations of Matthew 25 in Prophecy & Mystery Volume 5, and the
Abrahamic promise of Genesis 12:3 in Covenantal Volume 1. The volume also leaves
aside the passages sometimes taken to speak of an unseen being behind the kings of
Babylon and Tyre (Isaiah 14:12–15; Ezekiel 28:11–19). It reads what each prophet
says to the king he addresses, and names no further figure. Finally, it builds no

doctrine for the Body of Christ from the prophets’ oracles. They were spoken to
nations about nations, with Israel at the centre, and the volume keeps them there.

A reader may test the thesis in one move. Take any oracle against a nation and ask
four questions of it in order: against whom is it spoken, what is the ground, what is
the term, and what ending does the text give? If every oracle ends in the same way, or
if every oracle carries the same ground, the thesis fails. If the oracles instead differ,
and the differences are in the text and not in the reader’s theory, the thesis stands,
and the reader will see why the volume refuses to read all of them as fulfilled, all of
them as pictures, or all of them as future.

1. What an Oracle Against a Nation Is
Claim: an oracle against a nation is a word of the LORD to a named nation, with a
ground, a term and an ending, spoken by a prophet who is set over the nations as well
as over Israel. Because the LORD himself says that a word against a nation can be
turned by the nation’s turning, the first thing to ask of any oracle is not whether it

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 3

came true but what it says it rests on and what it promises. Scripture reports
fulfilment in four ways, and the volume reads each oracle by the way Scripture itself
reports it.
1.1 A prophet to the nations
The prophets’ words about foreign peoples are not an afterthought to their words
about Israel. Jeremiah is told at the start, “Before I formed thee in the belly I knew
thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee, and I ordained
thee a prophet unto the nations” (Jeremiah 1:5). The words that follow are a
commission, and its two halves are a list of verbs: “to root out, and to pull down, and
to destroy, and to throw down, to build, and to plant” (Jeremiah 1:10). Four verbs of
ending and two of beginning. The commission is over “the nations and over the
kingdoms,” and it reaches Jeremiah’s own people only as part of that sweep. The
book that carries his name bears it out, with a long run of oracles “against the
Gentiles” in chapters 46 to 51 (Jeremiah 46:1).
Amos does the same from another direction. His first sentence of the LORD’s own
voice is, “The LORD will roar from Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem” (Amos
1:2), and what follows is a circuit around Israel’s neighbours before the prophet turns
inward. Isaiah sets the burdens of Babylon, Moab, Damascus, Egypt and Tyre in the
middle of his book (Isaiah 13:1; 15:1; 17:1; 19:1; 23:1). In each of the three, the oracles
against the nations stand close to the oracles against Israel and Judah, and the
volume will keep reading them together.
1.2 The cup passes in order
Jeremiah 25 is the chapter that gives the oracles their frame. The prophet is told,
“Take the wine cup of this fury at my hand, and cause all the nations, to whom I send
thee, to drink it” (Jeremiah 25:15). The effect is stated at once: “And they shall drink,
and be moved, and be mad, because of the sword that I will send among them”
(Jeremiah 25:16). Jeremiah takes the cup and makes the nations drink, and the list
that follows has an order. “To wit, Jerusalem, and the cities of Judah, and the kings
thereof, and the princes thereof, to make them a desolation, an astonishment, an
hissing, and a curse; as it is this day” (Jeremiah 25:18). Jerusalem is first, and the
words “as it is this day” say that its drinking has already begun when the prophet
speaks. Then “Pharaoh king of Egypt” (25:19), the Philistine cities, “Edom, and Moab,
and the children of Ammon” (25:21), “Tyrus” and “Zidon” (25:22), Arabia and the east,
Elam and the Medes, and “all the kingdoms of the world, which are upon the face of
the earth: and the king of Sheshach shall drink after them” (25:26).
Two things in the passage will matter throughout. The first is the refusal clause: “And
it shall be, if they refuse to take the cup at thine hand to drink, then shalt thou say unto
them, Thus saith the LORD of hosts; Ye shall certainly drink” (Jeremiah 25:28). The
cup is not offered for the nations’ consent. The second is the reason given for
beginning where it began: “For, lo, I begin to bring evil on the city which is called by my
name, and should ye be utterly unpunished? Ye shall not be unpunished” (Jeremiah
25:29). H. G. L. Peels reads the whole collection in this light and puts the point in one
sentence: “the judgment on the nations is directly related to the judgment on Israel,
which has to drink the cup first” (European Journal of Theology 16:2, 2007). The
oracles against the nations are therefore not Israel’s revenge, and they are not a

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 4

separate book about strangers. They run alongside the judgment on Israel, and they
have Israel at the centre.
1.3 A word that can be turned
The first volume in this series read Jeremiah 18:7–10 in full, and its result is used
here. The LORD says: “At what instant I shall speak concerning a nation, and
concerning a kingdom, to pluck up, and to pull down, and to destroy it; if that nation,
against whom I have pronounced, turn from their evil, I will repent of the evil that I
thought to do unto them” (Jeremiah 18:7, 8). The same rule runs the other way: “And
at what instant I shall speak concerning a nation, and concerning a kingdom, to build
and to plant it; if it do evil in my sight, that it obey not my voice, then I will repent of the
good, wherewith I said I would benefit them” (Jeremiah 18:9, 10). The rule is stated of
“a nation” and “a kingdom,” and not of Israel only. Every oracle against a nation is
therefore a word that stands under this rule.
The rule does not make the oracles unreliable. It makes the first question of any
oracle a question about the nation: did it turn? When Nineveh turned, the LORD
“repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not”
(Jonah 3:10), and chapter 3 of this volume reads that case. When a nation did not
turn, the word stands as spoken. The volume reads each oracle by asking whether the
text reports a turning, a refusal or nothing at all.
1.4 How Scripture reports fulfilment
Scripture does not leave the reader to match oracles to history by guesswork. It
reports fulfilment in four ways, and the difference between them is the sorting the
volume uses.
A stated term. Some oracles carry their own clock. The nations “shall serve the king
of Babylon seventy years” (Jeremiah 25:11), and “when seventy years are
accomplished,” the LORD will punish the king of Babylon (Jeremiah 25:12). Of Moab,
“Within three years, as the years of an hireling, and the glory of Moab shall be
contemned” (Isaiah 16:14). Tyre “shall be forgotten seventy years, according to the
days of one king” (Isaiah 23:15). A term sets the test: the reader is told when to look.
A narrative that reports the event. In the historical books the event is told as event.
“And it came to pass that night, that the angel of the LORD went out, and smote in the
camp of the Assyrians an hundred fourscore and five thousand” (2 Kings 19:35); the
king of Assyria then “departed, and went and returned, and dwelt at Nineveh” (2 Kings
19:36), and was slain in the house of his god by his sons (2 Kings 19:37). The book of
Jonah does the same for Nineveh’s turning (Jonah 3:5, 10).
The prophet’s own later word. Ezekiel is the clearest example. In the twenty-seventh
year he is told that Nebuchadrezzar “caused his army to serve a great service against
Tyrus: every head was made bald, and every shoulder was peeled: yet had he no
wages, nor his army, for Tyrus, for the service that he had served against it” (Ezekiel
29:18). Then comes the substitution: “Behold, I will give the land of Egypt unto
Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon; and he shall take her multitude, and take her spoil,
and take her prey; and it shall be the wages for his army” (Ezekiel 29:19). The prophet
is reporting, within the book, what the siege did and did not yield and what the LORD
gives in its place. That is the text updating its own account.

When Jeremiah says of Moab, “Yet will I bring again the captivity of Moab in the latter

what ending does the text give: a closed sentence, a part-fulfilment that the text itself
marks, or a clause of restoration that stays open? The next chapters take the oracles
in groups and put the questions to each.

1.6 What the volume takes from the passages
• Jeremiah is ordained “a prophet unto the nations” (Jeremiah 1:5), and the
commission to root out and to build is over “the nations and over the kingdoms”
(Jeremiah 1:10).
• The cup of Jeremiah 25 passes in order, with Jerusalem first and “as it is this
day,” and the nations are told, “Ye shall certainly drink” (Jeremiah 25:18, 28).
• The rule of Jeremiah 18:7–10 is stated of nations and kingdoms, so the first
question of any oracle is what the nation did with it.
• Scripture reports fulfilment by a stated term, by a narrative of the event, by the
prophet’s own later word, or not at all.
• The volume puts four questions to each oracle: against whom, for what ground,
for what term, and with what ending.

2. The Grounds the Nations Are Charged With
Claim: the oracles against the nations give reasons, and the reasons are of a few
kinds that recur from prophet to prophet: violence done to the helpless, betrayal of
those bound to the nation by kinship or covenant, gloating over Israel’s fall, selfexaltation, and idols. The charges are brought against nations that were never given

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 6

Israel’s Law, and not one names a statute of Sinai. They name what the nations did to
other people and what they said of themselves. That is the first fact about the oracles,
and it is the one that makes the rest of the volume possible: the LORD judges the
nations on a ground he states.
2.1 Violence done to the helpless
Amos 1:3 to 2:3 is the plainest list. The formula is the same six times: “For three
transgressions of Damascus, and for four, I will not turn away the punishment
thereof,” and then a reason introduced by “because.” Damascus: “because they have
threshed Gilead with threshing instruments of iron” (Amos 1:3). Ammon: “because
they have ripped up the women with child of Gilead, that they might enlarge their
border” (Amos 1:13). Moab: “because he burned the bones of the king of Edom into
lime” (Amos 2:1). The three charges have one shape. A people with an army did to a
people without one what the strong may do to the weak, and the last of the three did it
to the dead. The reason Ammon gave was “that they might enlarge their border,”
which names a nation’s ordinary ambition as the thing charged.
Habakkuk’s taunt has the same ground in a different voice. “Shall not all these take up
a parable against him, and a taunting proverb against him, and say, Woe to him that
increaseth that which is not his! how long?” (Habakkuk 2:6). The proud man “hast
spoiled many nations” and the sentence follows from it: “all the remnant of the people
shall spoil thee; because of men’s blood, and for the violence of the land, of the city,
and of all that dwell therein” (Habakkuk 2:8). The refrain returns at the end of the
stanzas: “For the violence of Lebanon shall cover thee, and the spoil of beasts, which
made them afraid, because of men’s blood” (Habakkuk 2:17). The woes name a town
built with blood (2:12), a neighbour made drunk so that his nakedness may be looked
on (2:15), and the greed that sets its nest on high (2:9). The ground is what the
conqueror did to those he conquered.
2.2 Betrayal of the bound
Two of Amos’s six charges are against nations who handed over people to whom
they owed something. Gaza: “because they carried away captive the whole captivity,
to deliver them up to Edom” (Amos 1:6). Tyrus: “because they delivered up the whole
captivity to Edom, and remembered not the brotherly covenant” (Amos 1:9). The
phrase “the brotherly covenant” is the only place in the series of six that names a
covenant, and it is a covenant between Tyre and a people it had sold. Edom’s own
charge is of the same kind turned on a brother: “because he did pursue his brother
with the sword, and did cast off all pity, and his anger did tear perpetually, and he kept
his wrath for ever” (Amos 1:11). Obadiah gives the same charge at length: “For thy
violence against thy brother Jacob shame shall cover thee, and thou shalt be cut off
for ever” (Obadiah 10).
Joel names the trade in persons beside the nations he names. The LORD says he will
gather all nations to the valley of Jehoshaphat and plead with them “for my people
and for my heritage Israel, whom they have scattered among the nations, and parted
my land” (Joel 3:2). He charges Tyre and Zidon and the coasts of Palestine, “The
children also of Judah and the children of Jerusalem have ye sold unto the Grecians,
that ye might remove them far from their border” (Joel 3:6), and the sentence is exact:
“Behold, I will raise them out of the place whither ye have sold them, and will return

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 7

your recompence upon your own head” (Joel 3:7). The ground of the charge is the
sale. The recompence is the reversal of the sale.
2.3 Gloating over Israel’s fall
Ezekiel 25 is the chapter of the gloaters, and it states the charge in the speakers’ own
words. Ammon said, “Aha, against my sanctuary, when it was profaned; and against
the land of Israel, when it was desolate; and against the house of Judah, when they
went into captivity” (Ezekiel 25:3). The LORD adds, “Because thou hast clapped thine
hands, and stamped with the feet, and rejoiced in heart with all thy despite against the
land of Israel” (Ezekiel 25:6). Moab and Seir said, “Behold, the house of Judah is like
unto all the heathen” (Ezekiel 25:8). Edom “hath dealt against the house of Judah by
taking vengeance” (Ezekiel 25:12). The Philistines “have dealt by revenge, and have
taken vengeance with a despiteful heart, to destroy it for the old hatred” (Ezekiel
25:15). Tyrus says the same word as Ammon, in its own interest: “Aha, she is broken
that was the gates of the people: she is turned unto me: I shall be replenished, now
she is laid waste” (Ezekiel 26:2). Zephaniah collects the speech of Moab and Ammon
in the LORD’s words: “I have heard the reproach of Moab, and the revilings of the
children of Ammon, whereby they have reproached my people, and magnified
themselves against their border” (Zephaniah 2:8).
Obadiah’s charge against Edom is the most detailed. It names the standing apart, “In
the day that thou stoodest on the other side” (Obadiah 11), and says “even thou wast
as one of them.” It names what Edom ought not to have done: not to have looked on
the day of his brother, not to have “rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of
their destruction,” not to have “spoken proudly in the day of distress” (Obadiah 12).
Not to have entered the gate, not to have laid hands on the substance, and not to have
“stood in the crossway, to cut off those of his that did escape” (Obadiah 13, 14). Most
of what Obadiah lists is not what Edom did with a sword. It is what Edom did while
another was down. The volume leaves Edom there. The full treatment of Edom
belongs to the volume that reads Isaiah 63 (Prophetic Commentary Volume 5).
2.4 Pride and the claim to be more than a man
The second large ground is a claim. Moab’s is called pride in two prophets in nearly
the same words. “We have heard the pride of Moab, (he is exceeding proud) his
loftiness, and his arrogancy, and his pride, and the haughtiness of his heart”
(Jeremiah 48:29), and “We have heard of the pride of Moab; he is very proud” (Isaiah
16:6). Jeremiah adds the reason for the fall: “Because thou hast trusted in thy works
and in thy treasures, thou shalt also be taken” (Jeremiah 48:7), and “Moab shall be
destroyed from being a people, because he hath magnified himself against the LORD”
(Jeremiah 48:42). Edom’s is the same: “The pride of thine heart hath deceived thee,
thou that dwellest in the clefts of the rock, whose habitation is high” (Obadiah 3). The
sentence against it is stated in the figure of the claim: “Though thou exalt thyself as
the eagle, and though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down,
saith the LORD” (Obadiah 4).
Two kings go further than pride. The prince of Tyrus is told, “Because thine heart is
lifted up, and thou hast said, I am a God, I sit in the seat of God, in the midst of the
seas; yet thou art a man, and not God, though thou set thine heart as the heart of God”
(Ezekiel 28:2). The LORD’s answer is to a man, and says so: “Wilt thou yet say before
him that slayeth thee, I am God? but thou shalt be a man, and no God, in the hand of

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 8

him that slayeth thee” (Ezekiel 28:9). Pharaoh says of the Nile, “My river is mine own,
and I have made it for myself” (Ezekiel 29:3), and Nineveh’s city says in its heart, “I
am, and there is none beside me” (Zephaniah 2:15). The volume reads each as the
claim of a king or a city to the place that belongs to the LORD. These verses are
addressed to the prince of Tyrus and to Pharaoh. The volume does not carry them
past the men they address.
2.5 Idols and the false helper
A fifth ground runs through the others: the nation’s god. Habakkuk closes his taunt on
it. “What profiteth the graven image that the maker thereof hath graven it; the molten
image, and a teacher of lies, that the maker of his work trusteth therein, to make
dumb idols?” (Habakkuk 2:18). The final sentence of the chapter sets the one against
the other: “But the LORD is in his holy temple: let all the earth keep silence before him”
(Habakkuk 2:20). Nahum has the LORD say to Nineveh, “out of the house of thy gods
will I cut off the graven image and the molten image” (Nahum 1:14). Jeremiah says of
Moab, “Chemosh shall go forth into captivity with his priests and his princes together”
(Jeremiah 48:7), and of Egypt that the LORD will punish “Pharaoh, and Egypt, with
their gods, and their kings” (Jeremiah 46:25). The ground is not that the nations had
gods. It is that they trusted them, and that the gods were made.
2.6 One observation on the grounds, and one text that widens them
The grounds in these oracles are the sort of thing the nations could know without
Sinai: do not tear open the pregnant, do not sell captives to a brother’s enemy, do not
exult over a brother’s ruin, do not call yourself a god. Amos shows the contrast in his
own book. After the six nations he turns to Judah at 2:4, and there the charge is of the
LORD’s own law, a ground the nations are not given. The volume draws one
observation from this and no doctrine: the nations are charged with what they did to
other people and what they said of themselves, and Israel and Judah are charged with
what they did to the LORD’s own words. The same prophet who says it also says
something wider.
“Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel? saith the LORD.
Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt? and the Philistines from
Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?” (Amos 9:7). The LORD says he has acted in the
history of other peoples as he acted in Israel’s. The sentence does not make Israel
like the nations in every respect, for the next verse says “saving that I will not utterly
destroy the house of Jacob” (Amos 9:8). It does mean that the oracles are not a
quarrel between a god and strangers. The LORD claims the Philistines’ and the
Syrians’ own movements as his work, and the oracles judge them as a judge judges
the people he has dealt with.

2.7 What the volume takes from the passages
• Every oracle in the series gives a ground, and the same few grounds recur across
prophets and centuries.
• The grounds are violence to the helpless, betrayal of the bound, gloating over
Israel’s fall, pride and the claim to be more than a man, and idols.
• None of the grounds against the nations names a statute of Sinai; Amos charges
Judah, at 2:4, with the LORD’s own law, and the nations with what they did and
said.
• Amos 9:7 states that the LORD has dealt with other peoples’ histories, which is
why the oracles judge them as he does.
• The prince of Tyrus and Pharaoh are charged in what they said as men; the
volume carries the charge no further than the men addressed.

3. Nineveh: A Sentence Suspended and Then
Executed
Claim: Nineveh is the clearest case of a closed oracle, and it is closed in two steps
that the text itself separates. The first word against the city carried a number, forty
days, and the city turned, and the LORD did not do what he had said. The second
word, Nahum’s, comes against the same city with no number and no condition, and
carries no clause of restoration. The volume reads the two words side by side. They
are not a contradiction. The first is the rule of Jeremiah 18:8 at work. The second is
what stands when the word comes again and the text records no turning.
3.1 Forty days
The word came to Jonah “the second time,” and the commission was, “Arise, go unto
Nineveh, that great city, and preach unto it the preaching that I bid thee” (Jonah 3:1,
2). He entered the city and cried, “Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown”
(Jonah 3:4). The sentence is brief and has a term. It has no condition in it: nothing in
the words tells the city what to do. The people knew what to do all the same. “So the
people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from
the greatest of them even to the least of them” (Jonah 3:5). The king rose from his
throne, laid aside his robe, and covered himself with sackcloth. His decree reached
the animals and the men, and it named the ground of the sentence in the people’s

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 10

own words: “let them turn every one from his evil way, and from the violence that is in
their hands” (Jonah 3:8).
The king’s last question is the one the whole chapter hangs on: “Who can tell if God
will turn and repent, and turn away from his fierce anger, that we perish not?” (Jonah
3:9). He does not know. He acts anyway, and the text reports what followed. “And God
saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that
he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not” (Jonah 3:10). The prophet’s
sentence had not named a condition, and the LORD did not carry it out, because the
city turned. The rule of Jeremiah 18:7, 8 is stated in the same words, “if that nation,
against whom I have pronounced, turn from their evil, I will repent of the evil.” Jonah 3
is that rule shown in a case.
The book closes with the LORD’s question to the prophet who was angry about it, and
the question speaks to the city’s worth. “And should not I spare Nineveh, that great
city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between
their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?” (Jonah 4:11). The LORD’s
concern is stated as concern for persons and cattle. The volume reads this verse as
the LORD’s own account of why the sentence was lifted, and as a reason the lifting
was not weak.
3.2 The burden of Nineveh
The book of Nahum opens with a heading, “The burden of Nineveh. The book of the
vision of Nahum the Elkoshite” (Nahum 1:1), and the heading gives the oracle its
target. The book gives no date, and the volume does not need one. What matters is
what the word says. It begins with the LORD’s character, in two steps that sit side by
side: “God is jealous, and the LORD revengeth; the LORD revengeth, and is furious”
(Nahum 1:2), and then, “The LORD is slow to anger, and great in power, and will not at
all acquit the wicked” (Nahum 1:3). The two statements stand side by side: “slow to
anger” and “will not at all acquit the wicked.” Slowness is not acquittal. The same
passage carries a word for those inside the storm: “The LORD is good, a strong hold
in the day of trouble; and he knoweth them that trust in him” (Nahum 1:7). The oracle
against the city sits inside a statement about whom the LORD knows.
The sentence is stated in finished terms. “But with an overrunning flood he will make
an utter end of the place thereof, and darkness shall pursue his enemies” (Nahum
1:8). The ground is named: “There is one come out of thee, that imagineth evil against
the LORD, a wicked counsellor” (Nahum 1:11). And the ending: “And the LORD hath
given a commandment concerning thee, that no more of thy name be sown: out of the
house of thy gods will I cut off the graven image and the molten image: I will make thy
grave; for thou art vile” (Nahum 1:14). “No more of thy name be sown” is a statement
about posterity, not a figure of speech for a bad year. It is the opposite of the clauses
of return that close the oracles in later chapters.
Between the sentence and the ending the text turns to Judah and says what the fall
means for her. “Though I have afflicted thee, I will afflict thee no more” (Nahum 1:12),
and, “For now will I break his yoke from off thee” (Nahum 1:13). Verse 15 names
Judah: “O Judah, keep thy solemn feasts, perform thy vows: for the wicked shall no
more pass through thee; he is utterly cut off.” The oracle is therefore also good news,
and for whom is plain. The destruction of the city is the end of an oppression that fell
on Judah, and the book says so.

3.3 The bloody city
The third chapter returns to the ground, in a woe. “Woe to the bloody city! it is all full
of lies and robbery; the prey departeth not” (Nahum 3:1). The sound of battle fills the
next verses, with the whip, the wheels, the chariots, the sword and the spear and “a
multitude of slain” (Nahum 3:2, 3). The charge widens in verse 4 to a woman who
trades in nations: “Because of the multitude of the whoredoms of the wellfavoured
harlot, the mistress of witchcrafts, that selleth nations through her whoredoms, and
families through her witchcrafts.” The image of the harlot is Nahum’s own for
Nineveh. Prophetic Commentary Volume 3 takes the same image up in another
connection, as a picture for the woman of Revelation 17. This volume leaves that use
there and reads the image in Nahum for what Nahum says: Nineveh bought and sold
peoples.
Then the LORD’s own sentence: “Behold, I am against thee, saith the LORD of hosts;
and I will discover thy skirts upon thy face, and I will shew the nations thy nakedness,
and the kingdoms thy shame” (Nahum 3:5). The city’s fall is made public, and the
response of the onlookers is given in advance: “all they that look upon thee shall flee
from thee, and say, Nineveh is laid waste: who will bemoan her? whence shall I seek
comforters for thee?” (Nahum 3:7). The last two verses of the book address the king.
“Thy shepherds slumber, O king of Assyria: thy nobles shall dwell in the dust” (Nahum
3:18), and, “There is no healing of thy bruise; thy wound is grievous” (Nahum 3:19).
The book closes on a question that answers the closing question of Jonah from the
other side: “for upon whom hath not thy wickedness passed continually?” Jonah ends
on the LORD’s pity for the city. Nahum ends on the whole world’s knowledge of what
the city had done.
3.4 Zephaniah
Zephaniah adds a short and complete statement. “And he will stretch out his hand
against the north, and destroy Assyria; and will make Nineveh a desolation, and dry
like a wilderness” (Zephaniah 2:13). The city is named a second time at the close of
the oracle, and the voice is again of a place that spoke of itself: “This is the rejoicing
city that dwelt carelessly, that said in her heart, I am, and there is none beside me:
how is she become a desolation, a place for beasts to lie down in! every one that
passeth by her shall hiss, and wag his hand” (Zephaniah 2:15). The ground is named
in the quotation of her own words, which is pride, and the ending is a desolation
where beasts lie down. No restoration clause follows in Zephaniah, as none follows in
Nahum.
3.5 Why the volume calls Nineveh closed
The volume calls Nineveh a closed oracle for two reasons that rest on the text. The
sentences of Nahum and Zephaniah are final in their own terms: “an utter end,” “no
more of thy name be sown,” “I will make thy grave,” “no healing.” And none of the
three prophets who speak of the city attaches a clause of return to it. Compare the
oracles of the next chapter, where the sentence on Moab, Ammon and Elam is
followed by an explicit “I will bring again the captivity.” Nineveh has nothing like it.
One honest limit should be stated. Scripture does not narrate the fall of Nineveh.
What the Bible reports is the earlier reversal, at Jerusalem, of the king of Assyria, who
“departed, and went and returned, and dwelt at Nineveh” (2 Kings 19:36), and who
was killed in the house of his god by his sons (2 Kings 19:37). The end of the city is

known to the author from outside the Bible, where ancient records and modern
historians place it in the seventh century before Christ. The volume does not rest its
classification on those records. It rests it on what Nahum and Zephaniah say and on
the absence of a restoration clause. A reader who wants the historical witness will
find it in any history of Assyria, and the volume cites none.
A second limit is a connection to a later chapter. Isaiah 19:25 names Assyria among
the nations blessed in a coming day: “Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the
work of my hands, and Israel mine inheritance.” The volume reads this as different
from the sentence on the city and its king. Nahum and Zephaniah speak to Nineveh
and to the king of Assyria, and Isaiah 19:23–25 speaks of Assyria in a day when Egypt
and Assyria serve together with Israel. The two sets of texts name different subjects,
a city and its king in one and a nation in the other, and the volume keeps them apart,
reading Isaiah’s in chapter 5 and chapter 8. A reader who sees them as one subject
has a difficulty that the volume names and does not conceal.

4. Damascus, Philistia, Moab, Ammon and Elam
Claim: the nations nearest to Israel received sentences with their own terms and
endings, and the text sorts them. Damascus has a sentence and no clause of return.
The Philistines have a sentence, a remnant given to the LORD, and a coast given to
Judah. Moab, Ammon and Elam each have a sentence and, in Jeremiah, a clause that
brings the captivity again “in the latter days.” In the cases of Moab and Ammon
another prophet states the sentence in terms that seem to leave no room for the
clause. The volume puts the statements side by side as written and does not smooth
them into a timeline the text does not give.
4.1 Damascus
Three prophets speak to Damascus. Amos charges it with the threshing of Gilead and
sentences it to fire, the breaking of its bar, and exile: “the people of Syria shall go into
captivity unto Kir, saith the LORD” (Amos 1:5). Isaiah’s burden is shorter: “Behold,
Damascus is taken away from being a city, and it shall be a ruinous heap” (Isaiah
17:1). He joins it to Ephraim: “The fortress also shall cease from Ephraim, and the
kingdom from Damascus, and the remnant of Syria” (Isaiah 17:3). Jeremiah’s lines
are in the voice of rumour reaching a city: “Damascus is waxed feeble, and turneth
herself to flee, and fear hath seized on her” (Jeremiah 49:24), and “I will kindle a fire in
the wall of Damascus, and it shall consume the palaces of Ben-hadad” (Jeremiah
49:27).
No clause of return is attached to Damascus in any of the three. The volume therefore
reads the sentence on Damascus as closed in its own terms. One later text must be
named. Zechariah 9:1 opens a burden in the land of Hadrach and says “Damascus
shall be the rest thereof.” The verse names the city again, in a word that moves down
the coast (Tyrus, Zidon, Ashkelon, Gaza, Ekron, Ashdod) and ends in Judah’s
deliverance (Zechariah 9:1–8). The volume reads Zechariah 9 with Tyre in chapter 6
and does not decide here whether the naming of Damascus is a further stroke or only
the place from which the word starts. It reports the verse and does not build on it.
4.2 Philistia
Amos charges Gaza with the sale of a whole captivity to Edom and promises fire on
the wall of Gaza, the cutting off of the inhabitant from Ashdod and the sceptre from
Ashkelon, and the LORD’s hand turned “against Ekron: and the remnant of the
Philistines shall perish” (Amos 1:6–8). Jeremiah’s oracle has a date inside it, “before
that Pharaoh smote Gaza” (Jeremiah 47:1), and the figure is of waters: “waters rise up
out of the north, and shall be an overflowing flood” (Jeremiah 47:2). The sword of the
LORD is addressed, “how long will it be ere thou be quiet?” and it answers itself, “How
can it be quiet, seeing the LORD hath given it a charge against Ashkelon, and against
the sea shore? there hath he appointed it” (Jeremiah 47:6, 7). Zephaniah says, “Gaza
shall be forsaken, and Ashkelon a desolation: they shall drive out Ashdod at the noon
day, and Ekron shall be rooted up” (Zephaniah 2:4).
Here the oracle begins to carry a clause. The sentence goes on, “O Canaan, the land
of the Philistines, I will even destroy thee, that there shall be no inhabitant” (Zephaniah
2:5). Then: “And the coast shall be for the remnant of the house of Judah; they shall
feed thereupon: in the houses of Ashkelon shall they lie down in the evening: for the
LORD their God shall visit them, and turn away their captivity” (Zephaniah 2:7). The

clause that restores belongs to Judah, and it is Judah’s captivity that is turned.
Zechariah adds a different note. After the fall of Ashkelon, Gaza and Ekron, “he that
remaineth, even he, shall be for our God, and he shall be as a governor in Judah, and
Ekron as a Jebusite” (Zechariah 9:7). A remnant of the Philistines is taken in to the
LORD’s people. The volume reads the coast given to Judah as a sentence on Philistia
that closes, and the remnant of Zechariah 9:7 as a clause that no later passage
reports done, and so open.
4.3 Moab
Moab has the longest run. Isaiah 15 is a lamentation: “Because in the night Ar of
Moab is laid waste, and brought to silence” (Isaiah 15:1). The prophet weeps for the
cities (15:2–8) and the pitch of the grief is stated, “My heart shall cry out for Moab”
(Isaiah 15:5). Chapter 16 holds the clause that reaches furthest: after the call to send
the lamb to “the mount of the daughter of Zion” (Isaiah 16:1) and to hide the outcasts,
it says, “And in mercy shall the throne be established: and he shall sit upon it in truth
in the tabernacle of David, judging, and seeking judgment, and hasting righteousness”
(Isaiah 16:5). A throne in the tabernacle of David appears in the middle of the burden
of Moab. The next verses return to the sentence: “We have heard of the pride of
Moab” (Isaiah 16:6).
Isaiah 16 closes on a statement about its own layers. “This is the word that the LORD
hath spoken concerning Moab since that time. But now the LORD hath spoken, saying,
Within three years, as the years of an hireling, and the glory of Moab shall be
contemned, with all that great multitude; and the remnant shall be very small and
feeble” (Isaiah 16:13, 14). The text separates an older word from a new one and
attaches a term to the new, “within three years.” It is one of the oracles that carry a
clock. Jeremiah 48 takes up Moab at length: “Woe unto Nebo! for it is spoiled”
(Jeremiah 48:1), the ground (“trusted in thy works and in thy treasures,” 48:7), the
figure of Moab at ease, who “hath settled on his lees, and hath not been emptied from
vessel to vessel” (48:11), and the end, “Moab shall be destroyed from being a people,
because he hath magnified himself against the LORD” (48:42). Then, at the close of
the chapter: “Yet will I bring again the captivity of Moab in the latter days, saith the
LORD. Thus far is the judgment of Moab” (Jeremiah 48:47).
Zephaniah states the end of Moab in terms that sound like the opposite. “Surely Moab
shall be as Sodom, and the children of Ammon as Gomorrah, even the breeding of
nettles, and saltpits, and a perpetual desolation: the residue of my people shall spoil
them, and the remnant of my people shall possess them” (Zephaniah 2:9). Both
statements are in the LORD’s voice, and the volume does not say which governs. It
reports that Zephaniah 2:9 says the land is a perpetual desolation possessed by the
remnant of Israel, and Jeremiah 48:47 says the captivity of Moab is brought again in
the latter days. No passage joins the two, and the volume joins them with no theory. It
holds Jeremiah 48:47 as a clause of restoration that no later passage reports done,
and it holds Zephaniah 2:9 beside it. The words “Thus far is the judgment of Moab”
(Jeremiah 48:47) mark where Jeremiah’s own account of the sentence stops.
4.4 Ammon
Amos charges Ammon with the ripping of the women of Gilead “that they might
enlarge their border” (Amos 1:13). Jeremiah 49 begins with a question about land:
“Hath Israel no sons? hath he no heir? why then doth their king inherit Gad, and his

people dwell in his cities?” (Jeremiah 49:1). The sentence is that Rabbah “shall be a
desolate heap, and her daughters shall be burned with fire: then shall Israel be heir
unto them that were his heirs, saith the LORD” (Jeremiah 49:2). Jeremiah gives the
ground, “O backsliding daughter? that trusted in her treasures, saying, Who shall come
unto me?” (Jeremiah 49:4), and then the clause: “And afterward I will bring again the
captivity of the children of Ammon, saith the LORD” (Jeremiah 49:6).
Ezekiel says a thing that sits uneasily beside it. The LORD will deliver Ammon “to the
men of the east for a possession” and “I will make Rabbah a stable for camels, and
the Ammonites a couching place for flocks” (Ezekiel 25:4, 5). The sentence continues,
“Unto the men of the east with the Ammonites, and will give them in possession, that
the Ammonites may not be remembered among the nations” (Ezekiel 25:10). The
words “that the Ammonites may not be remembered among the nations” and “I will
bring again the captivity” come from two prophets, and neither prophet qualifies the
other. The volume reads them as two statements about Ammon in the LORD’s voice.
It does not claim to know what the second does to the first, and the clause of
Jeremiah 49:6 remains one that no later passage reports done.
4.5 Elam
Elam appears once at length. The word came to Jeremiah “in the beginning of the
reign of Zedekiah king of Judah” (Jeremiah 49:34). The LORD will “break the bow of
Elam, the chief of their might” (Jeremiah 49:35), will bring “the four winds from the
four quarters of heaven” and scatter them so that “there shall be no nation whither the
outcasts of Elam shall not come” (Jeremiah 49:36), and will say, “I will set my throne
in Elam, and will destroy from thence the king and the princes” (Jeremiah 49:38). The
last verse, and the last verse of the oracle, is the clause: “But it shall come to pass in
the latter days, that I will bring again the captivity of Elam, saith the LORD” (Jeremiah
49:39). Elam is named in the cup list of Jeremiah 25:25, and its one oracle has a
sentence, a scattering and a return.
4.6 “In the latter days”
The three clauses of Jeremiah (48:47; 49:6; 49:39) share a phrase for the time of the
return, and the Authorized Version translates the same Hebrew phrase by two English
forms, “last days” (Isaiah 2:2) and “latter days.” The volume notes the phrase and
draws no date from it. It does mean that the clause is not stated as “afterward” in the
sense of next year. The word for Ammon is “afterward” (Jeremiah 49:6), for Moab and
Elam it is “in the latter days.” The text sets Moab and Elam on a longer horizon than
the sentence itself.
Peels, writing on the whole collection of Jeremiah 46 to 51, counts these clauses and
uses them against the thought that the oracles were hidden comfort for Israel: “four
times in Jeremiah 46-51 a word of welfare is added to a word of judgment on a
foreign nation” (European Journal of Theology 16:2, 2007). He lists them as Jeremiah
46:26, 48:47, 49:6 and 49:39. The count helps the volume’s sorting in one respect. The
clauses are few, and they are placed on particular nations; the other oracles of
Jeremiah 46 to 51 have none. A nation with a clause is a different case from a nation
without one.

5. Egypt
Claim: Egypt has more oracles than any other nation, in three prophets, and they do
not say one thing. Isaiah 19 and 20 give a sentence of civil strife, a hard master, a
dried river and a captivity, and then, after the words “in that day,” a blessing in which

Egypt is called “my people.” Jeremiah 46 gives the Babylonian stroke and adds,
“afterward it shall be inhabited.” Ezekiel 29 to 32 gives a desolation of forty years, a
return, a base kingdom, and, in one dated verse, a correction of the first account of
what Nebuchadrezzar would receive. The text itself marks which parts have been
carried out and which have not. The volume classes Egypt as part-fulfilled, with three
clauses held open: Isaiah 19:18–25, Jeremiah 46:26 and Ezekiel 29:13–15.
5.1 The burden of Egypt
Isaiah’s oracle opens with the LORD’s coming: “Behold, the LORD rideth upon a swift
cloud, and shall come into Egypt: and the idols of Egypt shall be moved at his
presence, and the heart of Egypt shall melt in the midst of it” (Isaiah 19:1). The first
sentence is on the gods and on the people’s courage. Then the strife: “And I will set
the Egyptians against the Egyptians: and they shall fight every one against his brother,
and every one against his neighbour; city against city, and kingdom against kingdom”
(Isaiah 19:2). Then the master: “And the Egyptians will I give over into the hand of a
cruel lord; and a fierce king shall rule over them, saith the Lord, the LORD of hosts”
(Isaiah 19:4). Verses 5 to 10 take the river: the waters “shall fail,” the reeds wither, the
fishers mourn, and the weavers of fine flax are confounded.
The counsel of the state fails with the river. “Surely the princes of Zoan are fools, the
counsel of the wise counsellors of Pharaoh is become brutish” (Isaiah 19:11). The
cause is named and it is the LORD’s: “The LORD hath mingled a perverse spirit in the
midst thereof: and they have caused Egypt to err in every work thereof, as a drunken
man staggereth in his vomit” (Isaiah 19:14). The first half of the burden ends with the
effect on the nation: “In that day shall Egypt be like unto women: and it shall be afraid
and fear because of the shaking of the hand of the LORD of hosts” (Isaiah 19:16), and
with an unlikely cause of fear: “And the land of Judah shall be a terror unto Egypt,
every one that maketh mention thereof shall be afraid in himself, because of the
counsel of the LORD of hosts, which he hath determined against it” (Isaiah 19:17).
5.2 A sign with a date
Isaiah 20 is a short chapter that gives the burden a date and a prophet’s body. “In the
year that Tartan came unto Ashdod, (when Sargon the king of Assyria sent him,) and
fought against Ashdod, and took it” (Isaiah 20:1). In that year Isaiah was told to
loosen his sackcloth and put off his shoe, and he went “naked and barefoot” (Isaiah
20:2), and the LORD named it: “Like as my servant Isaiah hath walked naked and
barefoot three years for a sign and wonder upon Egypt and upon Ethiopia” (Isaiah
20:3). The meaning follows: “So shall the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptians
prisoners, and the Ethiopians captives, young and old” (Isaiah 20:4). The chapter
closes with the voice of the coastland that had looked to Egypt for help: “Behold, such
is our expectation, whither we flee for help to be delivered from the king of Assyria:
and how shall we escape?” (Isaiah 20:6).
The chapter is a sentence on Egypt and on those who trusted Egypt, and it is written
as a sign acted out. The Bible does not narrate the captivity it foretells. The volume
reports the sentence and the sign, and it classes the Assyrian stroke with the
sentences the text states but does not itself report finished.

and Assyria both worship the LORD, when there is a highway between them, when
they serve together, and when Israel stands with them as a third, a “blessing in the

volume does not claim to date the day. It claims only that the text says it is a day to
come, introduced by the same words the prophets use for the day of the LORD.
5.4 Jeremiah: Carchemish and what follows
Jeremiah’s oracle against Egypt opens with a date given by an event. It is “Against
Egypt, against the army of Pharaoh-necho king of Egypt, which was by the river
Euphrates in Carchemish, which Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon smote in the fourth
year of Jehoiakim the son of Josiah king of Judah” (Jeremiah 46:2). The first stroke is
therefore reported inside the heading, as a thing that had happened. The second word
is a further one: “how Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon should come and smite the
land of Egypt” (Jeremiah 46:13). The figures are of a heifer and of a flood (46:7, 8, 20),
and the voices that say what was left are of Pharaoh: “Pharaoh king of Egypt is but a
noise; he hath passed the time appointed” (Jeremiah 46:17).
The sentence is plain and it names the gods. “Behold, I will punish the multitude of
No, and Pharaoh, and Egypt, with their gods, and their kings; even Pharaoh, and all
them that trust in him” (Jeremiah 46:25). And then the clause that does what Moab’s
and Ammon’s do: “And I will deliver them into the hand of those that seek their lives,
and into the hand of Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon, and into the hand of his
servants: and afterward it shall be inhabited, as in the days of old, saith the LORD”
(Jeremiah 46:26). It stands in the same verse as the stroke. Verses 27 and 28 turn to

5.5 Ezekiel: forty years, a base kingdom, and a correction
Ezekiel’s Egypt oracles fill chapters 29 to 32. They open with Pharaoh, “the great
dragon that lieth in the midst of his rivers, which hath said, My river is mine own, and I
have made it for myself” (Ezekiel 29:3), and the charge of the weakness he showed to
Israel: “because they have been a staff of reed to the house of Israel” (Ezekiel 29:6).
The sentence names a term. The land shall be “utterly waste and desolate,” and
“neither shall it be inhabited forty years” (Ezekiel 29:10, 11); the Egyptians shall be
scattered “among the nations” (Ezekiel 29:12). Then the clause: “Yet thus saith the
Lord GOD; At the end of forty years will I gather the Egyptians from the people whither
they were scattered” (Ezekiel 29:13). “And I will bring again the captivity of Egypt, and
will cause them to return into the land of Pathros, into the land of their habitation; and
they shall be there a base kingdom” (Ezekiel 29:14). “It shall be the basest of the
kingdoms; neither shall it exalt itself any more above the nations” (Ezekiel 29:15). And
the effect on Israel: “And it shall be no more the confidence of the house of Israel”
(Ezekiel 29:16).
The clause of return is of a particular kind. It restores Egypt and it keeps Egypt low.
The volume reads the base kingdom of verse 14 and the blessing of Isaiah 19:25 as
two statements about the same nation that do not cancel each other. A nation can be
the basest of the kingdoms and still be named “my people.” The text says both, and
the volume holds both as written. Readers differ on whether the forty years of Ezekiel
29:11 and 12 are a count of years or a round number, and the volume reports the
figure as written and states no date for it.
Then comes the passage this volume has named already. The date is later than the
others: “in the seven and twentieth year, in the first month, in the first day of the
month” (Ezekiel 29:17). Nebuchadrezzar “caused his army to serve a great service
against Tyrus: every head was made bald, and every shoulder was peeled: yet had he
no wages, nor his army, for Tyrus, for the service that he had served against it”

Egypt unto Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon; and he shall take her multitude, and take
her spoil, and take her prey; and it shall be the wages for his army” (Ezekiel 29:19),
and “I have given him the land of Egypt for his labour wherewith he served against it,
because they wrought for me, saith the Lord GOD” (Ezekiel 29:20). The chapter closes
with a clause for Israel: “In that day will I cause the horn of the house of Israel to bud

with Tyre.

• Egypt is judged in three prophets, with the LORD’s coming, civil strife, a hard

• Three clauses of restoration are attached to Egypt: Jeremiah 46:26, Ezekiel

6. Tyre and Sidon
Claim: Tyre is the hardest case in the series, and the text itself explains why. Isaiah
gives it a term of seventy years and a trade that ends “holiness to the LORD.” Ezekiel
says it shall be built no more and never found again, names Nebuchadrezzar as the

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 21

first to come against it, and then, sixteen years later, reports that the siege paid him
nothing and gives him Egypt instead. Zechariah, later still, finds the city rich again and
says the LORD will cast her out. The volume reports these statements side by side as
written, adds no timeline the text does not give, and classes Tyre as part-fulfilled with
an open end. The reading does not call Ezekiel 26 a failed prediction, and it does not
call it a finished one.
6.1 Isaiah: the crowning city and the seventy years
The burden opens with a call to the ships. “The burden of Tyre. Howl, ye ships of
Tarshish; for it is laid waste, so that there is no house, no entering in” (Isaiah 23:1).
The city’s place in the world is told in the next verses: “she is a mart of nations”
(Isaiah 23:3). The question of verse 8 is who has done this: “Who hath taken this
counsel against Tyre, the crowning city, whose merchants are princes, whose
traffickers are the honourable of the earth?” The answer is the one the oracles of this
volume keep giving: “The LORD of hosts hath purposed it, to stain the pride of all
glory, and to bring into contempt all the honourable of the earth” (Isaiah 23:9). The
ground is pride in glory and honour. The LORD “stretched out his hand over the sea, he
shook the kingdoms: the LORD hath given a commandment against the merchant city,
to destroy the strong holds thereof” (Isaiah 23:11).
Then comes the term. “And it shall come to pass in that day, that Tyre shall be
forgotten seventy years, according to the days of one king: after the end of seventy
years shall Tyre sing as an harlot” (Isaiah 23:15). The seventy years are of forgetting,
not of ruin. After them: “And it shall come to pass after the end of seventy years, that
the LORD will visit Tyre, and she shall turn to her hire, and shall commit fornication
with all the kingdoms of the world upon the face of the earth” (Isaiah 23:17). The text
calls her trade fornication. The next verse is the oracle’s last: “And her merchandise
and her hire shall be holiness to the LORD: it shall not be treasured nor laid up; for her
merchandise shall be for them that dwell before the LORD, to eat sufficiently, and for
durable clothing” (Isaiah 23:18). Tyre’s trade becomes provision for the LORD’s own
people. The sentence ends with the city restored to commerce and the commerce
given a holy use.
6.2 Ezekiel 26: many nations, and one king
Ezekiel states the ground in the city’s own word, the one Ammon used: “Aha, she is
broken that was the gates of the people: she is turned unto me: I shall be replenished,
now she is laid waste” (Ezekiel 26:2). The LORD’s answer is, “Behold, I am against
thee, O Tyrus, and will cause many nations to come up against thee, as the sea
causeth his waves to come up” (Ezekiel 26:3). The figure is of waves, one after
another. Verse 4 sets out the work: “And they shall destroy the walls of Tyrus, and
break down her towers: I will also scrape her dust from her, and make her like the top
of a rock.” And verse 5: “It shall be a place for the spreading of nets in the midst of the
sea.”
Then the person changes. “For thus saith the Lord GOD; Behold, I will bring upon Tyrus
Nebuchadrezzar king of Babylon, a king of kings, from the north” (Ezekiel 26:7). The
verbs of verses 7 to 11 are in the singular, “he”: he shall slay the daughters in the field,
make a fort, cast a mount, set engines against the walls. In verse 12 they turn plural
again: “And they shall make a spoil of thy riches, and make a prey of thy merchandise:
and they shall break down thy walls.” The chapter then says the LORD will cause the

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 22

noise of the songs to cease (26:13), and, “And I will make thee like the top of a rock:
thou shalt be a place to spread nets upon; thou shalt be built no more: for I the LORD
have spoken it” (Ezekiel 26:14). The end of the chapter is the sharpest. The LORD will
“bring thee down with them that descend into the pit” (Ezekiel 26:20) and say, “I will
make thee a terror, and thou shalt be no more: though thou be sought for, yet shalt
thou never be found again, saith the Lord GOD” (Ezekiel 26:21).
Two observations arise from the grammar of the chapter. The first is that the
sentence names a first stroke, Nebuchadrezzar, and a larger sweep, “many nations,”
and keeps them in different persons. The second is that the spoil of verse 12 is the
work of “they,” not “he.” The volume notes both and builds on neither a claim about
what Nebuchadrezzar took. Critics who read the chapter as a failed prediction take
verses 7 to 14 together as one prediction of a single conquest with a single prize.
Paul Ferguson, who answers them, takes the opposite course and says “The passage
does not state that Nebuchadnezzar would capture the island city and get its wealth”
(Bible and Spade, 2006). The volume’s own claim is narrower than either. It is that the
text, in Ezekiel 29:17–20, gives its own report of what the siege yielded, and that this
report is a thing the first chapter did not have to say.
6.3 The prince of Tyrus
Between the two statements Ezekiel speaks to the ruler. “Son of man, say unto the
prince of Tyrus, Thus saith the Lord GOD; Because thine heart is lifted up, and thou
hast said, I am a God, I sit in the seat of God, in the midst of the seas; yet thou art a
man, and not God” (Ezekiel 28:2). The prince is told in irony, “Behold, thou art wiser
than Daniel” (Ezekiel 28:3), and the strangers who come will “defile thy brightness”
(Ezekiel 28:7). His end is a man’s: “Thou shalt die the deaths of the uncircumcised by
the hand of strangers: for I have spoken it, saith the Lord GOD” (Ezekiel 28:10). The
volume reads these ten verses as the LORD’s word to the ruler of Tyre. The
lamentation that follows (Ezekiel 28:11–19) is spoken to the king of Tyre, and the
volume does not read it as pointing to any unseen being. That question is not this
volume’s, and the volume names no one beyond the king the prophet addresses.
At the end of chapter 28 the oracle turns to the neighbours and to Israel. “And there
shall be no more a pricking brier unto the house of Israel, nor any grieving thorn of all
that are round about them, that despised them” (Ezekiel 28:24). “And they shall dwell
safely therein, and shall build houses, and plant vineyards; yea, they shall dwell with
confidence, when I have executed judgments upon all those that despise them round
about them” (Ezekiel 28:26). The judgment on the neighbours has a purpose that is
stated: the safety of Israel in her land. The statement is read again in chapter 8.
6.4 The wages of the siege
Ezekiel 29:17–20 is the text updating its own report. “Son of man, Nebuchadrezzar
king of Babylon caused his army to serve a great service against Tyrus: every head
was made bald, and every shoulder was peeled: yet had he no wages, nor his army, for
Tyrus, for the service that he had served against it” (Ezekiel 29:18). The prophet puts
the siege in the past tense: it has been served, and the pay has not come. The
substitution follows (Ezekiel 29:19, 20). The volume reads two things in this. The first
is that Ezekiel does not say the siege failed. The service was “a great service,” and the
men were worn by it. The second is that the LORD does not say he had promised
wages for it in chapter 26. He supplies them from a different quarter, and says why:

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 23

“because they wrought for me” (Ezekiel 29:20). The siege was for the LORD, and the
LORD pays.
Robert Carroll, Edwin Burtt and Keith Carley are named in Ferguson’s article among
those who treat the Tyre prophecies as unfulfilled or as a problem, and Ferguson
states the critical reading in these terms: “Ezekiel 29:17–20 is an apology by the
prophet for being wrong” (Bible and Spade, 2006). The volume knows these three only
through Ferguson’s report and says what they hold in its own words. Ferguson’s reply
is that the passages do not conflict: “There is no contradiction.” Hank Hanegraaff, in
the Christian Research Journal in 2013, writes of the same siege, “His efforts, however,
went largely unrewarded,” and then finds the full fulfilment of the prophecy in
Alexander’s capture of the island city, writing “Thus was fulfilled the most improbable
of prophecies.” The volume agrees with them on the facts of Ezekiel 29:18: the
service went unrewarded. It does not take them in their conclusions, that the whole of
chapter 26 is done, or that the prophet has erred. Chapter 9 sets out the
disagreement.
6.5 Zechariah: the city built again
Zechariah’s burden is later than Ezekiel’s and speaks of the city as it stands again.
“And Tyrus did build herself a strong hold, and heaped up silver as the dust, and fine
gold as the mire of the streets” (Zechariah 9:3). The sentence follows at once:
“Behold, the Lord will cast her out, and he will smite her power in the sea; and she
shall be devoured with fire” (Zechariah 9:4). The oracle goes on down the coast, to
Ashkelon, Gaza and Ekron (Zechariah 9:5–7), and turns to the house of the LORD:
“And I will encamp about mine house because of the army … and no oppressor shall
pass through them any more: for now have I seen with mine eyes” (Zechariah 9:8).
The oracle puts Tyre’s fall inside a word about Judah’s safety, as Ezekiel 28:24–26
did.
Two things follow for the volume. A word of the LORD speaks against Tyre after
Ezekiel’s, so the ending of Tyre was not closed by Ezekiel 26 and the text does not say
it was. And Zechariah 9:4 has no later fulfilment reported in Scripture, so it stands
among the open sentences. The volume cannot say that Zechariah 9:4 was
Alexander’s siege, and cannot say it was not; it reports the verse and says the text
gives no date for it.
6.6 Joel, Amos and the Lord’s own words
Two other prophets and the Lord himself name Tyre and Sidon. Amos charges Tyre
with delivering up “the whole captivity to Edom” and forgetting “the brotherly
covenant” (Amos 1:9), and sends “a fire on the wall of Tyrus” (Amos 1:10). Joel has
the LORD say, “Yea, and what have ye to do with me, O Tyre, and Zidon, and all the
coasts of Palestine?” (Joel 3:4). The charge is the theft of the temple’s silver and gold,
“my goodly pleasant things,” and the sale of Judah’s children (Joel 3:5, 6). The
sentence is a reversal: the LORD will “sell your sons and your daughters into the hand
of the children of Judah, and they shall sell them to the Sabeans, to a people far off”
(Joel 3:8). It is set in the day when the nations are gathered (Joel 3:2), which chapter
7 takes up.
In the Gospels the Lord names the two cities in a comparison. To the cities that did
not repent he says, “if the mighty works, which were done in you, had been done in
Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes,” and, “It

shall be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon at the day of judgment, than for you”
(Matthew 11:21, 22). Luke gives the same saying (Luke 10:13, 14). The Gospel

and Bethsaida. This volume draws one point: the Lord speaks of Tyre and Sidon as
places whose people stand at a coming judgment, and he does not say the cities
stand or lie in ruins. The volume leaves it there.

7. The Cup and the Day
Claim: the oracles against the nations are tied together by a measure and by a day.
The measure is that each nation’s sentence is cut to its ground: what it did to others
is done to it, and the conqueror is conquered. The day is the one the prophets call the

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 25

day of the LORD, on which the cup of Jeremiah 25 passes to every nation and the
LORD pleads with all flesh. Scripture reports the first stroke of the cup as begun in
Jeremiah’s own time and holds the rest open. The volume reads the passages in
order, keeps Babylon and Edom where their own volumes read them, and says plainly
what it does not take up.
7.1 Babylon drinks last, and drinks
Jeremiah 25 does not end the list at Egypt or at Tyre. The cup goes to “all the
kingdoms of the world, which are upon the face of the earth: and the king of Sheshach
shall drink after them” (Jeremiah 25:26). The one who administers the cup is itself on
the list. The LORD calls Nebuchadrezzar “my servant” (Jeremiah 25:9) and sends him
with the families of the north against Judah and “against all these nations round
about,” and then sets his term: “and these nations shall serve the king of Babylon
seventy years” (Jeremiah 25:11). Then: “And it shall come to pass, when seventy
years are accomplished, that I will punish the king of Babylon, and that nation, saith
the LORD, for their iniquity, and the land of the Chaldeans, and will make it perpetual
desolations” (Jeremiah 25:12). The verse after gives the book itself a part: “And I will
bring upon that land all my words which I have pronounced against it, even all that is
written in this book, which Jeremiah hath prophesied against all the nations”
(Jeremiah 25:13).
The reason follows, and it is the measure in one sentence: “For many nations and
great kings shall serve themselves of them also: and I will recompense them
according to their deeds, and according to the works of their own hands” (Jeremiah
25:14). Isaiah’s heading for Babylon is “The burden of Babylon, which Isaiah the son
of Amoz did see” (Isaiah 13:1), and Habakkuk’s taunt says the same of the conqueror:
“Because thou hast spoiled many nations, all the remnant of the people shall spoil
thee” (Habakkuk 2:8). The volume does not take up Babylon. Prophetic Commentary
Volume 3 reads Babylon with Revelation 17 and 18, and what it concludes there is
cited here and not repeated. This chapter keeps one point from Babylon: the
instrument is in the cup. That is why the volume says the oracles are fitted to their
grounds. Babylon is not exempt because the LORD used it.
7.2 From the city called by my name to all flesh
The cup is passed in order, and the order tells the reader where the sword begins.
“For, lo, I begin to bring evil on the city which is called by my name, and should ye be
utterly unpunished? Ye shall not be unpunished: for I will call for a sword upon all the
inhabitants of the earth, saith the LORD of hosts” (Jeremiah 25:29). The sentence on
the city that bears the LORD’s name is the first drink, and the widening follows. “The
LORD shall roar from on high, and utter his voice from his holy habitation; he shall
mightily roar upon his habitation; he shall give a shout, as they that tread the grapes,
against all the inhabitants of the earth” (Jeremiah 25:30). “A noise shall come even to
the ends of the earth; for the LORD hath a controversy with the nations, he will plead
with all flesh; he will give them that are wicked to the sword, saith the LORD”
(Jeremiah 25:31).
The next two verses carry the oracle past anything the book reports. “Thus saith the
LORD of hosts, Behold, evil shall go forth from nation to nation, and a great whirlwind
shall be raised up from the coasts of the earth” (Jeremiah 25:32), and, “And the slain
of the LORD shall be at that day from one end of the earth even unto the other end of

the earth: they shall not be lamented, neither gathered, nor buried; they shall be dung
upon the ground” (Jeremiah 25:33). The phrase “at that day” gives the sweep a day,
and the words “from one end of the earth even unto the other end of the earth” give it
a scope. The volume reads the scope as wider than any single campaign the Bible
names. The campaigns of Nebuchadrezzar are the first drink, and they are reported as
such. The roar of verse 30 and the slain of verse 33 are not reported by any later
narrative, and the volume holds them as open.
Peels draws the same line from the structure of the collection. He says that in these
oracles “the function and meaning of these texts are to be found in the proclamation
of the sovereign reign of the King of Zion,” and that “the oracles unanimously put the
acts of God at the centre” (European Journal of Theology 16:2, 2007). The volume
agrees. Where it differs is in what it does with the open clauses. Peels’s article is
about the function of the oracles and not about their fulfilment, and the volume’s
question is exactly that.
7.3 Obadiah 15 and the cup in the LORD’s hand
Obadiah has been in the volume since chapter 2 as Edom’s accuser. Its centre is
verse 15, a sentence that is addressed to Edom and widens in the same verse. “For
the day of the LORD is near upon all the heathen: as thou hast done, it shall be done
unto thee: thy reward shall return upon thine own head” (Obadiah 15). The clause “as
thou hast done” is the measure again, put as a rule. Verse 16 takes up the cup: “For as
ye have drunk upon my holy mountain, so shall all the heathen drink continually, yea,
they shall drink, and they shall swallow down, and they shall be as though they had
not been.” The drinking upon the holy mountain is Edom’s and the nations’; the
drinking that returns is that of all the heathen. The same figure, a cup to be drunk,
runs from Jeremiah 25 to Obadiah, and Habakkuk 2:16 has it at the close of his taunt:
“the cup of the LORD’s right hand shall be turned unto thee.”
Obadiah does not stop at the sentence. “But upon mount Zion shall be deliverance,
and there shall be holiness; and the house of Jacob shall possess their possessions”
(Obadiah 17). “And saviours shall come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau;
and the kingdom shall be the LORD’s” (Obadiah 21). The last sentence of the book
gives the end of the day: a kingdom that is the LORD’s, with Zion as its seat. The fate
of Edom itself is read in Prophetic Commentary Volume 5, and the volume cites that
reading and does not restate it. What this chapter takes from Obadiah is the shape:
the day of the LORD on all the heathen, a measure that returns on the head, and
deliverance and a kingdom on Zion.
7.4 Zephaniah, Joel and the gathering of the nations
Zephaniah gives the day as a purpose. “Therefore wait ye upon me, saith the LORD,
until the day that I rise up to the prey: for my determination is to gather the nations,
that I may assemble the kingdoms, to pour upon them mine indignation, even all my
fierce anger: for all the earth shall be devoured with the fire of my jealousy”
(Zephaniah 3:8). The word “determination” is the LORD’s own, and the content is a
gathering. The next verse turns: “For then will I turn to the people a pure language,
that they may all call upon the name of the LORD, to serve him with one consent”
(Zephaniah 3:9). The gathering is for judgment and the judgment is followed by a
purified speech. Chapter 8 reads the second half.

Joel places the gathering in a time: “For, behold, in those days, and in that time, when I
shall bring again the captivity of Judah and Jerusalem, I will also gather all nations,
and will bring them down into the valley of Jehoshaphat, and will plead with them
there for my people and for my heritage Israel, whom they have scattered among the
nations, and parted my land” (Joel 3:1, 2). The time is the time when Judah’s captivity
is brought again. The cause is what the nations did to Israel: scattered her among the
nations and parted her land. The gathering of the nations is therefore tied to the
restoration of Judah and Jerusalem, and it is the same clause the volume has met in
Jeremiah 48:47, 49:6 and 49:39, the captivity brought again, now attached to a day on
which the nations are judged.
7.5 The measure: ground and sentence
The reader can set the grounds of chapter 2 beside the sentences of chapters 3 to 6
and see that the sentences are cut to the grounds. Moab burned the bones of Edom’s
king, and fire is sent on Moab (Amos 2:1, 2). Ammon ripped up the women of Gilead
to enlarge its border, and Ammon’s border is given away (Ezekiel 25:4, 10). Gaza and
Tyre sold captives, and the LORD says to Tyre and Zidon that he will sell their sons
and daughters into the hand of Judah (Joel 3:8). Edom stood on the other side, and
“as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee” (Obadiah 15). The conqueror who “hast
spoiled many nations” is spoiled (Habakkuk 2:8). Egypt was “a staff of reed to the
house of Israel” and is made the basest of kingdoms (Ezekiel 29:6, 15). Pharaoh said,
“My river is mine own,” and his river is made dry (Ezekiel 29:3; 30:12). The pattern is
consistent enough that the volume treats it as a rule of the oracles, not as a
coincidence.

8. The Nations That Come
Claim: the open clauses of the oracles against the nations are not loose ends. They
join the prophets’ other word about the nations, that in the last days they come to Zion
to be taught, to worship and to seek the LORD, and that Israel stands among them
with a name. The volume holds the open oracles as one picture: nations blessed
through Israel’s restored line in the Kingdom, each still named as the nation it is, with
Jerusalem at the centre. It does not read the Body of Christ or the church into any of
these passages, and it does not read them as already done.
8.1 The mountain of the LORD’s house
Isaiah 2 is the prophets’ plainest picture of the nations coming. “And it shall come to
pass in the last days, that the mountain of the LORD’s house shall be established in
the top of the mountains, and shall be exalted above the hills; and all nations shall
flow unto it” (Isaiah 2:2). The people’s speech is in the next verse: “Come ye, and let
us go up to the mountain of the LORD, to the house of the God of Jacob; and he will
teach us of his ways, and we will walk in his paths: for out of Zion shall go forth the
law, and the word of the LORD from Jerusalem” (Isaiah 2:3). The nations travel to a
place, to a house, and to a teaching that comes out of a city. Then the judging: “And
he shall judge among the nations, and shall rebuke many people: and they shall beat

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 29

their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks: nation shall not lift
up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more” (Isaiah 2:4).
The phrase “the last days” matters for the volume’s reading of the clauses in
Jeremiah. The King James translators render one Hebrew phrase by “last days” in
Isaiah 2:2 and by “latter days” in Jeremiah 48:47 and 49:39. The volume draws no
date from the phrase and no more than a link. The captivities of Moab and Elam are
brought again in the same stretch of time that Isaiah gives to the nations’ going up to
Zion, and the clause for Moab sits beside a sentence about a throne in the tabernacle
of David (Isaiah 16:5). The restoration of the nations and the establishment of Zion
are told in one season.
8.2 Egypt, Assyria and Israel
The passage this volume has called its hardest test of the reading is Isaiah 19:23–25,
read now in the company of Isaiah 2. “In that day shall there be a highway out of
Egypt to Assyria, and the Assyrian shall come into Egypt, and the Egyptian into
Assyria, and the Egyptians shall serve with the Assyrians. In that day shall Israel be
the third with Egypt and with Assyria, even a blessing in the midst of the land: whom
the LORD of hosts shall bless, saying, Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the
work of my hands, and Israel mine inheritance” (Isaiah 19:23–25). The three names
are kept. Egypt is still Egypt and Assyria is still Assyria, and Israel is the third. The
blessing is “in the midst of the land,” which is the land of Israel in the other passages
of the volume’s texts. The nations are not made into Israel, and Israel is not made into
the nations.
The same note sounds in Zephaniah, who, in the last chapter of his book, has the
LORD say, “For then will I turn to the people a pure language, that they may all call
upon the name of the LORD, to serve him with one consent” (Zephaniah 3:9). The
suppliants come from far: “From beyond the rivers of Ethiopia my suppliants, even the
daughter of my dispersed, shall bring mine offering” (Zephaniah 3:10). Earlier in the
same prophet, in the oracle against the Philistines and Moab and Ammon, the LORD
says, “The LORD will be terrible unto them: for he will famish all the gods of the earth;
and men shall worship him, every one from his place, even all the isles of the heathen”
(Zephaniah 2:11). The oracle on the nations holds the two sides together: the gods
are starved and the nations worship. Habakkuk closes his taunt on the same two
sides, “For the earth shall be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD, as the
waters cover the sea” (Habakkuk 2:14), and, six verses on, “let all the earth keep
silence before him” (Habakkuk 2:20).
8.3 Jerusalem at the centre
Zechariah speaks of the nations’ coming in terms that centre on a city and a people.
“Yea, many people and strong nations shall come to seek the LORD of hosts in
Jerusalem, and to pray before the LORD” (Zechariah 8:22). And then: “In those days it
shall come to pass, that ten men shall take hold out of all languages of the nations,
even shall take hold of the skirt of him that is a Jew, saying, We will go with you: for
we have heard that God is with you” (Zechariah 8:23). Ten men from every language
take hold of one Jew. The picture holds the people, the places, and the reason the
nations come: they have heard that God is with them. The nations ask to go with
Israel.

Zechariah 14 gives the same coming in terms of those who survive. “And it shall
come to pass, that every one that is left of all the nations which came against
Jerusalem shall even go up from year to year to worship the King, the LORD of hosts,
and to keep the feast of tabernacles” (Zechariah 14:16). The word “left” says that
these are the survivors of nations that had come against the city. Then the penalty for
not coming: “even upon them shall be no rain” (Zechariah 14:17), and Egypt is named:
“And if the family of Egypt go not up, and come not, that have no rain; there shall be
the plague, wherewith the LORD will smite the heathen that come not up to keep the
feast of tabernacles” (Zechariah 14:18). The chapter ends on the holiness of the city:
“Yea, every pot in Jerusalem and in Judah shall be holiness unto the LORD of hosts”
(Zechariah 14:21).
Two points follow. The first is that the nations are still nations in these texts, with
families, and Egypt is named, so a nation’s blessing does not erase it. The second is
that judgment and blessing live in the same chapter. Egypt that is “my people” in
Isaiah 19:25 is a family under the plague in Zechariah 14:18 if it does not come up.
The volume does not harmonize the two by timeline. It reads them as the same
kingdom seen from two sides: a kingdom in which the nations are blessed and ruled.
The blessing is real and the rule is real.
8.4 Israel’s name among them
The oracles that restore the nations also restore Israel, and the prophets keep the two
in step. Ammon’s fall is a sentence “then shall Israel be heir unto them that were his
heirs” (Jeremiah 49:2). Ezekiel says of the neighbours that despised Israel, “they shall
dwell safely therein, and shall build houses, and plant vineyards” after the LORD has
“executed judgments upon all those that despise them round about them” (Ezekiel
28:26). Zephaniah closes his book on the same pairing: “At that time will I bring you
again, even in the time that I gather you: for I will make you a name and a praise
among all people of the earth, when I turn back your captivity before your eyes, saith
the LORD” (Zephaniah 3:20). And Amos closes his on the tabernacle of David raised,
so that the house of David “may possess the remnant of Edom, and of all the heathen,
which are called by my name” (Amos 9:11, 12). The restoration of the nations is not a
separate event from the restoration of Israel. In every one of these passages the
nations are blessed through Israel’s restored line, and Israel is given a name among
them.
This is the point at which the volume refers to the promise to Abraham, “and in thee
shall all families of the earth be blessed” (Genesis 12:3). The promise is read in
Covenantal Volume 1, and the volume takes it up in one respect only: the open
clauses of this volume are the kind of text in which Genesis 12:3 is spoken of in the
prophets, nations blessed through the line the LORD has set in Zion. The volume does
not read Genesis 12:3 or Galatians 3 further than that.
8.5 Where the Body is not read
The Body of Christ is not named in any passage of this chapter or of the oracles that
precede it. The volume does not read the Body into them. The oracles are addressed
to nations, their grounds are what nations did, and their open clauses are carried by
Israel’s restored line in a kingdom with Jerusalem at its centre. The church is not the
five cities of Egypt that speak the language of Canaan, it is not the mountain of the
LORD’s house, and it is not the family of Egypt that goes up to Jerusalem or does not.

passages are not the place the Body finds it. The Body is in no Israelite covenant, and
the promises of the nations’ blessing are tied to Israel’s house and Zion.

9. Objections and Replies
Claim: the volume’s reading, that the oracles against the nations differ in their ground,
term and ending, and that the open ones are held open for the Kingdom, answers the
objections best made against it, and where an objector is right the volume says so.
Modern writers who support parts of the argument are named beside those who

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 32

press against it. A writer is quoted only where the author has read the words in a free
text. Where a position is known only because another writer reports it, the volume
says so, names the report, and puts no quotation marks around the position.
9.1 Who agrees, and where the volume differs
Two modern writers support parts of the argument. H. G. L. Peels, in an article in
European Journal of Theology in 2007 on the oracles in Jeremiah, writes that “four
times in Jeremiah 46-51 a word of welfare is added to a word of judgment on a
foreign nation.” The four are Jeremiah 46:26, 48:47, 49:6 and 49:39, and they are the
clauses on which the open class of this volume rests. Peels also writes that the
oracles “unanimously put the acts of God at the centre.” The volume agrees, and adds
a point he does not take up, which is the question of what became of the clauses. His
article is about the function of the oracles and not their fulfilment.
W. A. Criswell, in a sermon of 1975 on Isaiah 19:18–25, reads the passage as “the
golden prophecy of the golden millennial tomorrow” and speaks of “the people of the
Nile.” He holds Egypt’s blessing to the day of the kingdom and names it, in his words,
“a blessing in the midst of the earth.” The volume agrees with him that Isaiah 19:23–
25 waits for the Kingdom and that Egypt and Assyria are kept as Egypt and Assyria. It
differs in method. His is a sermon, and this volume asks of every oracle what the text
itself reports about its term, so the open class is drawn from the clauses and the
silence of the later books, not from the verses of one chapter.
9.2 Objection: the oracles were all fulfilled in history
This is the strongest opposing position, and for Tyre it is argued with care. Paul
Ferguson, in Bible and Spade in 2006, answers critics of Ezekiel 26 and holds that the
prophecy was fulfilled as written. He says that “the passage does not state that
Nebuchadnezzar would capture the island city and get its wealth,” and that the total
destruction of the city was to be accomplished “gradually by one nation after
another.” On his reading Ezekiel 26:1–14 and 29:17–20 agree: “there is no
contradiction.” Hank Hanegraaff, in Christian Research Journal in 2013, holds that the
prophecy was fulfilled in stages, with the greatest stroke by Alexander, and writes of
Nebuchadnezzar’s siege that “his efforts, however, went largely unrewarded.” The
wider claim behind both is that the oracles against the nations are closed.
Reply. For Tyre the volume sits near this reading and says so in chapter 6. The
grammar of Ezekiel 26 does change from “many nations” to a single “he” to “they,”
and the prophet’s own later word in Ezekiel 29:17–20 reports that the first siege had
not paid. Where the volume parts from the objection is the move from Tyre to all the
nations. A reading that closes every oracle by history must say what happened to the
clauses that no later passage reports: the captivity of Moab and of Elam brought
again “in the latter days” (Jeremiah 48:47; 49:39), the sons of Ammon returned
(Jeremiah 49:6), Egypt “my people” (Isaiah 19:25). Ferguson and Hanegraaff do not
argue these, because their subject is Tyre. The reading that a prophecy closes when
its first stroke falls is right for Nineveh, whose sentence carried no clause of return,
and right for the sentences on Damascus in their terms. It is not right for an oracle
that states its own further word.
9.3 Objection: the prophecies failed
A third voice treats the oracle on Tyre as a case of failure. The volume knows this
position only because Ferguson reports it in the article above. As he reports it, a line

ZAY-MARIE PRESS THE PROPHETS SERIES, VOLUME 3 • 33

of critical scholarship, among whom he names Carroll, Burtt and Carley, holds that
Ezekiel predicted the fall of Tyre to Nebuchadnezzar, that the siege did not deliver it,
and that the prophet then retracted the claim in Ezekiel 29:17–20. The volume has not
read these writers, describes the position through Ferguson’s report, and puts it in no
quotation marks. Ferguson’s own summary of the objection is that Ezekiel 29:17–20
is “an apology by the prophet for being wrong.”
Reply. The objection reads Ezekiel 29:17–20 as a confession, and the verses do not
say so. They report that Nebuchadrezzar served “a great service against Tyrus,” that
“yet had he no wages, nor his army, for Tyrus,” and that Egypt is given to him as the
wages for his army (Ezekiel 29:18, 19). The verse reports what the siege did and did
not bring, and what the LORD gives in its place. Nothing in the text withdraws Ezekiel
26. The volume holds the two chapters side by side and does not harmonize them
beyond what the text says: Ezekiel 26 and Isaiah 23:15–18 stand together with no
timeline the passages do not give. The charge of failure assumes the prophecy of
Ezekiel 26:7–11 was a promise of what one king would take, and the chapter’s
grammar says otherwise.
9.4 Objection: the open clauses are the church
The Ligonier Ministries magazine Tabletalk printed an unsigned reading of Isaiah
19:18–25 in March 2013, titled “Egypt and Assyria Serve the Lord.” It says that
“humanly speaking, it was unthinkable for even one city to serve the LORD because
pagan Egypt hated him so,” and that the image of five converted cities shows how
miraculous the conversion would be. It reads the altar in the midst of the land as “a
spiritual altar, a gospel church, in the midst of the land of Egypt.” The objection is that
the blessing of Egypt and Assyria is the conversion of the nations to Christ, and that
the open clauses are therefore met in the church.
Reply. The volume grants the miracle and the wonder at it. It parts from the reading in
three places. First, the passage keeps three names, and a church that is neither Egypt
nor Assyria nor Israel cannot be the third beside them. The text says “Israel be the
third with Egypt and with Assyria” (Isaiah 19:24), and a blessing on Israel as a people
in a land is not met by a church in which that distinction is gone. Second, the passage
speaks of a highway between Egypt and Assyria and of “the midst of the land,” the
language of place, in a chapter whose every verse of promise opens with “in that day.”
Third, the Body of Christ is in no Israelite covenant, and the passage belongs to
Israel’s promised future, in which nations are blessed through the line the LORD
restores. The church of this age is not the place the prophecy waits for.
9.5 Objection: all of it is still to come
The opposite objection holds that every oracle is future, so that Nineveh, Damascus
and Tyre wait for a last-day fulfilment. No writer is named for it; it is stated in the
author’s words. Reply. The texts do not support it. Jonah 3:10 reports that the LORD
repented of the evil and “he did it not” for the forty days, and Nahum and Zephaniah
2:13–15 give the later sentence in terms that look to the city’s ruin, with no clause of
return. Amos 1:3–5 gives Damascus its sentence and Zechariah 9:1 speaks of the
word against Damascus, but neither attaches a clause of return to it. An oracle with
no restoration clause and a stated ending is not made open by being stretched. The
volume holds the closed class closed for the same reason it holds the open class
open: it follows what the passage says.

9.6 Objection: the open clauses are only a manner of speaking
Peels’s observation that the oracles put the acts of God at the centre can be turned
into an objection: if the oracles proclaim God’s rule, the welfare clauses are part of
that proclamation and not forecasts of what happens to Moab or Elam. The objection
is not Peels’s. His article does not say it, and he writes of the clauses as words of
welfare added to words of judgment. It is the volume’s own statement of an objection
a reader may make. Reply. The clauses are stated as acts of the LORD, with a term:
“Yet will I bring again the captivity of Moab in the latter days, saith the LORD”
(Jeremiah 48:47). A sentence of this form can be read as a manner of speaking only if
the sentences of judgment beside it are read the same way, and then the whole oracle
becomes a rhetoric of God’s rule with no event in it. That is not how Jeremiah 25:12
treats the seventy years or how Ezekiel 29:17–20 treats Tyre. The volume takes the
clauses as statements of what the LORD will do.
9.7 Objection: do the oracles say anything to nations today?
A last objection asks whether the oracles bind or promise anything to a nation now.
No writer is named for it. Reply. The oracles are addressed to nations that existed
when they were spoken, on grounds that those nations had incurred against the LORD
and against Israel. The volume draws a limited point and no more: the grounds,
violence, betrayal, gloating, pride and idols, are sins the prophets name, and the
LORD’s concern with them is not confined to Israel (Amos 9:7). That is a statement
about the grounds. The volume does not turn the sentences into predictions about
any nation now, and it does not read the open clauses as promises to any nation but
those the text names.

9.8 What the volume takes from the replies
• Peels and Criswell support parts of the argument: the welfare clauses in Jeremiah
46–51 are real, and Isaiah 19:23–25 waits for the Kingdom with Egypt and
Assyria kept as themselves.
• Ferguson and Hanegraaff are right for Tyre in the main and cannot close the
clauses for Moab, Ammon, Elam and Egypt, which they do not treat.
• The charge of failure is known only through Ferguson’s report; Ezekiel 29:17–20
reports what the siege did not bring and does not withdraw Ezekiel 26.
• The church reading of Isaiah 19 loses the three names, the language of place and
the line of Israel’s restoration.
• The two opposite errors, all closed and all open, are each answered by the
oracles’ own terms, and the Body of Christ is read into no oracle.

Conclusion
The volume began with a plain statement: it argues a position. The position is that the
oracles against the nations are not one prophecy on one timeline. Each names a
ground, a term and an ending, and the text sorts them into three classes. Some are
closed, because the sentence is final and no clause of return is attached. Some are
part-fulfilled, because the text itself reports what happened and what remains. Some
are open, because they carry a restoration clause that no later passage reports
fulfilled. The volume has argued against three readings that flatten the difference: that
all are fulfilled, that all are spiritualized into the church, and that all are still to come.
Nineveh is the plainest closed case. The forty days were suspended when the city
turned, and the sentence in Nahum and Zephaniah that follows carries no clause of
return. Egypt and Tyre are the plainest part-fulfilled cases. Ezekiel reports the forty
years and the base kingdom for Egypt, and he reports in his own later word what the
siege of Tyre did and did not bring. Moab, Ammon, Elam and the end of Egypt are
open, because Jeremiah 48:47, 49:6 and 49:39 and Isaiah 19:23–25 state a further
word and Scripture reports none of them done. The volume holds them open as
nations blessed through Israel’s restored line in the Kingdom, with Jerusalem at its
centre.
