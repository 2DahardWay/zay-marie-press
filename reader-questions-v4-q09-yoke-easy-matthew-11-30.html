What Does “My Yoke Is Easy” Mean in Matthew 11:30?

From Reader Questions, Volume 4: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 4 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

It is the Lord’s invitation to Israel’s weary to take his teaching in place of the burden that the scribes and Pharisees laid on them. “Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me.” A yoke is the discipleship of a pupil to his master, and the Lord’s yoke is “easy,” kind or good, because he is “meek and lowly in heart” and his burden is “light” beside the heavy burdens of the Pharisees (23:4). It is still a yoke and still asks him to be followed. It was spoken to Israel in the kingdom ministry and is not the Body’s call to be saved.

Where the Verses Stand

Matthew 11 begins with John the Baptist’s question and the Lord’s answer, then his upbraiding of the cities where most of his works were done, Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum, “because they repented not.” He thanks the Father that he has “hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes,” says all things are delivered to him, and gives the invitation. Chapter 12 opens with the quarrel about the sabbath, where the Lord is “Lord even of the sabbath day,” and chapter 23 describes the scribes’ “heavy burdens and grievous to be borne.”

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the invitation is to “all ye that labour and are heavy laden” and its promise is “rest unto your souls,” a phrase from Jeremiah 6:16, where Israel was told to ask for the old paths and “ye shall find rest for your souls” and answered, “We will not walk therein.”
  • Stated: the Lord calls his yoke easy and his burden light, and in the same sentence says he is “meek and lowly in heart.” The ease is that of his character as teacher.
  • Shown by the context: the people addressed are those in the cities of Galilee he has just upbraided and the multitudes he has taught, and the burden they carry is shown in chapter 23 to be the scribes’ rule “they bind heavy burdens and grievous to be borne.”
  • Shown by the context: Paul’s word to the Body about a yoke is different. He tells it to “stand fast … in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage” (Galatians 5:1), and Peter at Jerusalem calls the law “a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear” (Acts 15:10).
  • Inferred: that “my yoke” is the Lord’s own teaching and requirement in Israel’s kingdom ministry, light compared with the Pharisees’ load but still a yoke. The argument rests on the setting in Galilee, the Jeremiah allusion and the sermon’s demands, which the passage does not set aside.

The Other Readings

The Lord as wisdom, inviting all. W. D. Davies and Dale Allison (The Gospel According to Saint Matthew, International Critical Commentary) and others hear the invitation against the call of wisdom in Sirach 51, with the Lord offering himself as the one who gives rest to all who come. It must explain why the call follows the woes on Israel’s cities and why the burden named is the scribes’, and why Paul never invites the Body by this call. An offer to Israel. Others take it as the Lord’s offer to Israel’s weary, the sense taken here.

What It Means for the Reader

The verse shows who the Lord is to those who follow him: gentle, and not like the teachers who burdened them. It is not a way of being saved. The Body rests on what Paul says of grace and of liberty from the law, and does not take a yoke of the Mosaic kind (Galatians 5:1).
