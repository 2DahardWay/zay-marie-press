Romans

Paul’s sustained exposition of the gospel of God: universal guilt, justification by faith, life in Christ, Israel’s place in God’s purposes, and transformed service under grace.

TestamentNew Testament
GenrePauline Epistle
RecipientsBelievers in Rome
Central ClaimThe righteousness of God revealed in the gospel

Purpose

Romans presents Paul’s gospel in a carefully ordered argument to believers in a city he had not yet visited. Paul explains humanity’s need, God’s righteousness in justifying the ungodly through faith in Jesus Christ, the believer’s new position and life in Christ, and the practical outworking of grace.

He also addresses the place of Israel within God’s purposes in Romans 9–11 and prepares the Roman believers to understand his ministry ambitions, including his intended work farther west.

Historical and Biblical Context

Paul writes as the apostle of the Gentiles to an assembly containing believers from Jewish and Gentile backgrounds. The letter reflects questions raised by sin, law, circumcision, Abraham, Israel, Gentile inclusion, liberty, and fellowship.

Romans belongs to Paul’s ministry during the Acts 9–28 overlap. Its doctrine must be allowed to speak in its epistolary context: Paul’s authorship is important, but individual passages still require attention to their immediate subject and audience. Material is not classified merely because Paul wrote it.

Structure and Outline

  1. The gospel introduced — Romans 1:1–17
    Paul identifies his calling, mission, and thesis: God’s righteousness is revealed in the gospel.
  2. Universal guilt and justification — Romans 1:18–5:21
    Gentile and Jew stand guilty; God justifies by faith apart from works of law.
  3. New life, conflict, and assurance — Romans 6–8
    Union with Christ, freedom from sin’s dominion, the law question, life in the Spirit, and secure hope.
  4. Israel and God’s faithfulness — Romans 9–11
    Paul addresses Israel’s privilege, unbelief, remnant, Gentile inclusion, and future salvation.
  5. Life under grace in community and mission — Romans 12–16
    Consecrated service, civil conduct, liberty, mutual reception, ministry plans, and greetings.

Major Themes

The Righteousness of God

The gospel reveals God’s righteous action in judging sin and justifying those who believe. Salvation is grounded in Christ’s work rather than human merit.

Universal Sin and Justification by Faith

Neither Gentile morality nor Jewish possession of the law removes guilt. Abraham demonstrates that righteousness is counted by faith apart from works.

Union with Christ and Life under Grace

Believers are identified with Christ’s death and resurrection, released from sin’s former dominion, and called to present themselves to God in renewed service.

Israel and the Gentiles

Romans 9–11 rejects arrogance, defends God’s faithfulness, and distinguishes present unbelief and remnant realities from God’s declared purposes for Israel.

Doctrine Expressed in Practice

The mercies expounded in Romans 1–11 ground the transformed conduct of Romans 12–16: humility, service, love, responsible liberty, mutual edification, and mission.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Follow Paul’s argument across paragraphs. Isolating individual verses can obscure the questions and conclusions that govern them.
  • Distinguish justification, sanctification, Israel’s national questions, and practical exhortation without separating what Paul deliberately connects.
  • Do not treat every occurrence of “law” as identical. Determine whether the context concerns the Mosaic law, a governing principle, or another usage.
  • Romans 9–11 should not be detached from Romans 1–8 or collapsed into a denial of Israel’s stated identity and promises.
  • The olive-tree figure warns Gentiles against pride; it does not merge Israel and the Body of Christ into one undifferentiated program.
  • Let the doctrinal indicatives of grace govern the ethical imperatives that follow. Conduct is the response to divine mercy, not the basis of justification.

Study Questions

  1. How does Romans 1:16–17 state the controlling thesis of the letter?
  2. How does Paul establish the guilt of both Gentile and Jew before presenting justification?
  3. What roles do Abraham and Adam play in the argument of Romans 4–5?
  4. How do Romans 6–8 describe the believer’s new position, conflict, and hope?
  5. Which questions about Israel does Paul answer in Romans 9–11?
  6. How do the mercies of Romans 1–11 shape the conduct commanded in Romans 12–16?
