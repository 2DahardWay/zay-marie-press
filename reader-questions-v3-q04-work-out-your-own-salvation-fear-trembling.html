What Does “Work Out Your Own Salvation with Fear and Trembling” Mean in Philippians 2:12?

From Reader Questions, Volume 3: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 3 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

It is a word to the Body about living out the salvation it already has, and it is not about earning or keeping it. “Work out” means to bring to full expression what God has already worked within; the matter is sanctification, not justification. “Fear and trembling” is seriousness, humility and dependence on God’s enabling, and the very next verse says so: “it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure.” It is instruction for the Body’s spiritual maturity and is not Kingdom-discipleship language.

Where the Verses Stand

The letter is written “to all the saints in Christ Jesus which are at Philippi” (1:1), to people who are already saved. Its first chapter speaks of a company under pressure and urges them to “stand fast in one spirit” (1:27). Chapter 2 asks for lowliness and unity, sets before them Christ’s humbling of himself, and then says “Wherefore.” The command about salvation is the conclusion of that appeal, and it is followed by “Do all things without murmurings and disputings.”

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the command is addressed to those who have “always obeyed,” and it carries its own correction in the next verse: God is the one who works in them, both the willing and the doing. The verb is “work out,” not “work for.”
  • Stated: in Ephesians 2:8–9 the same apostle writes that salvation is “not of works, lest any man should boast.” Salvation is God’s gift, and what is worked out in Philippians is what God has already given.
  • Shown by the context: the letter is written to saints and its subject is their conduct: lowliness (2:3–4), the mind of Christ (2:5), no “murmurings and disputings” (2:14), unity of mind (4:2). “Work out” falls on this ground as the bringing of their saved life to full expression.
  • Shown by the context: Paul himself uses “salvation” of a present deliverance in 1:19, written from prison. The word in this letter is not confined to the moment of being saved.
  • Inferred: that “salvation” in 2:12 is the saved life brought to full expression, that is, sanctification, and not justification or the gift by which they were saved. The letter does not define the word, and the argument rests on the verb “work out,” on 2:13 and on the setting.

The Other Readings

The believer’s growth in holiness. The command is read as the believer’s progress in sanctification, to be worked out in daily life by the power God supplies. That is the reading taken here. The well-being of the community. Gordon Fee (Paul’s Letter to the Philippians, New International Commentary on the New Testament) reads the command as the community’s task of living out its salvation together in the face of pressure. It must explain why the command is “your own.” Salvation kept by effort. Some systems take the verse as saying that final salvation depends on the believer’s own work. It cannot stand with “it is God which worketh in you,” or with the verses that say salvation is “not of works.”

What It Means for the Reader

The verse is not a warning that a believer’s salvation hangs on how hard he works. It tells the saved to bring God’s work in them to full expression, in humility and with reverence for the One who is doing it, and it gives them the reason: God is working in them. It is the Body’s instruction for maturity and has nothing to do with Israel’s covenant obedience or the requirements of the kingdom.
