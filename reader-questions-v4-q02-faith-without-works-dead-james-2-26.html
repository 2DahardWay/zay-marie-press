What Does “Faith without Works Is Dead” Mean in James 2:26?

From Reader Questions, Volume 4: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 4 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

It means that a faith which is only a claim, with nothing done, is lifeless and useless, as a body without breath is lifeless. James writes to “the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad,” Israel’s prophetic remnant, and his examples, Abraham and Rahab, are figures of Israel’s covenant story. In that setting faith is expected to express itself in covenant obedience, and works are what show the faith is alive: “I will shew thee my faith by my works.” Works do not save; they demonstrate living faith. This is not Paul’s teaching on justification, which is salvation by grace through faith alone for the Body. James addresses faithfulness within Israel’s prophetic identity, and the Body’s works relate to reward and not to justification (1 Corinthians 3:11–15).

Where the Verse Stands

James’s letter opens by naming its readers, the twelve tribes scattered abroad (1:1), and the argument of chapter 2 begins with a case: a believer who says to a hungry brother, “Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled,” and gives him nothing. James asks whether such faith, “can faith save him?”, answers that it is “dead, being alone,” and then shows the point by Abraham offering Isaac and Rahab sending out the messengers. The verse is the closing comparison: as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead.

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the letter is written to “the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad” (1:1) His readers are Israel’s prophetic remnant, and his examples, Abraham and Rahab, are covenant figures.
  • Stated: the faith James calls dead is the faith of a man who “say he hath faith, and have not works” (2:14), and he compares it with the belief of the devils, who “believe, and tremble” (2:19). It is assent without life.
  • Shown by the context: the test James sets is visible to others: “I will shew thee my faith by my works” (2:18). “Justified” in the examples of Abraham and Rahab is what their acts showed and established before men, since James quotes Genesis 15:6, “Abraham believed God, and it was imputed unto him for righteousness” (2:23), as already true before the offering of Isaac.
  • Shown by the context: Paul writes to the Body of a standing before God that is received by faith “without the deeds of the law” (Romans 3:28), and of “him that worketh not, but believeth” (Romans 4:5). The question James answers, what a profession is worth within Israel’s covenant life, is not the question Paul answers.
  • Inferred: that James’s “justified” means vindicated or shown to be righteous, and not the declaring righteous that Paul treats. The word is used in both letters, and the argument rests on James’s own tests, 2:18 and 2:23, and on the different audiences.

The Other Readings

Works as necessary evidence of saving faith. Douglas Moo (The Letter of James, Pillar New Testament Commentary) and Peter Davids (The Epistle of James, New International Greek Testament Commentary) read James as speaking of the judgment before God, where only a faith that has produced works will stand. It must explain James’s own limits, a faith alone shown before men, and why a letter to Israel’s believers is read as setting out the terms of the Body’s salvation. Deliverance, not eternal salvation. Zane Hodges (The Epistle of James: Proven Character Through Testing) takes “save” in 2:14 as deliverance from the temporal ruin of a barren life. Zane Hodges reads the word the same way in 5:20. Others object that “save” in the New Testament most often speaks of eternal salvation, and the entry answers from the setting of James 2 itself.

What It Means for the Reader

The verse warns against a faith that stops at saying. It does not state the terms on which the Body is saved, and it does not undo Paul’s word that the Body is saved by grace through faith, “not of works” (Ephesians 2:8–9). The reader in the Body is told of works in another way, as the fruit of what God has already done, and as bearing on reward and not on justification: “we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works” (Ephesians 2:10).
