What Does “Until Seventy Times Seven” Mean in Matthew 18:22?

From Reader Questions, Volume 4: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 4 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

It means that there is no number at which forgiveness stops; the number is symbolic of limitless forgiveness, as God is patient with Israel. Peter asks how often he must forgive a brother who sins against him, and offers “till seven times?”, a generous count; the Lord answers, “Until seventy times seven,” and tells the parable of the servant who was forgiven ten thousand talents and would not forgive a hundred pence. The saying is given to Israel’s disciples in the kingdom ministry, where the Father’s forgiveness is shown answering the disciple’s own and ends with a warning, “if ye from your hearts forgive not.” Forgiveness is also taught in Paul’s letters, but this is not a Body command. The Body is told to forgive “even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you,” which begins from forgiveness already given and carries no such warning.

Where the Verses Stand

Matthew 18 gathers instruction for the Lord’s disciples: the little ones, the lost sheep, and what to do when a brother trespasses: tell him alone, then with witnesses, then “tell it unto the church.” Peter’s question follows directly, and the Lord’s answer opens the parable of the king who reckoned with his servants. The first servant was forgiven a debt he could never repay, and then seized a fellow servant who owed him a hundred pence. The king’s last word is that the heavenly Father will deal with the unforgiving the same way.

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the answer is a number that no one counts to: “Until seventy times seven.” Peter’s “seven times” was already the limit the teachers allowed, and the Lord replaces any limit with an immeasurable one.
  • Stated: the parable contrasts “ten thousand talents” with “an hundred pence,” a debt too large to pay and a debt small by comparison. The point is the disproportion between what was forgiven and what was refused.
  • Shown by the context: the saying is part of the Lord’s instruction to his disciples about life among them, and the parable begins “the kingdom of heaven is likened.” The standard is the Father’s dealing with his kingdom’s servants, and its sanction is that the Father “do also unto you, if ye from your hearts forgive not.”
  • Shown by the context: the Body’s word is of a different shape: “forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you” (Ephesians 4:32). It grounds the forgiving in a forgiveness already received and attaches no sanction.
  • Inferred: that the saying does not set aside the pattern of 18:15–17 or Luke 17:3–4 (“if he repent, forgive him”), since the Lord there says what to do with a brother who sins, and here says how often to forgive one who does. Whether forgiveness is offered before repentance is not settled by this passage.

The Other Readings

Seventy-seven times. Commentators note that the Greek can be read as seventy-seven times, which recalls Lamech’s boast of vengeance “seventy and sevenfold” (Genesis 4:24), so that the Lord reverses Lamech’s vengeance into forgiveness. The sense is the same: no ceiling. Forgiveness only for the repentant. Some read Luke 17:3–4 as limiting forgiveness to the one who repents, and set the saying inside it. It must explain why Peter asks how often, and why the Lord answers with a figure that no one counts.

What It Means for the Reader

The reader is shown the size of what has been forgiven him and the smallness of what is owed him. In the Body, the same pattern is given in its own terms: the forgiving is the answer to a forgiveness already received, not the condition of it.
