Matthew

The Gospel presenting Jesus as Israel’s promised Messiah and Davidic King, proclaiming the kingdom of heaven and preparing His disciples for faithful service.

TestamentNew Testament
GenreGospel

Central EmphasisThe promised King and His kingdom

Purpose

Matthew identifies Jesus as the Christ, the Son of David and Son of Abraham, and demonstrates that His person and ministry fulfill Israel’s Scriptures. The book records the King’s authoritative teaching, His kingdom proclamation and signs, the varied responses within Israel, His rejection and crucifixion, and His resurrection.

Matthew also trains attention on discipleship. Its major teaching sections show how the King instructs the Twelve and other hearers concerning kingdom righteousness, mission, community life, coming judgment, and faithful readiness.

Historical and Biblical Context

The Gospel opens within Israel’s prophetic story. Its genealogy, fulfillment formulas, Davidic titles, references to Moses, and repeated appeals to the prophets place Jesus’ ministry in relation to Israel’s covenants and promised kingdom. John the Baptist, Jesus, and the Twelve announce the nearness of the kingdom of heaven.

Matthew does not name its author within the narrative. Early Christian testimony associates it with Matthew the tax collector and apostle. Proposals for its date and first readership vary, though the book’s Jewish scriptural texture and concern for a community learning obedience are unmistakable. The Gospel’s original historical and programmatic setting must be established before later doctrinal use or present application is considered.

Structure and Outline

  1. The King’s arrival and preparation — Matthew 1–4
    Genealogy, birth, preservation, baptism, testing, and the beginning of the kingdom proclamation.
  2. Kingdom authority in word and deed — Matthew 5–10
    The Sermon on the Mount, messianic signs, and the commissioning of the Twelve to Israel.
  3. Responses to the King and kingdom mysteries — Matthew 11–13
    Opposition intensifies while Jesus explains varied responses to the word of the kingdom.
  4. Discipleship, identity, and the journey toward Jerusalem — Matthew 14–20
    The disciples confess Jesus, receive instruction, and confront the cost and character of service.
  5. The King in Jerusalem — Matthew 21–25
    Royal entry, temple confrontation, judgment controversies, and teaching concerning coming events.
  6. Death, resurrection, and commission — Matthew 26–28
    Jesus gives His life, rises, and commissions the apostles to disciple the nations.

Major Themes

Jesus the Davidic Messiah

Matthew’s opening title, genealogy, royal language, and fulfillment patterns identify Jesus as Israel’s promised King. His rejection does not overturn His identity or God’s faithfulness.

The Kingdom of Heaven

The kingdom proclamation governs the Gospel. Jesus describes its righteousness, displays its powers, exposes resistance to it, and prepares His servants for accountability and future fulfillment.

Fulfillment of Scripture

Events in Jesus’ life repeatedly occur in accordance with Israel’s Scriptures. Matthew presents fulfillment through direct prediction, typological correspondence, and the gathering up of Israel’s story in the Messiah.

Authority and Discipleship

Jesus teaches with authority, commands nature and disease, forgives sins, and directs His disciples. True discipleship involves hearing, understanding, obeying, serving, forgiving, and remaining watchful.

Rejection and Vindication

Opposition develops from questioning to conspiracy and crucifixion. The resurrection vindicates Jesus and grounds the apostles’ worldwide commission.

Matthew 12:22–45Escalating rejection and warning against that generation.
Matthew 13:1–52Parables concerning the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven.
Matthew 16:13–28Peter’s confession, Jesus’ announced suffering, and discipleship.
Matthew 28:1–20Resurrection and the commission to disciple the nations.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Read Matthew within Israel’s prophetic and covenant setting. “Kingdom of heaven” is not a vague synonym for all divine activity or for the Body of Christ.
  • Distinguish the audiences within the Gospel. Teaching addressed to crowds, opponents, the Twelve, or a smaller circle should not be flattened into one undifferentiated audience.
  • In Matthew 10, the Twelve are sent to the lost sheep of the house of Israel and told not to enter Gentile or Samaritan territory. That mission’s stated limits must be respected before comparing it with Matthew 28.
  • The Sermon on the Mount reveals the King’s authority and kingdom righteousness. Establish its meaning and assignment in that setting before drawing genuine principles or later applications.
  • Matthew’s fulfillment language is not uniform. Examine the cited Old Testament context and determine whether the relationship is predictive, typological, thematic, or representative.
  • Meaning comes first, assignment second, and application third. Later Scripture may use Gospel events and teaching doctrinally without erasing their original setting.

Study Questions

  1. How do the genealogy and birth narrative establish Jesus’ identity?
  2. What does Matthew mean by the kingdom of heaven, and who proclaims its nearness?
  3. How do Jesus’ words and works together demonstrate His authority?
  4. How does opposition develop from Matthew 9 through Matthew 12?
  5. What changes in the presentation of kingdom truth in Matthew 13, and why?
  6. How do the crucifixion and resurrection confirm the claims made throughout the Gospel?
