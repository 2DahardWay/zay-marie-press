Leviticus

God instructs redeemed Israel how to approach Him, maintain covenant holiness, and live as a people among whom the holy LORD dwells.

TestamentOld Testament
GenreLaw / Priestly instruction
Primary SettingIsrael at Mount Sinai
Key EmphasisHoliness in worship and life

Purpose

Leviticus teaches Israel how a holy God can dwell among a covenant people marked by sin, mortality, and impurity. It regulates sacrifices, priesthood, purity, atonement, worship, festivals, conduct, vows, and the use of the land.

The book’s commands shape both Israel’s approach to God and Israel’s life before one another. Ritual and ethical holiness are not competing concerns; both arise from the identity and presence of the LORD.

Historical and Biblical Context

Leviticus begins where Exodus ends: the tabernacle has been erected and the glory of the LORD fills it. Before Israel departs Sinai in Numbers, the nation must learn how worship and communal life are ordered around God’s holy presence.

The instructions are addressed chiefly to Moses for Israel, with particular sections governing Aaron and the priests. Their covenant setting, sanctuary, land, calendar, and priesthood must remain visible when the book is interpreted.

Evidence and inference: The book repeatedly introduces commands as words spoken by the LORD to Moses. Modern proposals about the development of priestly material go beyond those explicit literary claims and should be identified as reconstructions rather than presented as the text’s own account.

Structure and Outline

  1. Sacrifices and offerings — Leviticus 1–7
    Instructions govern burnt, grain, peace, purification, and reparation offerings.
  2. Priestly consecration and failure — Leviticus 8–10
    Aaron and his sons are ordained; Nadab and Abihu die after unauthorized worship.
  3. Purity and cleansing — Leviticus 11–15
    Food, childbirth, skin conditions, houses, and bodily discharges are classified and regulated.
  4. The Day of Atonement — Leviticus 16
    Annual rites cleanse the sanctuary and address Israel’s sins and impurities.
  5. Holiness in covenant life — Leviticus 17–22
    Blood, sexuality, neighbor-love, priestly conduct, and acceptable offerings are governed by God’s holiness.
  6. Sacred calendar, land, and covenant consequences — Leviticus 23–27
    Festivals, sabbatical patterns, vows, blessings, and judgments order Israel’s worship and life.

Major Themes

Holiness

God’s holiness is the controlling reality of the book. Israel is called to be holy because the LORD who set the nation apart is holy.

Sacrifice and Atonement

God provides ordered means for worship, forgiveness, purification, restitution, fellowship, and covenant maintenance. Leviticus 16 gives the annual Day of Atonement a central role.

Priesthood and Mediation

The priests teach, distinguish, offer, guard, and mediate within Israel’s covenant order. Their privilege brings heightened accountability.

Clean and Unclean

Ritual impurity is not always identical with moral guilt. The categories teach Israel to distinguish what may approach sacred space and how cleansing occurs.

Holiness in Community

Leviticus joins worship to justice, truthful dealings, sexual integrity, care for the vulnerable, and love of neighbor.

Key Passages

Leviticus 1:1–9The burnt offering introduces sacrificial approach.
Leviticus 10:1–11Unauthorized worship and the priestly duty to distinguish.
Leviticus 16:1–34The Day of Atonement cleanses sanctuary and people.
Leviticus 17:10–14The sanctity of blood and its atoning use.
Leviticus 19:1–18Holiness expressed in communal conduct and neighbor-love.
Leviticus 23:1–44Israel’s appointed feasts and sacred calendar.
Leviticus 25:1–24Sabbath years, Jubilee, land, and redemption.
Leviticus 26:1–46Covenant blessing, judgment, confession, and remembrance.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Keep moral guilt, ritual impurity, physical condition, and priestly qualification distinct unless the passage connects them.
  • Interpret each sacrifice according to its stated function rather than assuming every offering represents exactly the same thing.
  • Read holiness as both separation to God and conduct consistent with His character.
  • Do not transfer Israel’s priesthood, food laws, sacred calendar, or land regulations directly into the governing program of the Body of Christ.
  • Later biblical use of sacrificial and priestly imagery depends upon the historical meaning of Leviticus; it does not make that original covenant setting unreal.
  • Read difficult judgments and purity rules within the book’s concern for the holy God dwelling in Israel’s midst.

Study Questions

  1. What distinct purposes are assigned to the major offerings?
  2. What does the death of Nadab and Abihu teach about holy service?
  3. How does Leviticus distinguish impurity from deliberate moral rebellion?
  4. Why is Leviticus 16 structurally and theologically central?
  5. How does chapter 19 connect holiness with treatment of other people?
  6. What do the Sabbath year and Jubilee reveal about God’s ownership of the land?
