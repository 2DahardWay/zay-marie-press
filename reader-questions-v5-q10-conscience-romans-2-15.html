What Is the Conscience? Romans 2:15

From Reader Questions, Volume 5: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 5 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

The conscience is the inward moral witness God put in every person. Paul says it bears witness, “their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another.” It is not infallible: some have a weak conscience that is easily wounded and “defiled.” It must be shaped by Scripture, especially Paul’s doctrine, and Paul allows believers to be “fully persuaded in his own mind” in matters of conscience. A strong conscience is formed by truth, as those “who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil,” a general principle. The conscience does not save but shows a person’s moral accountability: God will judge “the secrets of men by Jesus Christ according to my gospel.” The Spirit uses Scripture to renew the mind and refine the conscience. It belongs to every human, as God’s moral design for all.

Where the Verses Stand

Romans 2 speaks of Gentiles who do not have the law and yet do by nature what the law requires, showing “the work of the law written in their hearts,” with their conscience bearing witness. Paul then names the day of judgment. In 1 Corinthians 8 he speaks of the weak conscience wounded by food offered to idols, and in Romans 14 of being persuaded in one’s own mind.

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: Paul says the Gentiles show “the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another” (Romans 2:15). The conscience is a witness within.
  • Stated: he says some believers have a conscience that is weak and can be defiled and wounded (1 Corinthians 8:7, 12). The conscience can be wrong or misinformed.
  • Shown by the context: Paul puts the conscience under God’s judgment, not above it: “In the day when God shall judge the secrets of men by Jesus Christ according to my gospel” (Romans 2:16). It bears witness; it does not decide.
  • Shown by the context: where the Spirit works on the mind, the result is transformation by renewing the mind (Romans 12:2), which the weak and strong believer need alike. Hebrews 5:14 states the principle that sense is exercised by use to discern good and evil, and is used here as a general truth.
  • Shown by the context: Paul calls himself to account before God and men: “herein do I exercise myself, to have always a conscience void of offence toward God, and toward men” (Acts 24:16). The conscience is something to be kept clear by attention, not merely a feeling to be obeyed.
  • Inferred: that the conscience is a universal, God-given faculty, fallible and in need of Scripture to inform it. Paul’s words about the Gentiles and about weak believers support both halves; the argument joins them.

The Other Readings

The voice of God. C. S. Lewis (Mere Christianity) takes the moral law within as the clue that a moral lawgiver stands behind the universe. This fits Paul’s picture of a witness within and must explain why Paul treats the conscience as fallible. A self-judging function. C. A. Pierce (Conscience in the New Testament) describes the conscience in Paul as a retrospective, self-judging pain over past acts, and Robert Jewett (Paul’s Anthropological Terms) as an autonomous function that is disabled when it is violated. The reading taken here rests the point that the conscience must be informed on Paul’s own texts.

What It Means for the Reader

The reader is told to take the conscience seriously, because God put it there, and not to trust it blindly, because it can be weak or wrong. It is to be informed by Scripture and renewed, and it is to be respected in a brother: to wound a weak conscience is to sin against Christ. The passage does not say that every person’s conscience agrees, and it does not set the conscience above Scripture. It describes a witness that accuses and excuses, and it leaves the reader to bring that witness to the word of God.
