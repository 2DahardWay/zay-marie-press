2 Corinthians

Paul defends the integrity of his apostleship and portrays new-covenant ministry as Christ-centered service displayed through weakness, suffering, sincerity, generosity, and divine power.

TestamentNew Testament
GenrePauline Epistle
RecipientsThe church at Corinth and saints in Achaia
Central EmphasisApostolic ministry in weakness

Purpose

Paul writes after a painful period in his relationship with Corinth. He explains his changed travel plans, expresses relief over the assembly’s responsive grief, urges forgiveness for a repentant offender, and calls the believers to complete their collection for impoverished saints.

He also answers opponents who judge ministry by outward impressiveness. Paul defends his God-given apostleship while refusing self-exalting standards: Christ’s power is displayed through a servant marked by affliction, integrity, sacrificial love, and dependence.

Historical and Biblical Context

Second Corinthians reflects communications and visits extending beyond the letters preserved in the canon. References to a painful visit, a severe letter, Titus’s report, and continuing opposition reveal a strained but recovering relationship.

Paul writes during the Acts 9–28 overlap while organizing a multi-assembly collection for saints at Jerusalem. His discussion of the new covenant identifies the character and sufficiency of his ministry; it should be read carefully alongside the covenant’s prophetic promises to Israel rather than used to erase their stated recipients.

Structure and Outline

  1. Comfort, integrity, and restored relations — 2 Corinthians 1–2
    Paul explains affliction and travel plans and urges forgiveness and reaffirmed love.
  2. The character of apostolic ministry — 2 Corinthians 3–7
    Sufficiency comes from God; glory is carried in weakness; reconciliation and holiness shape service.
  3. The collection for the saints — 2 Corinthians 8–9
    Grace produces willing, proportionate, accountable, and cheerful generosity.
  4. Paul’s apostolic defense — 2 Corinthians 10–13
    Paul confronts rival claims, recounts sufferings and revelations, and prepares for a searching visit.

Major Themes

Comfort in Affliction

God comforts His servants so they may comfort others. Suffering does not disprove ministry; it exposes dependence on the God who raises the dead.

Ministry of the New Covenant

Paul contrasts letter and Spirit, condemnation and righteousness, fading and surpassing glory. God makes His servants sufficient and transforms beholders of Christ’s glory.

Treasure in Earthen Vessels

Divine power appears through fragile messengers. Death works in Paul so that life may work in those he serves.

Reconciliation and Godly Sorrow

God reconciles through Christ and entrusts the word of reconciliation. Godly sorrow produces repentance rather than destructive regret.

Grace-Governed Giving

Christ’s self-giving supplies the pattern for generosity that is willing, equitable, transparent, and thankful.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Reconstruct the Corinthian circumstances modestly. The letter supplies real clues but not every detail of the visits, letters, offender, or opponents.
  • Paul’s defense is pastoral and theological, not personal vanity. He protects the gospel and the people from distorted measures of spiritual authority.
  • Distinguish the prophetic parties and promises of the new covenant from Paul’s Spirit-enabled ministry of its life and righteousness during the overlap.
  • “New creation” in 5:17 belongs to the surrounding argument about Christ’s death, changed evaluation, and reconciliation; it should not be reduced to self-improvement.
  • Chapters 8–9 teach grace-based giving without turning Paul’s specific Jerusalem collection into a compulsory levy.
  • Allow weakness and power, sorrow and joy, death and life to remain paired throughout the letter.

Study Questions

  1. How does Paul interpret suffering in chapters 1 and 4?
  2. What makes Paul’s ministry sufficient according to chapter 3?
  3. How do Christ’s death and resurrection shape the ministry of reconciliation?
  4. What distinguishes godly sorrow from worldly sorrow?
  5. Which principles govern the collection in chapters 8–9?
  6. How does Paul’s weakness answer the boasts of the rival teachers?

Continue your study.
Trace Paul’s contrasts—affliction and comfort, death and life, weakness and power, sorrow and joy—and note how each explains ministry.
