What Does “The Just Shall Live by His Faith” Mean in Habakkuk 2:4?

From Reader Questions, Volume 3: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 3 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

The sentence has two program-specific applications that never merge. In Habakkuk, in Israel’s program, it is God’s answer to a prophet who cannot see how he can use a cruel nation to judge his own people: the proud man’s “soul … is not upright in him,” but Israel’s faithful remnant will keep its life through the coming national distress by trusting God’s promises, “though it tarry, wait for it.” In the Mystery, Paul takes the same sentence to teach that the saints of the Body live by faith apart from the Law (Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11). The writer to the Hebrews uses it within Israel’s line, to call suffering believers to endure. It states a universal truth, and it works differently in each program’s own structure.

Where the Verse Stands

Habakkuk opens with a complaint about violence in Judah (1:2–4). The Lord answers that he is raising up the Chaldeans, “that bitter and hasty nation” (1:5–6). The prophet protests that God is “of purer eyes than to behold evil” and asks why he is silent when “the wicked devoureth the man that is more righteous than he” (1:13). He then stations himself to wait for an answer, and the Lord tells him to write the vision. The sentence about the just comes in the middle of that answer. The book ends with a prayer in which the prophet will rejoice in God though the fig tree does not blossom.

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the verse is a contrast: the one “lifted up” has a soul “not upright in him,” and “the just shall live by his faith.” The sentence closes an answer to a question about how the righteous are to stand when evil seems to prosper.
  • Stated: the answer is a waiting: “the vision is yet for an appointed time … though it tarry, wait for it; because it will surely come, it will not tarry” (2:3). “His faith” is the steadiness of one who holds to that word while he waits.
  • Shown by the context: the setting is a coming invasion. To “live” in the book is to keep his life through the judgment, as 3:17–19 shows: the man who has nothing, no fig tree, no flock, “yet” rejoices in the Lord and finds his strength in him.
  • Stated: Paul uses the sentence in Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11, where it stands for the life that belongs to the man who is righteous by faith, in contrast to the law, and so teaches the saints of the Body that they live by faith apart from the Law. In Hebrews 10:38 it is joined with “the just shall live by faith: but if any man draw back,” an appeal to those who had “endured a great fight of afflictions.”
  • Inferred: that the same sentence has two program-specific applications and does not carry the same content of faith in each. In Habakkuk, and in Hebrews within Israel’s line, the faith is in what God has said to Israel: the vision, the promise, the One who is coming. In Romans and Galatians it is in the gospel Paul preaches to the Body. They are never merged. The text does not state this, and the argument rests on each passage’s own words.

The Other Readings

Faithfulness in Habakkuk. Commentators on Habakkuk, Francis Andersen (Habakkuk, Anchor Bible) among them, discuss whether the Hebrew word means steadiness or faithfulness, so that the just man lives by his steadfast trust. The reading is close to the one taken here, which does not hold that faith and faithfulness can be pulled apart in this verse, since the steady waiting is the faith. The man righteous by faith shall live. Commentaries on Romans discuss whether Paul means that the righteous man shall live by faith or that the man who is righteous by faith shall live. One meaning everywhere. It is often taught that the sentence carries one meaning, saving faith in Christ, in all four places. It must explain how Habakkuk could have meant the content Paul preaches, which the book does not state.

What It Means for the Reader

The sentence is an answer to waiting. A person who trusts what God has said, and holds to it through a time when God seems slow, lives through the judgment that overtakes the proud. Paul takes the same sentence, in the Mystery, to show that the saints of the Body live by faith apart from the Law. Hebrews takes it within Israel’s line to urge endurance. The truth is universal, but it does not merge Israel and the Body: what each is told to believe is what God has said in that program’s own setting.
