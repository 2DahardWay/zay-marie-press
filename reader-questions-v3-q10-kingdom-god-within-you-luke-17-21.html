What Does “The Kingdom of God Is within You” Mean in Luke 17:21?

From Reader Questions, Volume 3: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 3 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

Jesus is speaking to Israel during his earthly ministry, to Pharisees who were asking when the kingdom would come, and he answers that it does not come with signs to be watched for, because the King himself stands “in the midst of” them. The words cannot mean that the kingdom was inside those men, who were rejecting him. It is a statement about the nearness of Israel’s promised kingdom, because the King was physically present. The visible kingdom in power is still ahead: he turns to his disciples and speaks of “the day when the Son of man is revealed.” It is not the Body’s heavenly kingdom and not an internal reign.

Where the Verse Stands

Luke 17 opens a stretch of teaching on the road to Jerusalem. The Pharisees have been described as “covetous” and as those who “justify yourselves before men” (16:14–15). After ten lepers are cleansed (17:11–19), they ask “when the kingdom of God should come.” The Lord answers them, and then, “unto the disciples,” speaks of days to come when they will desire to see one of the days of the Son of man.

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the question is put by Pharisees, and the Lord’s words are given as an answer to them: “he answered them and said.” The statement that it “cometh not with observation” and that men shall not say, “Lo here! or, lo there!” is a correction of their way of asking.
  • Stated: the very next verse marks a change of audience: “And he said unto the disciples.” What follows is future: “the days will come,” “the Son of man … in his day,” “the day when the Son of man is revealed” (17:22, 24, 30).
  • Shown by the context: the people addressed are the ones the Lord has just called self-justifying (16:15) and who, in 17:25, are part of “this generation” by which he is “rejected.” The kingdom cannot be said to be inside them.
  • Shown by the context: elsewhere the Lord says that his casting out of devils shows that the kingdom “is come upon you” (Luke 11:20). The kingdom was present in the King and his works among them, and they were rejecting both.
  • Inferred: that the Greek word rendered “within” can also mean “among” (the KJV margin offers “among you”) and that here it means “in the midst of you.” The word does not decide it, and the argument rests on the audience and the next verses.

The Other Readings

An inner, spiritual kingdom. The verse is widely taken to say that God’s kingdom is the reign of Christ in the believer’s heart. The reading is common in devotional writing. It must explain why the Lord would say it to Pharisees he had just called self-justifying, and why in the next verses he speaks of a future revealing. The King in their midst. I. Howard Marshall (The Gospel of Luke, New International Greek Testament Commentary) and others take the sense as “among you,” the kingdom present in the person and work of Jesus. That is the reading taken here, with the added point that the kingdom in power is future and is Israel’s, and that the statement is not a description of the Body.

ReadingWhat it must sayWhere it strains
Within the heartThe kingdom is Christ’s reign inside a person.It is said to Pharisees; 17:22–30 is future.
In the midst of themThe King stands among the men asking.The Greek allows both; the case rests on the audience.

What It Means for the Reader

The verse is not a statement about a private inner life. It tells the Pharisees, and the reader, that the question of when the kingdom comes was being asked in front of the King, and that the open reign still lay ahead. The Body’s own statement, “Christ in you, the hope of glory” (Colossians 1:27), is a different statement in a different letter and is not this one.
