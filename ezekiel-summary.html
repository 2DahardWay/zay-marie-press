Ezekiel

Among the exiles, Ezekiel reveals the departure of divine glory, the certainty of Jerusalem’s judgment, individual accountability, national restoration, and the return of God’s presence.

TestamentOld Testament
GenreMajor Prophets
Primary SettingBabylonian exile before and after Jerusalem’s fall
Key MovementFrom departing glory and judgment to restored Israel, temple, land, and presence

Purpose

Ezekiel vindicates the holiness and faithfulness of the LORD before exiles tempted to misunderstand Jerusalem’s fall. Through visions, signs, oracles, and enacted messages, the prophet exposes Israel’s abominations and announces judgment. After the city falls, the book turns decisively toward cleansing, renewed life, reunified Israel, a Davidic shepherd, restored land, and returning glory.

Historical and Biblical Context

Ezekiel is a priest called as prophet among the exiles by the Chebar canal. His dated visions span the years surrounding Babylon’s destruction of Jerusalem. The book’s recurring purpose formula declares that people and nations will know that He is the LORD.

Evidence and inference: Prophetic texts join historical setting, inspired proclamation, symbolic vision, and future promise. This summary distinguishes explicit textual claims from debated conclusions about authorship, date, chronology, structure, or fulfillment.

Structure and Outline

  1. Call, signs, and siege announced — Ezekiel 1–7
    Ezekiel sees divine glory, receives the scroll, becomes watchman, and enacts Jerusalem’s coming judgment.
  2. Abominations and departing glory — Ezekiel 8–11
    Temple corruption is revealed, judgment begins, and the glory leaves the city.
  3. Oracles explaining judgment — Ezekiel 12–24
    False confidence, failed leaders, idolatry, and covenant unfaithfulness are exposed until Jerusalem’s fall is signaled.
  4. Oracles against the nations — Ezekiel 25–32
    Neighboring powers and Egypt are judged for pride and hostility.
  5. Restoration of Israel — Ezekiel 33–39
    The watchman is renewed; the true Shepherd, new heart, dry bones, reunified nation, and victory over Gog are promised.
  6. Restored temple, worship, and land — Ezekiel 40–48
    A visionary temple, river, tribal allotments, and returning glory culminate in “The LORD Is There.”

Major Themes

The Glory and Holiness of God

The mobile throne reveals that God is not confined to Jerusalem, while departing and returning glory frame the book.

Judgment and Responsibility

National history and individual response are both taken seriously under divine justice.

New Heart and Spirit

God promises cleansing, internal renewal, and obedient life for restored Israel.

National Resurrection and Reunion

The dry bones vision interprets Israel’s national restoration and reunification.

Temple, Land, and Presence

The closing vision presents ordered worship, renewed inheritance, life-giving water, and God dwelling among His people.

Key Passages

Ezekiel 1:1–28The prophet sees the glory of the LORD upon the mobile throne.
Ezekiel 8:1–11:25Temple abominations are exposed and the glory departs.
Ezekiel 18:1–32The proverb about inherited guilt is corrected through individual accountability.
Ezekiel 24:15–27The death of Ezekiel’s wife becomes a sign concerning the sanctuary.
Ezekiel 34:1–31False shepherds are judged and a Davidic shepherd is promised.
Ezekiel 36:22–38God promises cleansing, a new heart and spirit, and restored land.
Ezekiel 37:1–28Dry bones live and the divided nation is reunited under one king.
Ezekiel 43:1–12The glory returns to the restored temple.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Interpret symbolic actions and visions by the explanations supplied within the book before seeking additional meanings.
  • Ezekiel 36–37 explicitly concerns Israel, its land, cleansing, national life, and reunification; preserve that assignment.
  • The departing glory explains exile theologically, and its return governs the closing vision.
  • Debates about the temple vision should acknowledge details that resist reduction to a mere metaphor.
  • The restoration promises belong to Israel’s Prophecy Program and must not be absorbed into the Body of Christ.

Study Questions

  1. How does the opening vision prepare exiles to understand God’s presence?
  2. Why does the glory depart from Jerusalem?
  3. How does Ezekiel join corporate history with individual accountability?
  4. What restoration elements appear in chapters 34–37?
  5. How does the final vision reverse the book’s opening judgments?
