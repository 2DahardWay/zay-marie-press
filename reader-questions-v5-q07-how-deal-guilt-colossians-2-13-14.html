How Do I Deal with Guilt? Colossians 2:13–14

From Reader Questions, Volume 5: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 5 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

Guilt is dealt with by believing that the Body is fully forgiven in Christ. Paul says God has “forgiven you all trespasses” and the “handwriting of ordinances that was against us” is gone. “Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God.” Confession acknowledges the sin, but cleansing rests on Christ’s finished work: “we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins.” Lasting guilt often comes from not understanding grace, as when the Galatians began in the Spirit and fell back on the flesh. The Spirit assures the believer of his standing: “The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God.” Guilt should lead to a change of mind, not despair, and truth, not emotion, defeats it.

Where the Verses Stand

Colossians 2 says that the Body, once dead in sins, has been made alive with Christ and forgiven all trespasses, with the record against it cancelled at the cross. Paul’s other words are Romans 5 (peace by justification), Ephesians 1 (redemption), Galatians 3 (grace not the flesh), Romans 8 (the Spirit’s witness), 2 Corinthians 7 (godly sorrow) and Philippians 4 (what to think on).

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: Paul says the forgiveness is already complete: “having forgiven you all trespasses” (Colossians 2:13), with the record “nailing it to his cross” (2:14). The believer’s peace is “being justified by faith” (Romans 5:1).
  • Stated: Paul writes that the Body has “redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins, according to the riches of his grace” (Ephesians 1:7), and the Spirit “beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God” (Romans 8:16).
  • Shown by the context: Paul rebukes the Galatians for starting in the Spirit and being “made perfect by the flesh” (Galatians 3:3), which shows that lasting guilt after faith is a sign of looking to one’s own performance.
  • Shown by the context: Paul distinguishes “godly sorrow” that “worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of” from “the sorrow of the world” that “worketh death” (2 Corinthians 7:10). The repentance in view is a change of mind in believers who had sinned, not a condition of the forgiveness already given.
  • Shown by the context: Paul says the old charge was against “us” and was taken “out of the way, nailing it to his cross” (Colossians 2:14). The accuser has no standing document left to produce.
  • Inferred: that persistent guilt in a believer is chiefly a failure to believe what has been given. The passages say the forgiveness is complete and that sorrow should lead to a change of mind; the argument joins the two.

The Other Readings

Confession restores fellowship. A common teaching is that the believer’s eternal forgiveness is settled and that confession restores fellowship. This fits the reading taken here and must show that Paul speaks to guilt by pointing to the finished forgiveness. Guilt and shame. Edward Welch (Shame Interrupted) distinguishes guilt from shame and treats both through the gospel’s cleansing and acceptance. The reading taken here agrees that the answer is what God has done.

What It Means for the Reader

The reader is told to believe what is written: forgiven all trespasses, justified, at peace, a child with the Spirit’s witness. When conscience accuses, the answer is what Christ did, not a fresh effort to feel forgiven. The passage does not say that sin no longer matters, and it does not forbid sorrow over it. It says the debt is paid and the record cancelled, and it asks that the believer’s sorrow be of the kind that turns toward God and not toward despair.
