STUDY 67 · DIGITAL STUDY

Melchizedek

The King-Priest of Salem

Who is Melchizedek in the three passages that name him — and to whom does the priesthood argument of Hebrews speak?

Melchizedek is named in only three places in Scripture, yet he has drawn more speculation than almost any figure of comparable length. The way through is to read what each passage says, in order, before deciding what any of it means.

This study begins in Genesis 14, with a battle, two kings, and a meeting in the valley of Shaveh. It follows the oath of Psalm 110, then reads Hebrews 5–7 step by step, testing each inference against the passage it reads, and only then asks to whom the argument is addressed, holding to one order throughout: meaning first, assignment second, application third.

ABOUT THIS STUDY

Read the Passages in Order: Meaning, Assignment, Application

You will read Genesis 14 in its own setting: after a war and a rescue, with two kings coming out to meet Abram. You will follow the titles, the bread and wine, the blessing, and the tithe as the text gives them, trace the oath of Psalm 110, and then follow the argument of Hebrews 5–7 inference by inference before drawing any application.

Readers who already own Study 28 (The Warning of Hebrews 6:4–6), Study 17 (The Abrahamic Covenant), or Study 12 (The New Covenant) will recognize adjoining ground. Study 28 reads the warning of Hebrews 6 in its argument; this study touches Hebrews 6 only where the oath and the forerunner (6:13–20) lead to Melchizedek. Study 17 establishes the covenant promises made to Abraham; this study treats Genesis 14 only for what it says about the king of Salem. Study 12 examines Israel’s promised restoration under Messiah; this study notes the change of priesthood and law in Hebrews 7 without re-deriving the covenant. The question this study alone answers: who is Melchizedek in the three passages that name him, what does Hebrews make of his priesthood, and to whom does that argument speak?

WHAT YOU’LL STUDY

Trace the Passages Before You Conclude

The Genesis 14 Setting

Read Genesis 14 in its own setting by tracing the war of the kings, the two kings who meet Abram, and Abram’s answer to each before interpreting a single title of the king of Salem.

Titles and the Divine Name

Weigh the titles “king of Salem” and “priest of the most high God” by following the divine name and the blessing formula through Genesis 14:18–22 and into Psalm 76:2.

Bread, Blessing, and Tithe

Follow the tithe, the blessing, and the bread and wine as the text describes them, noting what Genesis states and what it leaves unsaid.

The Oath of Psalm 110

Trace the wording of Psalm 110:1–4, including the oath of verse 4, and note whom David calls “my Lord.”

The Argument of Hebrews

Follow the argument of Hebrews 5:1–10, 6:13–20, and 7:1–28 step by step, testing each inference against the Genesis text it reads.

Testing Four Readings

Apply a repeatable discipline for testing four common readings of Melchizedek, and for receiving the priesthood argument of Hebrews without transferring its assignment.

KEY SCRIPTURES

Read the Word in Its Context

Genesis 14:17–24

Two kings meet Abram after the rescue, and Abram answers each.

Psalm 110:1–7

The Lord at God’s right hand, the king in Zion, and the sworn priesthood.

Hebrews 5:1–10

A priest defined, and the words of Psalm 110 applied to Christ.

Hebrews 6:13–20

The oath to Abraham, the anchor of the soul, and the forerunner.

Hebrews 7:1–10

The retelling of Genesis 14 and the argument from the tithe.

Hebrews 7:11–28

Two priesthoods, the change of law, and the oath.

2 Chronicles 26:16–21

Why Uzziah was struck when he went into the temple to burn incense.

Zechariah 6:12–13

The Branch who builds the temple and is a priest upon his throne.

FROM THE STUDY

Stated, Not Supplied

Genesis states what Melchizedek did and what Abram did in return.

It supplies no reason for the bread and wine, no terms for the blessing, and no commandment for the tithe, and what it leaves unsaid is not filled in from later practice.
