The Reproach of the Faithful Servant

A close reading of Psalm 69:7–12

A close reading of reproach, family estrangement, zeal, and public ridicule in Psalm 69, with attention to Paul’s limited citation in Romans 15:3.

Open the free two-page PDFRead on this page

The voice and setting of the lament

Psalm 69 begins with the speaker’s cry to be saved from overwhelming waters (vv. 1–3). He faces numerous enemies and charges he regards as unjust (v. 4). Verse 5 also acknowledges that God knows his folly and wrongs. The poem therefore does not invite us to make its entire first-person voice a simple transcript of a sinless person’s biography. Its movement must be heard before any later application is made.

In verses 6–12 the speaker is concerned that his disgrace might also bring shame to others who seek the Lord. This concern places his suffering within a community. He is not merely describing private hurt; he is telling God that his devotion and public humiliation are visible to fellow worshipers.

Shame reaches the household Psalm 69:7–8

The speaker says he bears reproach for God’s sake and that shame has covered his face. He then describes being treated as a stranger by his brothers and as an alien by his mother’s sons. The second expression makes the alienation concrete: even the people from whom he would expect family recognition distance themselves.

The passage does not tell us the names of those relatives, a single occasion for their estrangement, or how long it lasted. Its language gives the shape of the reproach, not an independent account of a family dispute. The reader should not supply a specific event that the psalm does not identify.

Zeal and reproaches Psalm 69:9

The speaker next gives a reason for this hostility: zeal for God’s house consumes him, and the reproaches directed at God fall on him. The parallel lines connect his devotion to the Lord with the disgrace he bears. The second line is the one Paul uses in Romans 15:3 when he says Christ did not please himself.

Paul’s citation belongs to his appeal in Romans 15:1–7 for the strong to bear with the weak and seek a neighbor’s good. He invokes Christ’s selfless bearing of reproach in that argument. The citation makes a specific apostolic use of the psalm; it does not, by itself, reassign every first-person statement or circumstance in Psalm 69 to every member of the Body.

Fasting becomes a taunt Psalm 69:10–12

The speaker recounts fasting and weeping, but these practices become occasions for reproach. When he wears sackcloth, others turn him into a byword. People sitting at the gate talk about him, and drinkers make him the subject of songs. The movement from family distance to public talk widens the scene of humiliation.

These lines resist a tidy formula in which visible devotion wins social respect. The text reports the opposite in this case. It does not say that fasting or sackcloth always produces ridicule, nor does it command later readers to reproduce either practice. The details belong to the lament’s portrayal of this sufferer.

What this close reading permits

Psalm 69:7–12 portrays reproach borne in connection with devotion to God, moving from shame and family estrangement to public mockery. Romans 15:3 draws one line into Paul’s appeal by pointing to Christ’s refusal to please himself. The original lament and Paul’s use can be read together without erasing either setting.

Read Psalm 69:1–13 and Romans 15:1–7 together. Mark the speaker’s claims, the exact line Paul cites, and the action Paul asks his readers to take.
