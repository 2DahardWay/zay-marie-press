Jonah

Mercy, mission, and repentance.

TestamentOld Testament
GenreMinor Prophets
Primary SettingIsraelite prophet sent to Assyrian Nineveh
Key MovementFrom prophetic flight through Gentile repentance to divine correction
On This PagePurposeHistorical and Biblical ContextStructure and OutlineMajor ThemesKey PassagesInterpretive ConsiderationsStudy Questions

Purpose

Jonah reveals the LORD’s sovereign freedom to show mercy to a violent Gentile city and exposes the prophet’s resistance to that compassion. The narrative emphasizes God’s word, control of creation, response to repentance, and concern for those unable to discern rightly.

Historical and Biblical Context

Jonah son of Amittai is also named in 2 Kings 14:25. Nineveh represents the feared Assyrian world. The book’s narrative focus rests less on Assyria’s later history than on the prophet’s confrontation with God’s mercy.

Evidence and inference: Prophetic texts join historical setting, covenant proclamation, symbolic imagery, and future promise. This summary distinguishes explicit textual claims from debated conclusions about date, structure, chronology, or fulfillment.

Structure and Outline

  1. Flight and storm — Jonah 1
    Jonah flees, the LORD sends a storm, and pagan sailors fear the LORD.
  2. Prayer from the fish — Jonah 2
    Jonah thanks God for deliverance while acknowledging that salvation belongs to the LORD.
  3. Nineveh repents — Jonah 3
    The renewed commission produces citywide response and withheld judgment.
  4. Jonah corrected — Jonah 4
    The prophet’s anger and the plant lesson reveal God’s compassion.

Major Themes

Sovereignty

Sea, wind, fish, plant, worm, and city remain under God’s rule.

Repentance and Mercy

God responds to Nineveh’s turning from violence.

Prophetic Resistance

Jonah knows God’s character yet resents its extension to enemies.

Concern for the Nations

The LORD’s compassion reaches beyond Israel without dissolving Israel’s distinct identity.

Key Passages

Jonah 1:1–17Flight ends in storm, witness, and the appointed fish.
Jonah 2:1–10Jonah prays from distress and is delivered.
Jonah 3:1–10Nineveh responds to warning and judgment is withheld.
Jonah 4:1–4Jonah explains that mercy motivated his flight.
Jonah 4:5–11The plant exposes disordered pity and divine compassion.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Read Jonah as theological narrative rather than reducing it to the fish episode.
  • Nineveh’s response concerns the announced judgment; the text does not claim complete knowledge of Israel’s covenant revelation.
  • The sailors and Ninevites often respond more appropriately than the prophet, creating deliberate irony.
  • God’s mercy to Gentiles does not merge His program for Israel with the Mystery Program.

Study Questions

  1. Why does Jonah flee?
  2. How do the sailors contrast with Jonah?
  3. What produces Nineveh’s reprieve?
  4. What does the plant reveal about Jonah and God?
