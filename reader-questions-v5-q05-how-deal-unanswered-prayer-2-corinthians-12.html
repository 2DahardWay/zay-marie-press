How Do I Deal with Unanswered Prayer? 2 Corinthians 12:8–9

From Reader Questions, Volume 5: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 5 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

Unanswered prayer is not a covenant judgment on the Body, which is not under the Law and not Israel. God answers according to his wisdom, and Paul’s own case shows what that can mean: he besought the Lord three times that the thorn “might depart from me,” and the answer was “My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness.” Some requests are not granted because they cut across God’s purposes for the believer’s growth, and “all things work together for good to them that love God.” The Spirit helps in prayer according to the will of God and not ours. Believers are told to give thanks in everything, to continue in prayer, and to rest in the character of the God they know: “I know whom I have believed.”

Where the Verses Stand

In 2 Corinthians 12 Paul describes the thorn in the flesh and says he asked for its removal three times. The answer follows at once, with his conclusion about weakness. His other words on prayer are Philippians 4 (prayer with thanksgiving), Romans 8 (the Spirit’s help), Colossians 4 (continue in prayer) and 2 Timothy 1 (the confidence of one who knows whom he has believed).

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: Paul prayed three times for relief and was told the grace was sufficient and the weakness would carry Christ’s power (2 Corinthians 12:8–9). His prayer was heard and the answer was not removal.
  • Stated: the Spirit “helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought,” and intercedes “according to the will of God” (Romans 8:26–27).
  • Shown by the context: Paul does not treat the unanswered request as a failure of faith or a judgment; he accepts it and boasts in his infirmities (2 Corinthians 12:9–10). The Body is told “ye are not under the law, but under grace” (Romans 6:14), so a withheld answer is not a covenant curse.
  • Shown by the context: the instructions that follow unanswered prayer in Paul are thanksgiving (1 Thessalonians 5:18) and perseverance: “Continue in prayer, and watch in the same with thanksgiving” (Colossians 4:2).
  • Shown by the context: Paul draws a lesson from the refusal: “Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me” (2 Corinthians 12:9). He does not demand a reason; he accepts the answer and finds in it what the Lord intended.
  • Inferred: that a withheld request can serve a purpose the believer cannot yet see, and that the proper answer is trust in God’s character. Paul’s case and Romans 8:28 support it; no passage promises that every request will be granted.

The Other Readings

Pray with faith and God will give. The word-of-faith teaching, as in Kenneth Copeland (The Laws of Prosperity), holds that confident, specific prayer secures what is asked. It must explain why Paul’s own prayer was refused and why his answer was grace. God answers in his way. D. A. Carson (A Call to Spiritual Reformation) and Philip Yancey (Prayer: Does It Make Any Difference?) write of prayer as a relationship in which God does not always give what is asked. The reading taken here agrees and rests the point on Paul’s own case and on the Body’s standing under grace.

What It Means for the Reader

The reader is not told that unanswered prayer means he has failed or that God has turned from him. He is told what Paul was told: grace is sufficient. He is to keep praying, give thanks and trust a God whose character he knows. The passage does not promise that every request will be granted, and it does not say how long a request may go unanswered. It records one man’s prayer, the Lord’s answer and the apostle’s response, and it asks the reader to bring his own requests in the same way.
