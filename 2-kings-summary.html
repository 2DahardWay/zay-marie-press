2 Kings

Persistent covenant rebellion leads first Israel and then Judah into exile, even as the prophetic word remains certain and God preserves hope.

TestamentOld Testament
GenreHistorical Narrative

Purpose

Second Kings explains the fall of the northern kingdom to Assyria and the later fall of Judah to Babylon as covenant judgment. It records prophetic ministry, limited reforms, persistent idolatry, and the certainty of God’s announced word.

Historical and Biblical Context

The book continues the reign of Ahaziah and the ministry of Elijah, then gives prominence to Elisha. Royal accounts alternate until Samaria falls in 722 BC; Judah’s history continues through reform, relapse, Jerusalem’s destruction, and exile.

Evidence and inference: This summary distinguishes the narrative’s explicit claims from historical reconstruction and interpretive inference. Where authorship, date, chronology, or structure is debated, the book’s received form and stated theological movement remain primary.

Structure and Outline

  1. Elijah succeeded by Elisha — 2 Kings 1–8
    Elijah is taken up, and Elisha’s signs authenticate the prophetic word.
  2. Dynastic upheaval and Israel’s decline — 2 Kings 9–17
    Jehu destroys Ahab’s house, yet northern idolatry continues until Assyria conquers Samaria.
  3. Hezekiah and Assyrian crisis — 2 Kings 18–20
    Hezekiah reforms Judah and trusts God when Sennacherib threatens Jerusalem.
  4. Manasseh, Josiah, and final decline — 2 Kings 21–23
    Deep apostasy is followed by Josiah’s reform, but national judgment remains.
  5. Jerusalem falls — 2 Kings 24–25
    Babylon destroys the city and temple; Judah goes into exile.

Major Themes

The Certainty of God’s Word

Prophetic announcements govern the interpretation and outcome of events.

Covenant Judgment

Exile results from sustained idolatry, injustice, and refusal to heed God’s servants.

Reform and Its Limits

Hezekiah and Josiah act faithfully, but reform cannot erase entrenched national guilt.

Preserved Hope

The release of Jehoiachin at the end keeps the Davidic line in view amid exile.

Key Passages

2 Kings 2:1–15Elijah is taken up and Elisha receives prophetic succession.
2 Kings 5:1–19Naaman is cleansed and confesses Israel’s God.
2 Kings 17:5–23The narrator explains the fall of the northern kingdom.
2 Kings 19:14–37Hezekiah prays, and God delivers Jerusalem from Assyria.
2 Kings 22:8–20The book of the law is found and Josiah responds humbly.
2 Kings 24:18–25:21Jerusalem and the temple fall to Babylon.
2 Kings 25:27–30Jehoiachin is released and maintained in Babylon.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Read the falls of Samaria and Jerusalem through the narrator’s explicit covenant explanations.
  • Miracles in the Elijah-Elisha narratives authenticate prophetic ministry within Israel’s crisis.
  • Distinguish temporary political relief from the deeper need for national repentance.
  • The exile does not imply that God’s promises to Israel have been transferred to another people.
  • The final notice concerning Jehoiachin is modest but intentionally preserves the Davidic horizon.

Study Questions

  1. How do Elijah and Elisha confront Israel’s rulers and worship?
  2. What reasons does chapter 17 give for the northern kingdom’s exile?
  3. How does Hezekiah respond to Assyrian threat?
  4. Why does Josiah’s reform not prevent Babylonian judgment?
  5. What hope remains at the book’s conclusion?
