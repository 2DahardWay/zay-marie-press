What Does “Out of Egypt Have I Called My Son” Mean in Hosea 11:1 and Matthew 2:15?

From Reader Questions, Volume 3: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 3 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

Hosea 11:1 looks back. It is the Lord’s own word that Israel, his covenant son, was called out of Egypt at the exodus. Matthew applies the same line to Jesus, God’s eternal Son, whose flight into Egypt and return re-live Israel’s pattern. These are two sons, each in his own place: Israel in national sonship, Christ in his own Sonship. Matthew’s use is typological fulfilment, not a transfer of identity: Israel remains Israel, and Jesus does not replace Israel. He does not claim that Hosea was predicting the infant’s journey.

Where the Verses Stand

Matthew 2 tells of Joseph’s flight with the child and his mother to Egypt and of their stay “until the death of Herod,” and then gives the quotation. In Hosea 11 the Lord speaks of Israel’s childhood, of the nation’s turning to the Baalim after he called it, and of his refusal to give it up. The two passages are about the same name for the same people.

When he arose, he took the young child and his mother by night, and departed into Egypt: And was there until the death of Herod: that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet, saying, Out of Egypt have I called my son.”The flight to Egypt, the stay, and “that it might be fulfilled.”

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: Hosea 11:1 is about Israel in the past: “When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt.” The next verse reports what Israel did after it was called.
  • Stated: the title is Israel’s from the exodus. At the burning bush the Lord tells Moses to say, “Israel is my son, even my firstborn … Let my son go” (Exodus 4:22–23).
  • Stated: Matthew writes that the flight and return came to pass “that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet,” and quotes only Hosea’s opening clause.
  • Shown by the context: Matthew sets the same pattern in his next chapters. The Son is brought out of Egypt, passes through water (3:13–17), and is led into the wilderness, where he answers each temptation from Deuteronomy 6–8, the passage that recalls Israel’s forty years.
  • Shown by the context: Matthew opens by calling the child “the son of David, the son of Abraham” (1:1). He is writing of Israel’s King, who passes through the places Israel passed through. Israel is God’s son in one sense and the King is his Son in another, and neither stands in the other’s place.
  • Inferred: that “fulfilled” means the pattern re-lived by the eternal Son, and not a prediction hidden in Hosea and not the replacing of Israel. The text does not define the word, and the argument rests on the past tense of Hosea 11:1 and on the wilderness parallel Matthew himself supplies.

The Other Readings

A direct prediction. A reading common in popular apologetics takes Hosea 11:1 as a prophecy of the Messiah’s return from Egypt that Matthew simply records as fulfilled. It must explain why Hosea speaks of Israel “when … a child” and goes on at once to Israel’s idolatry, which cannot be said of the infant. Matthew’s freer reading. Raymond Brown (The Birth of the Messiah) describes Matthew’s quotations as an interpretive use of Israel’s Scriptures in the light of Jesus, in which a likeness of experience is enough to link the texts. It leaves the link resting on the evangelist’s method. A pattern completed by the true Son. R. T. France (Matthew, Tyndale New Testament Commentary) and Craig Blomberg (his treatment of Matthew in Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament, edited by Beale and Carson) take the fulfilment as a correspondence between the nation’s experience and the Messiah’s. That is the reading taken here, with the added point that the two sons stay distinct.

What It Means for the Reader

The verse is a reminder of who is in view when the Lord says “my son.” In Hosea it is Israel; in Matthew it is the King, whose Sonship is his own. Neither is the Body, which has no exodus and no covenant sonship in Israel’s sense. This is Israel’s line of Scripture, and the Body’s place in Christ is spoken of elsewhere and in other terms.
