The New Testament's Use of the Old Testament

A Survey of How, Where and Why the Writers Reach Back into Israel's Scriptures

One God, faithful across two programs that never merge. The New Testament writers were steeped in Israel's Scriptures; what they do with them depends on who they are writing to.

What this guide is. A survey. It shows how, where and how often the New Testament reaches into the Old, and what each writer appears to be doing when he does. It does not argue for or against any one theory. Each row says what the text marks, what the context shows, and what a reader has to infer.

Part 1 · Why the New Testament Uses the Old

Same Scriptures, different readers

The New Testament was written by men who knew Israel's Scriptures. Matthew, John, Peter, James, the writer to the Hebrews and Paul all cite, echo or retell them, and they do it for different readers and different purposes.

  1. The Scriptures were the shared book. For the Jewish readers of the Gospels, Acts, Hebrews, James and 1 and 2 Peter, the Old Testament was the common ground. A writer could say "it is written" and be understood.
  2. The audience differs, so the use differs. The Gospels, Acts 1–8, Hebrews, James, 1 and 2 Peter, Jude and Revelation address Israel or speak within Israel's line. Romans through Philemon are Paul's letters, written from the Mystery that Paul says was kept secret since the world began (Romans 16:25) and hid in God (Ephesians 3:9). The guide reports each use in its own letter and does not carry a use from one audience to another.
  3. Paul quotes the Scriptures as written. Paul cites Israel's Scriptures for examples, for argument and for instruction. This guide does not say that the Old Testament foretold the Mystery, because Paul says it did not (Ephesians 3:5, 9).
  4. Quotation does not rewrite. To cite a passage is not to change it. Where the New Testament uses an Old Testament text, the guide gives both texts, side by side, in the KJV.

Three tags

Every row in the chart carries one. Marked by the text The New Testament passage itself states the link. Shown by the context The link is clear from the passage and its setting, but no formula states it. Inferred The link is drawn by a reader and is not stated. The chart uses the first two only, so a reader can see where the New Testament is explicit. (The search page adds a tag for parallels, which the text does not signal.)

Part 3 · The Major Old Testament Books the New Testament Uses

In canonical order. Not ranked.

The list is not ranked. A count would depend on method (whether allusions are counted, where quotations are drawn from), and this guide does not need one.

  • Genesis. Abraham's faith (15:6) and the promise to the nations (12:3) appear in Romans, Galatians, Hebrews and James; Adam appears in Romans 5.
  • Exodus. The commandments (20:12), the Passover and the covenant at Sinai appear in the Gospels, Paul's letters, Hebrews and 1 Peter.
  • Leviticus. The dwelling promise (26:12) appears in 2 Corinthians 6:16, and the sacrifices run through Hebrews.
  • Numbers. The bronze serpent (21:9) appears in John 3, and the wilderness account in 1 Corinthians 10.
  • Deuteronomy. The Lord answers the tempter from chapters 6–8 (Matthew 4), and the commandment to honour father and mother is repeated (5:16).
  • Psalms. Psalms 2, 22, 110 and 118, among others, are cited in the Gospels, Acts, Paul's letters and Hebrews.
  • Isaiah. Cited across the Gospels, Acts, Paul's letters, 1 Peter and Revelation (for example 7:14, 40:3, 53 and 61:1–2).
  • Jeremiah. The new covenant (31:31–34) is quoted at length in Hebrews 8.
  • Ezekiel. Echoed in 2 Corinthians 6:16 and in Revelation.
  • Daniel. The Son of man (7:13) is echoed in the Gospels and in Revelation 1:13.
  • Hosea. Cited in Matthew 2:15 and Romans 9:25–26.
  • Joel. Quoted at Pentecost (Acts 2:16–21).
  • Habakkuk. One verse (2:4) is quoted by three writers: Romans 1:17, Galatians 3:11 and Hebrews 10:38.
  • Zechariah. Cited in John 19:37 and echoed in Revelation 1:7.
  • Malachi. Quoted in Mark 1:2, in the account of John the Baptist.

Books not on the list are not unused. The list names the books the chart draws from.

Part 4 · What the New Testament Writers Are Doing

The same text, put to different uses for different readers

  • Matthew ties events in the Lord's life to Israel's Scriptures with a repeated fulfilment formula ("that it might be fulfilled").
  • Mark and Luke report the Lord's own use: Mark opens with "as it is written in the prophets," and Luke records the Lord reading Isaiah 61 in the synagogue.
  • John sets the signs and events beside what "the scripture" says, and records the Lord's own comparison with the bronze serpent.
  • Acts records Peter at Pentecost ("this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel") and the apostles' use of Psalm 118 before the council.
  • Hebrews builds an argument by laying Old Testament texts end to end (chapter 1) and quotes the new covenant of Jeremiah 31 as the covenant of the house of Israel and the house of Judah.
  • James and 1 Peter write to Israel's scattered people (James 1:1; 1 Peter 1:1) and use Genesis and Exodus language in that setting.
  • Paul quotes the Scriptures for argument (Romans 4), for example (1 Corinthians 10), for instruction (Ephesians 6:2) and, in one place, through a device he names himself, an allegory (Galatians 4:24). The seed is prophetic fulfillment in Christ; the Body receives blessing through Christ alone, not through participation in the Abrahamic covenant.
  • Revelation draws its imagery from Daniel, Zechariah and others, mostly by allusion, and almost never names a source.

What the guide does not say. It does not say that every New Testament use is a prediction fulfilled, that every Old Testament person is a type, or that a text addressed to Israel was written to another audience. It reports the use and the audience and stops.

1:17 For therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith: as it is written, The just shall live by faith. 3:11 But that no man is justified by the law in the sight of God, it is evident: for, The just shall live by faith. 10:38 Now the just shall live by faith: but if any man draw back, my soul shall have no pleasure in him.

Old Testament (KJV)

2:4 Behold, his soul which is lifted up is not upright in him: but the just shall live by his faith.

Part 6 · How to Use This Guide

  1. Start with the audience. For any New Testament passage, ask who is being written to before asking what the Old Testament text is doing.
  2. Open both texts. Read the Old Testament passage in its own setting before reading the New Testament use.
  3. Look for the marker. Is there a formula ("it is written," "that it might be fulfilled")? Is there a word like "figure," "examples" or "an allegory"? Mark what the text itself states.
  4. Keep the tags honest. Where you infer a link, say that you are inferring.
  5. Stay with the passage. The aim is to see what the writer did with the text, not to settle every question about how.
