Who Are “the Spirits in Prison” in 1 Peter 3:19?

From Reader Questions, Volume 3: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 3 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

They are angelic beings and not human souls: the angels who sinned in the days of Noah, now held in prison for judgment (2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6). After he was “quickened by the Spirit,” Christ made a proclamation to them. The word is the herald’s word, a declaration of his victory and of their judgment, and it is not an offer of the gospel to the dead. The passage ends with “angels and authorities and powers being made subject unto him.” It is written to Israel’s scattered believers and has no connection with the Body’s heavenly calling.

Where the Verses Stand

Peter writes to “the strangers scattered” (1:1), Israel’s scattered believers, who are suffering for doing right. Chapter 3 tells them that it is better, if God wills, to suffer for well doing than for evil doing (3:17), and then gives the reason: Christ also suffered, the just for the unjust, was “quickened by the Spirit,” and went and preached to the spirits in prison. The passage closes with Christ in heaven and every power subject to him.

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the beings are “spirits” “in prison,” and the disobedience is dated: “Which sometime were disobedient, when once the longsuffering of God waited in the days of Noah” (3:20).
  • Stated: Christ’s act is put in the Greek with the herald’s verb, which Peter does not use when he speaks elsewhere of preaching the gospel (1:12; 4:6). It is a proclamation, not a gospel offer.
  • Stated: the passage closes with the outcome: he “is gone into heaven, and is on the right hand of God; angels and authorities and powers being made subject unto him” (3:22).
  • Shown by the context: Peter’s letter to his own suffering readers is meant to give courage. Christ suffered, was made alive, and won; hostile powers do not stand outside his rule. A word of victory to rebel powers belongs to that argument, and a second chance for the dead does not.
  • Shown by the context: Second Peter 2:4–5 sets “the angels that sinned,” cast into “chains of darkness,” beside “the old world” of the flood, and Jude 6–7 joins angels held in “everlasting chains” to Sodom. The prison of the spirits of Noah’s day is the prison of those angels, and Volume 2, entry 9, takes up the sons of God of Genesis 6.
  • Inferred: that the proclamation was made after the resurrection (“quickened by the Spirit,” 3:18) and before or at his going into heaven (3:22). The text does not state the time or the place, and the entry does not name them.

The Other Readings

Christ preaching through Noah. Wayne Grudem (1 Peter, Tyndale New Testament Commentary) holds that Christ by his Spirit preached through Noah to the people of Noah’s day, who are now spirits in prison. It must explain why the preaching is placed after he was “quickened,” and why the spirits are “in prison” now and not the people of that generation. A preaching to the dead. An older tradition has Christ go to the place of the dead to preach to those who had died. Grudem (Systematic Theology) discusses and rejects it. It must explain why the herald’s verb is used and why only the disobedient of one generation are named. A proclamation of victory to fallen angels. J. Ramsey Michaels (1 Peter, Word Biblical Commentary) takes the spirits as the demonic offspring of the fallen angels of Genesis 6, though he is far from dogmatic about it, and other commentaries read the spirits as the angels themselves. Both read the proclamation as the declaration of his triumph. That is the reading taken here, with the spirits as the beings of Genesis 6.

First Peter 4:6, “the gospel preached also to them that are dead,” is a different statement. It speaks of those to whom the gospel was preached and who have since died, so that “they might be judged according to men in the flesh, but live according to God in the spirit.” It uses the gospel verb of 1:12 and does not carry the spirits of chapter 3.

What It Means for the Reader

The passage does not give a map of the unseen world, and it is not a message of salvation but a declaration of judgment, with no second chance for the dead. It is addressed to Israel’s scattered believers and does not describe the Body’s heavenly destiny. It tells believers who are suffering for righteousness that the One who suffered for them rose and sits on the right hand of God, and that no power, whether the imprisoned ones of Noah’s day or the “principalities and powers” of Colossians 2:15, is outside his rule.
