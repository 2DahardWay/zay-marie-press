What Does “Take Up His Cross, and Follow Me” Mean in Matthew 16:24?

From Reader Questions, Volume 3: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 3 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

This is Kingdom-discipleship instruction, spoken by Christ in his earthly ministry to Israel’s disciples. “Take up his cross” is the figure of a condemned man: self-denial, readiness to suffer and loyalty to the Messiah as they follow him toward the promised kingdom, with the reward when the Son of man comes “in his kingdom.” It is the requirement for Israel’s remnant and it is not a command to the Body of Christ, which is never told to take up a cross or to follow Jesus on his earthly path. The Body’s word is that it is “crucified with Christ.”

Where the Verse Stands

The saying comes at a turn in the Gospel. Peter has just confessed, “Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God” (16:16). “From that time forth” the Lord begins to show his disciples that he must suffer, be killed and be raised (16:21). Peter takes him aside and rebukes him, and the Lord answers, “Get thee behind me, Satan.” Only then does he turn to all the disciples and say what following him costs.

What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the saying is spoken to “his disciples” and follows directly on the announcement that he will be killed and Peter’s refusal to hear it. “Let him deny himself” is said to the man who has just said, “this shall not be unto thee.”
  • Stated: the reason is “for my sake,” and the exchange is the man’s “life” (the same word is rendered “soul” in 16:26) against “the whole world.” Whoever will save it shall lose it; whoever will lose it for the Lord’s sake shall find it.
  • Stated: the saying looks forward to a reckoning: “the Son of man shall come in the glory of his Father with his angels; and then he shall reward every man according to his works” (16:27). Some standing there will see “the Son of man coming in his kingdom” (16:28).
  • Shown by the context: the hearers are Israel’s disciples, and the setting is the kingdom: the confession is “the Christ,” the reward is at the Son of man’s coming, and the same cost is put to the “great multitudes” in Luke 14:25–27. These are Israel’s disciples following their Messiah toward the promised earthly kingdom, and nothing in the setting is addressed to a company called out of the nations.
  • Inferred: that “his cross” is the figure of a condemned man carrying the instrument of his own execution. The text does not describe the figure, and the argument rests on the Lord’s announcement of his own death just before it.
  • Inferred: that the saying is not a command to the Body. Paul never repeats it, and the Body is never told to follow Jesus in his earthly path. His word to the Body is that it has been crucified with Christ and that the cross is the ground of its standing, not a load to be taken up (Galatians 2:20; 6:14).

The Other Readings

Any hardship a believer bears. The saying is often applied to whatever burden life brings: an illness, a hard marriage, a difficult relative. It must explain why a cross, which was carried to an execution, became a figure for something endured at home, and why the Lord joins it to “for my sake.” The call to every follower in every age. Many take the cross as the suffering and rejection that come to anyone who follows Christ, a reading that keeps the figure of death. It must explain why the Body is given as a command what its own apostle gives as a finished fact. The first disciples’ readiness to be rejected and killed. The saying is read first as the readiness of the Lord’s own disciples to share his rejection and death. That is the reading taken here, with the added point that its setting is Israel’s kingdom and not the Body’s.

What It Means for the Reader

This is program-specific instruction and not a universal rule for every believer. It shows what the King asked of the men who followed him on the road to Jerusalem, and what he promised them at his coming. The Body is not asked to earn anything by it. The Body’s word is the other one: the cross was borne for it, and it is told that it died with Christ.
