What Was Paul’s “Thorn in the Flesh”?

From Reader Questions, Volume 1: Questions on Difficult Bible Verses. Free to read here; the whole volume is a free PDF.

FreeDownload Volume 1 (PDF)
THE ANSWER

The thorn was opposition: a messenger of Satan sent to buffet Paul in his ministry. It was not an illness. The Lord’s answer, “My grace is sufficient for thee,” meets that pressure, and Paul’s own list that follows is of reproaches, necessities, persecutions and distresses.

Where the Verse Stands

The thorn comes late in a long defence. In chapters 10 to 13 Paul answers men who set themselves up against his apostleship, and he calls them “false apostles, deceitful workers, transforming themselves into the apostles of Christ” (11:13). He answers them by a list of what his ministry has cost him (11:23–28), and then by a revelation he was given, in which a man in Christ was “caught up to the third heaven” (12:2). The thorn is given because of that revelation, “lest I should be exalted above measure.”

Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me. What Carries the Weight

  • Stated: the thorn is “the messenger of Satan to buffet me,” and it was “given to me.” It is a person or agent sent against Paul, and it is permitted for a stated purpose.
  • Shown by the context: the whole of chapters 10 to 13 is about opponents. Satan’s ministers (11:15) and “false brethren” (11:26) are the people named in the lead-up, and the thorn follows them in the same defence.
  • Shown by the context: Paul’s summary in 12:10 is a list of pressures from outside: reproaches, necessities, persecutions, distresses. It names no sickness.
  • Inferred: that the thorn is the opposition to his ministry in the same argument. The text calls it a messenger of Satan and does not name a person or a company.
  • Inferred: that the figure is the familiar one. In Numbers 33:55, Joshua 23:13 and Judges 2:3, the peoples left in the land become thorns and snares to Israel. Paul does not quote these, and the entry does not rest on them.

The Other Readings

A bodily illness. This is the most common popular reading. It is usually built on Galatians 4:13–15, where Paul speaks of “infirmity of the flesh” and of the Galatians’ readiness to give him their eyes, and it takes “in the flesh” in 12:7 as a reference to the body. Ralph Martin (2 Corinthians, Word Biblical Commentary) surveys the range of views and judges that the thorn cannot be identified with certainty. The reading has to explain why a sickness is called Satan’s messenger and why Paul’s own list in 12:10 does not include one. A temptation. This reads the thorn as an inward struggle with sin. The text says the thorn is “to buffet” him from outside and that he “besought the Lord thrice that it might depart.” An opponent or the opposition. This is the reading taken here.

What It Means for the Reader

The passage is not a pattern for refused healing and does not tell the reader to read every weakness as a thorn. It is Paul’s answer to the opposition he met in the ministry committed to him, and the Lord’s answer is that his grace is enough and that power is “made perfect in weakness.”
