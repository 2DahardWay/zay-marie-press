Luke

An orderly account of Jesus the promised Savior, tracing God’s faithfulness to Israel, the Spirit-empowered Messiah’s ministry, His compassion for the overlooked, and His journey to the cross and resurrection.

TestamentNew Testament
GenreGospel

Central EmphasisThe promised Savior seeking the lost

Purpose

Luke writes an orderly narrative so that Theophilus may know the certainty of the matters in which he has been instructed. Drawing on prior accounts and eyewitness testimony, Luke presents Jesus’ words and deeds in a carefully shaped narrative that continues in Acts.

The Gospel shows God acting faithfully within Israel’s prophetic hope while extending mercy broadly. Jesus brings good news to the poor, calls sinners to repentance, confronts self-righteousness, seeks the lost, journeys resolutely to Jerusalem, dies, rises, and prepares His witnesses for the promised work ahead.

Historical and Biblical Context

Luke’s opening chapters are saturated with Israel’s Scriptures, temple worship, Davidic hope, covenant remembrance, and expectation of redemption. Jesus is born under Roman rule, raised within faithful Jewish practice, anointed by the Spirit, and identified as the promised Son of David and Son of God.

Although the Gospel itself does not name its author, early testimony and the unity of Luke–Acts associate both volumes with Luke, Paul’s companion. The address to Theophilus suggests an educated reader or patron, while the narrative is written for a wider readership as well. Debates about date and sources should be weighed without allowing reconstructions to outrank the text’s stated purpose and narrative design.

Structure and Outline

  1. Promise, birth, and preparation — Luke 1:1–4:13
    John and Jesus are announced and born; Jesus is prepared, identified, and tested.
  2. Ministry in Galilee — Luke 4:14–9:50
    Jesus proclaims fulfillment, teaches with authority, performs signs, calls disciples, and is confessed as the Christ.
  3. The journey toward Jerusalem — Luke 9:51–19:27
    A long travel section emphasizes discipleship, prayer, possessions, reversal, repentance, and God’s pursuit of the lost.
  4. Jerusalem ministry and controversy — Luke 19:28–21:38
    Jesus enters as King, confronts the temple order, answers opponents, and teaches about coming judgment.
  5. Passion, resurrection, and promised witness — Luke 22–24
    Jesus suffers, dies, rises, opens the Scriptures, and prepares the disciples for witness beginning at Jerusalem.

Major Themes

Fulfillment and God’s Faithfulness

Luke connects Jesus to Israel’s Scriptures, Davidic promise, Abrahamic mercy, prophetic expectation, and hope of redemption. The resurrection confirms that the Messiah’s suffering and glory accord with Scripture.

Salvation for the Lost

Jesus’ mission reaches sinners, tax collectors, the poor, the socially overlooked, Samaritans, and Gentiles. This breadth grows from God’s promised saving action rather than erasing Israel’s place in the narrative.

The Holy Spirit and Prayer

The Spirit fills, leads, empowers, and confirms participants throughout the Gospel. Prayer marks crucial moments in Jesus’ ministry and instructs disciples in dependence and persistence.

Reversal, Wealth, and Stewardship

Luke repeatedly contrasts proud and humble, rich and poor, satisfied and hungry, self-justifying and repentant. Possessions expose loyalties and create opportunities for generous stewardship.

The Journey to Jerusalem

From Luke 9:51, Jesus advances deliberately toward His appointed suffering. The journey organizes sustained instruction on what it means to hear, repent, follow, serve, and wait faithfully.

Interpretive Considerations

  • Read Luke and Acts as two coordinated volumes, but allow each passage to retain its own historical moment, audience, and purpose.
  • Luke’s universal horizon does not cancel the Gospel’s Israel-centered prophetic setting. Its opening, temple scenes, Davidic claims, journey to Jerusalem, and resurrection commission all depend on that setting.
  • Attend to narrative contrasts and repeated themes. Meals, wealth, prayer, joy, reversal, journey, and the Holy Spirit often interpret events more clearly than isolated sayings do.
  • Parables should be read in their immediate controversy or teaching setting. Not every narrative detail carries an independent symbolic meaning.
  • Commands given to particular disciples during Jesus’ earthly ministry should be assigned according to their stated audience and mission before principles or later applications are drawn.
  • Meaning first, assignment second, and application third preserves both the original kingdom and prophetic setting and the Gospel’s enduring testimony to Christ.

Study Questions

  1. What does Luke 1:1–4 reveal about the author’s purpose and method?
  2. How do the songs and prophecies in Luke 1–2 place Jesus within Israel’s hope?
  3. What themes introduced in Jesus’ Nazareth sermon recur through the Gospel?
  4. How does the journey section shape Luke’s teaching about discipleship?
  5. What do Luke’s meals and parables reveal about repentance, mercy, and self-righteousness?
  6. How does Luke 24 connect the Messiah’s suffering, resurrection, Scripture, and coming witness?
